Lag compensation

The lag compensator as a modified PI controller, its two forms, the two design procedures on the Bode plot, and when to choose lag over lead. Built from Lecture 11 and the Week 5 examples, with FPE Chapter 6.7.4.

01From PI control to the lag compensator

Week 4's lead compensator was a modified PD controller that adds phase near crossover. The lag compensator is a modified PI controller. It suits applications where a low bandwidth is acceptable and a small steady-state error is needed.

PI compensator\[ D(s) = k_P + \frac{k_I}{s} \;\;\rightarrow\;\; D_c(s) = \frac{K}{s}\left(s + \frac{1}{T_I}\right) \]
\(k_P,\,k_I\)
Proportional and integral gains.
\(K\)
Gain (equal to \(k_P\)).
\(T_I\)
Integral time (s), \(T_I = k_P/k_I\); the zero breaks at \(1/T_I\).

The integrator gives infinite gain at DC, so the PI controller reduces (removes) the steady-state error. Its cost is a 90° phase lag at low frequency, which reduces stability. In the alternative notation \(T\) is short for \(T_I\), the time constant tied to the break frequency on the Bode plot.

02The lag compensator

The lag compensator replaces the integrator with a pole at a low but non-zero frequency, below the zero. The low-frequency gain is then high but finite, and the phase lag is limited and confined to low frequencies.

Lag compensator (FPE 6.43)\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Normal: } KD_c(s) = K\alpha\,\frac{Ts + 1}{\alpha Ts + 1}, \\ \text{Unity DC gain: } KD_c(s) = K\,\frac{Ts + 1}{\alpha Ts + 1}, \qquad \alpha \gt 1 \end{gathered} \]
\(K\)
Gain.
\(T\)
Time constant of the zero; zero breaks at \(1/T\).
\(\alpha\)
Ratio of zero to pole break frequency, greater than 1 for a lag. The pole breaks at \(1/(\alpha T)\).
\(K\alpha\)
Normal form: DC gain \(K\alpha\), high-frequency gain \(K\).
\(K\)
Unity DC gain form: DC gain \(K\), high-frequency gain \(K/\alpha\).

Figure 2.1: Both forms have the same shape and the same phase: a dip between the two breaks that is gone a decade above the zero. The normal form lifts the low-frequency gain by α and leaves the high-frequency gain at K. The unity DC gain form keeps the DC gain at K and cuts the high-frequency gain by α. Which one you use depends on whether the problem is the error (normal) or the phase margin at the present crossover (unity DC gain).

What a lag compensator is for

Use a lag when the steady-state error is too large but the transient (phase margin, crossover) is acceptable, or when the phase margin is too low and a slower loop is acceptable. It works on the gain at low frequency and leaves the region around crossover almost untouched.

  • Normal form: raises the low-frequency gain by α while the gain and phase at crossover hardly change. \(K_p\) or \(K_v\) rises by α, so the steady-state error falls by about the same factor, with the same overshoot and speed.
  • Unity DC gain form: keeps the low-frequency gain (and so the error) and cuts the gain above the pole by α. The magnitude then crosses 0 dB at a lower frequency, where the plant's own phase is less negative, so the phase margin rises.
  • The cost: the lag adds a few degrees of phase lag at crossover, the unity DC gain form lowers the bandwidth (a slower response), and the slow pole-zero pair near the origin leaves a slow tail in the step response.

A lag is a PI controller whose integrator has been moved off the origin to the pole \(1/(\alpha T)\): it gives high but finite gain at DC instead of infinite gain.

Rule

Place the lag's zero one decade below the crossover frequency: \(1/T = \omega_c/10\), so \(T = 10/\omega_c\). The pole then follows from \(\alpha\): \(1/(\alpha T) = \omega_c/(10\alpha)\).

Why one decade, and not closer or further? The lag's phase dip sits between its pole and zero and dies away above the zero, but not completely. At the crossover it still subtracts \(\tan^{-1}(\omega_cT) - \tan^{-1}(\alpha\omega_cT)\), which depends on where the zero is:

Zero at\(\omega_cT\)Lag at crossover (α = 10)Effect
Half a decade below \(\omega_c\)3.1615.7°Too much: eats most of the margin the design was meant to give.
One decade below \(\omega_c\)105.1°Small and predictable: covered by the 5° allowance in the design procedure.
Two decades below \(\omega_c\)1000.5°Hardly any lag, but the pole-zero pair is ten times slower.

One decade is where the two costs balance:

  • Closer than a decade: the lag at crossover grows quickly and the phase margin falls short of the requirement.
  • Further than a decade: the phase gain is tiny (at most 5° to recover), but the pole and zero move ten times nearer the origin. The slow closed-loop pole that sits beside the zero then decays with a time constant close to \(T\), so the step response creeps to its final value about ten times more slowly. It also needs a larger \(T\) and \(\alpha T\), which means bigger components or longer time constants to implement.

At exactly one decade, \(\omega_cT = 10\) and the lag at crossover is \(\tan^{-1}10 - \tan^{-1}(10\alpha)\): 2.8° for α = 2, 5.1° for α = 10 and never more than 5.7° however large α is. That is why the design procedure adds about 5° when it chooses the new crossover. Place the zero exactly one decade below; the 5° allowance assumes it.

03Design: unity DC gain lag

Use this form when the low-frequency gain \(K\) is already fixed by the error requirement but the phase margin is too low. The lag lowers the gain at higher frequency so that the curve crosses 0 dB earlier, at a frequency where the plant's own phase is acceptable.

  1. Find \(K\) to satisfy the low-frequency (steady-state) error requirement.
  2. Evaluate the PM of \(KG(s)\).
  3. Find the frequency where the phase of \(KG\) gives the required PM plus about 5° (the residual lag of the compensator). This will be the new crossover \(\omega_c\).
  4. Calculate \(\alpha\) as the attenuation needed to make \(|KG|\) cross 0 dB there: \(\alpha = |KG(j\omega_c)|\).
  5. Select \(T\) so the zero break frequency \(1/T\) is one decade below the new \(\omega_c\).
  6. Calculate the pole break frequency \(1/(\alpha T)\).
  7. Draw the compensated Bode plot and check.
  8. Iterate (the course focuses on the first iteration).
Worked example 3.1 Stabilising a loop without losing its error constant

\(KG(s) = \dfrac{5}{s(s+1)(0.5s+1)}\) already gives \(K_v = 5\) s⁻¹, which must be kept. Design a unity DC gain lag so that PM \(\ge 40^\circ\).

1
\(K = 1\) is set by the error requirement, so \(K_v = 5\).
2
Current margins: \(\omega_c = 1.80\) rad/s, PM \(= -13.0^\circ\). The phase passes −180° at 1.41 rad/s, below crossover: the closed loop is unstable.
3
Required phase: \(-180^\circ + 40^\circ + 5^\circ = -135^\circ\), reached at \(\omega = 0.562\) rad/s. This becomes the new crossover.
4
There \(|KG| = 7.47\) (17.5 dB), so \(\alpha = 7.47\).
5
Zero a decade below: \(1/T = 0.562/10\), \(T = 17.8\) s.
6
Pole at \(1/(\alpha T) = 0.00751\) rad/s, that is \(\alpha T = 133.1\) s.
\(KD_c(s) = \dfrac{17.8s + 1}{133.1s + 1}\). The compensated loop crosses at 0.564 rad/s with PM \(= 39.9^\circ\) and GM \(= 4.16\), so the requirement is just met; a slightly larger safety allowance would add margin. \(K_v\) is still 5 s⁻¹. The price is speed: crossover has fallen from 1.80 to 0.564 rad/s, and the step response settles in about 25 s (2 %).

04Design: normal lag

Use the normal form when you choose the gain for phase margin first, and then need extra low-frequency gain for the error requirement. The compensator multiplies the DC gain by \(\alpha\) while leaving the gain near crossover at \(K\).

  1. Find \(K\) that satisfies the PM requirement: the gain that makes \(|KG| = 1\) where the phase gives the required PM (plus about 5°).
  2. Evaluate the low-frequency error of \(KG(s)\).
  3. Calculate \(\alpha\) to satisfy the low-frequency (steady-state) error requirement: \(\alpha = K_{v,\text{required}}/K_{v}(KG)\).
  4. Select \(T\) so that the zero break frequency is one decade below \(\omega_c\).
  5. Calculate the pole break frequency \(1/(\alpha T)\).
  6. Draw the compensated Bode plot.
  7. Iterate (mostly first iteration).
Worked example 4.1 Normal lag for ramp error and phase margin

\(G(s) = \dfrac{4}{s(0.5s+1)(0.05s+1)}\) is in unity feedback. Design a normal lag compensator so that the error to a unit ramp is below 0.02 and PM \(\ge 45^\circ\).

1
Required \(K_v = 1/0.02 = 50\) s⁻¹. The uncompensated \(G\) has \(K_v = 4\) and PM \(= 31.7^\circ\) at 2.49 rad/s.
2
K for PM. Phase \(-180^\circ + 45^\circ + 5^\circ = -130^\circ\) at \(\omega = 1.45\) rad/s, where \(|G| = 2.24\). So \(K = 1/2.24 = 0.447\).
3
Error of KG. \(K_v = 4K = 1.79\) s⁻¹, far below 50.
4
α. \(\alpha = 50/1.79 = 28.0\).
5
T. Zero one decade below crossover: \(T = 10/1.45 = 6.92\) s. This gives PM \(= 44.4^\circ\), just short.
6
Iterate. Move the zero further down (a factor of 20 below \(\omega_c\)) to cut the residual lag at crossover: \(T = 13.8\) s, pole at \(1/(\alpha T) = 0.00259\) rad/s. Now PM \(= 47.2^\circ\) at 1.45 rad/s.
\(KD_c(s) = 0.447\times28.0\,\dfrac{13.8s + 1}{387s + 1}\). \(K_v = 4K\alpha = 50\) s⁻¹ (ramp error 0.02) with PM = 47.2°. Bandwidth is only 2.45 rad/s, and the slow pole-zero pair gives a long, low tail in the step response (Figure 4.2), the typical signature of a lag design.

Figure 4.1: The dashed curve is the gain chosen for phase margin; the final design follows it near crossover and is lifted by α (about 29 dB) at low frequency, which is what sets the ramp error.

Check the class example

In the Week 5 lag example (\(G = 10/(s(0.2s+1)(0.005s+1))\), \(\xi \ge 0.4\), ramp error below 0.01) the Bode cursor at 4.64 rad/s reads −16 dB and gives \(K = 6.3\). With the 10 in the numerator, \(|G(j4.64)| = 4.0\) dB, not −16 dB; −16 dB (-16.0 dB) is the magnitude of \(1/(s(0.2s+1)(0.005s+1))\). So the plot was drawn without the 10, and \(K = 6.3\) is the total loop gain (equivalent to \(K = 0.63\) on \(G\) as written). \(\alpha = 100/6.3 = 15.9\) comes out the same either way.

05Choosing between lead and lag

The Lecture 11 comparison shows a lead and a lag design for the same plant and requirements: the lead design crosses over near 49 rad/s, the lag design near 5 rad/s. Both meet the steady-state and phase-margin requirements, but the lead loop is about ten times faster. The phase curves show why: the lead adds phase around crossover; the lag takes some away, but only at low frequency.

Rules of thumb
  • Need a faster response or more phase margin, error already acceptable: lead.
  • Need a smaller steady-state error, transient already acceptable, lower bandwidth acceptable (or wanted, to reject noise): lag.
  • Need both, or the bandwidth must not fall: lead-lag (or PID). Tutorial 3 asks exactly this.
  • If the lead would need more than about 60° of phase, use two lead stages or reconsider.

06Practice problems

Problem 1. Write the normal and the unity DC gain lag with zero at 0.2 rad/s, \(\alpha = 8\) and \(K = 3\). Give each one's DC and high-frequency gain.

\(T = 5\) s, \(\alpha T = 40\) s. Normal: \(24\,\dfrac{5s+1}{40s+1}\), DC 24, HF 3. Unity DC: \(3\,\dfrac{5s+1}{40s+1}\), DC 3, HF 0.375.

Problem 2. With \(KG\) fixed by the error constant, the required PM is reached at 2 rad/s where \(|KG| = 14\) dB. Design the unity DC gain lag.

\(\alpha = 10^{14/20} = 5.01\). Zero at 0.2 rad/s: \(T = 5\) s. Pole at \(1/(\alpha T) = 0.0399\) rad/s. \(D_c = \dfrac{5s+1}{25.1s+1}\).

Problem 3. Tutorial 3, lag part: for \(G = 50/(s(0.2s+1))\) the PM requirement is met at 4.2 rad/s where \(|G| = 19\) dB. Find \(K\) and \(\alpha\) for a ramp error below 0.005.

\(K = 10^{-19/20} = 0.112\). \(K_v(KG) = 50K = 5.61\). Need 200, so \(\alpha = 200/5.61 = 35.7\) (tutorial: 0.11 and 36.4). Zero a decade below 4.2 rad/s: \(T \approx 2.4\) s.

Problem 4. Why is the lag zero placed one decade below crossover, and not closer or further?

At \(\omega_cT = 10\) the lag left at crossover is \(\tan^{-1}10 - \tan^{-1}(10\alpha)\), between 2.8° and 5.7° for any α: small, and covered by the 5° allowance. Half a decade below, it is 15.7° (α = 10) and the margin falls short. Two decades below, it is only 0.5°, but the pole-zero pair is ten times slower, so the slow tail in the step response lasts about ten times longer and \(T\) is ten times larger.

07Formula sheet

Lag forms

Normal: \(K\alpha\dfrac{Ts+1}{\alpha Ts+1}\), \(\alpha \gt 1\)

Unity DC: \(K\dfrac{Ts+1}{\alpha Ts+1}\)

Zero \(1/T\), pole \(1/(\alpha T)\)

Unity DC design

\(K\) from error; new \(\omega_c\) where \(\angle KG = -180^\circ + \text{PM} + 5^\circ\)

\(\alpha = |KG(j\omega_c)|\); \(1/T = \omega_c/10\)

Normal design

\(K = 1/|G(j\omega_c)|\) for PM

\(\alpha = K_{v,req}/K_v(KG)\); \(1/T = \omega_c/10\)

Lead (week 4)

\(\sin\phi_{max} = (1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)\), \(\omega_{max} = 1/(T\sqrt\alpha)\)
