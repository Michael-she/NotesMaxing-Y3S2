CONTROL SYSTEMS 354 · CHAPTER 6

Frequency response and lead compensation

The steady-state response to sinusoids, Bode plots, gain and phase margins, bandwidth, and designing a lead compensator on the Bode plot. Built from Lectures 9 and 10, the Week 4 example and Tutorial 3, with FPE Chapter 6 as the reference.

01Why design in the frequency domain

Weeks 2 and 3 designed controllers to meet time-domain specifications, mostly with step responses and without needing a plant model. The practical also asked for the closed-loop response to sinusoids. This week uses that frequency response to design.

The frequency-response method is popular in industry because it gives good designs when the plant model is uncertain, and because it works directly from experimental data: all you need is the amplitude and phase of the output while the plant is driven by sinusoids, which is the Bode plot. No transforms or intermediate theory are needed, and specifications are often given in frequency terms anyway (bandwidth, amplification, lag).

The method builds on PID ideas: the lead compensator this week is a modified PD controller, and next week's lag compensator is a modified PI controller.

02Response to a sinusoid

For \(Y(s) = G(s)U(s)\) and a sinusoidal input \(u(t) = A\sin(\omega_0t)\,1(t)\), the input transform is \(U(s) = \dfrac{A\omega_0}{s^2 + \omega_0^2}\) (row 17 of FPE Table A.2). The output then has two parts in its partial-fraction expansion: terms from the poles of \(G\), which give the natural transient \(y_1\) and die out if \(G\) is stable, and terms from the input poles \(\pm j\omega_0\), which give the steady-state sinusoid \(y_2\) that remains:

Frequency response (FPE 3.1.2, Example 3.7)\[ \begin{gathered} y_{ss}(t) = AM\sin(\omega_0t + \phi), \qquad M = |G(j\omega_0)| = \sqrt{\{\mathrm{Re}[G(j\omega_0)]\}^2 + \{\mathrm{Im}[G(j\omega_0)]\}^2}, \\ \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{Im}[G(j\omega_0)]}{\mathrm{Re}[G(j\omega_0)]} = \angle G(j\omega_0) \end{gathered} \]
\(A\)
Input amplitude.
\(\omega_0\)
Input frequency (rad/s).
\(M\)
Magnitude ratio: output amplitude over input amplitude, the magnitude of \(G\) at \(s = j\omega_0\).
\(\phi\)
Phase shift: the angle of \(G(j\omega_0)\). In time, the output lags the input by \(\delta t = |\phi|/\omega_0\).
Valid for stable linear systems only

The result needs the transient \(y_1\) to decay, so \(G\) must be stable. The lecture flags a possible sign or form error in the textbook's version of this equation; the form above, \(y = AM\sin(\omega_0t + \phi)\), is the correct one.

Worked example 2.1 Steady-state output

Find the steady-state output of \(G(s) = \dfrac{15}{s + 5}\) driven by \(u(t) = 2\sin 5t\).

1
\(G(j5) = \dfrac{15}{5 + 5j}\). \(M = 15/\sqrt{50} = 2.12\).
2
\(\phi = 0 - \tan^{-1}(5/5) = -45^\circ\).
3
Lag in time: \(\delta t = (\pi/4)/5 = 0.157\) s.
\(y_{ss}(t) = 4.24\sin(5t - 45^\circ)\): amplified by 2.12 and lagging the input by 0.157 s.

03Drawing Bode plots

Drawing Bode plots from first principles (FPE 6.1.1) is self-study, but you need it for the hand-drawn asymptotes in Tutorial 3. Write the transfer function in Bode form, with each factor normalised to 1 at low frequency:

Bode form\[ KG(j\omega) = K_0\,(j\omega)^n\,\frac{(j\omega\tau_1 + 1)(j\omega\tau_2 + 1)\cdots}{(j\omega\tau_a + 1)(j\omega\tau_b + 1)\cdots} \]
\(K_0\)
Bode gain: the low-frequency gain once the \((j\omega)^n\) factor is taken out.
\(n\)
Number of differentiators (positive) or integrators (negative).
\(\tau_i,\,\tau_a\)
Time constants of zeros and poles; each breaks at \(\omega = 1/\tau\).

Because magnitudes multiply, their logs add: each factor's curve in dB is added up, and so are the phases.

FactorMagnitude asymptotePhase
\(K_0\)Flat at \(20\log K_0\)0° (or −180° if negative)
\((j\omega)^n\)Slope \(20n\) dB/decade, through 0 dB at ω = 1\(90n\)°
\((j\omega\tau + 1)^{\pm1}\)0 dB below \(1/\tau\), then ±20 dB/decade0° to ±90°, ±45° at the break; straight line from \(0.1/\tau\) to \(10/\tau\)
Complex pair \(\omega_n, \zeta\)0 dB then −40 dB/decade from \(\omega_n\); resonant peak if ζ is small0° to −180°, −90° at \(\omega_n\)

Figure 3.1: In Bode form this is 10/(s(0.25s + 1)): an integrator (−20 dB/dec, −90°) with gain 10, and a pole breaking at 4 rad/s that steepens the slope to −40 dB/dec and adds another −90° of phase. The largest asymptote error is 3 dB, at the break.

04Stability margins

The closed-loop transfer function is not always available to inspect, but the Bode plot of the open loop \(KG(j\omega)\) still tells you about closed-loop stability (the full criterion, Nyquist's, is FPE 6.2-6.3). The closed-loop poles satisfy \(1 + KG(s) = 0\), that is \(KG(s) = -1\). A closed-loop pole sits on the imaginary axis, so the loop is neutrally stable, when for some \(s = j\omega\):

Neutral stability (FPE 6.2.1)\[ |KG(j\omega)| = 1 \quad\text{and}\quad \angle G(j\omega) = -180^\circ \]
\(K\)
Loop gain (proportional controller).
\(G(j\omega)\)
Plant frequency response.
\(-180^\circ\)
The angle of −1.

For the usual case, where raising the gain eventually causes instability, the closed loop is stable if \(|KG(j\omega)| \lt 1\) at the frequency where the phase is −180°.

Definition

Crossover frequency \(\omega_c\): where \(|KG(j\omega)| = 1\) (0 dB).

Phase margin (PM): how far the phase is above −180° at \(\omega_c\): \(\text{PM} = 180^\circ + \angle KG(j\omega_c)\). Positive means stable.

Gain margin (GM): the factor by which the gain can be raised before instability: \(\text{GM} = 1/|KG(j\omega)|\) at the frequency where the phase is −180°. On a dB plot it is the distance from the curve up to 0 dB. Keep GM > 1 (positive in dB). In some systems raising the gain stabilises the loop; then you keep GM < 1.

Figure 4.1: Crossover is at \(\omega_c = 1.83\) rad/s with PM \(= 56.3^\circ\). The phase reaches −180° at 4.12 rad/s, where the magnitude is 12.5 dB below 0 dB, so GM = 4.20 (12.5 dB).

Phase margin and damping

Phase margin is a measure of damping. For a loop whose closed loop is the standard second-order system, the exact relation (FPE Figure 6.36) is close to a straight line for PM below about 70°:

Damping from phase margin (FPE 6.32)\[ \zeta \approx \frac{\text{PM}}{100} \]
\(\zeta\)
Closed-loop damping ratio.
\(\text{PM}\)
Phase margin in degrees.

Figure 4.2: After FPE Figures 6.36 and 6.37. The curves are computed for the loop ωₙ²/(s(s+2ζωₙ)). Read the overshoot curve backwards to turn an overshoot requirement into a phase margin: 25 % overshoot needs about 45°, 10 % about 58°.

Key idea

These links are exact for second-order systems and guidelines otherwise. A common rule of thumb is to aim for PM > 30°. Watch the units: \(|KG| = 1\) is the same as \(|KG|_{dB} = 0\) dB.

Worked example 4.1 Reading margins

For the unity-feedback loop \(L(s) = \dfrac{30}{(s+1)(s+2)(s+5)}\), find \(\omega_c\), PM, GM and estimate the closed-loop damping.

1
Solve \(|L(j\omega)| = 1\) numerically: \(\omega_c = 1.83\) rad/s. \(\angle L(j\omega_c) = -\tan^{-1}\omega_c - \tan^{-1}(\omega_c/2) - \tan^{-1}(\omega_c/5) = -124^\circ\), so PM \(= 56.3^\circ\).
2
Phase −180° at \(\omega = 4.12\) rad/s (exactly \(\sqrt{17}\), from the imaginary part of the denominator). There \(|L| = 0.238\), so GM \(= 4.20\).
3
\(\zeta \approx 56.3/100 = 0.56\).
PM ≈ 56.3°, GM ≈ 4.20 (12.5 dB): the gain could rise by a factor of about 4.20 before the loop goes unstable, consistent with the gain at which the closed-loop poles reach the imaginary axis (substituting \(s = j\omega\) gives \(\omega = \sqrt{17}\) and \(K = 126\)) for \(K\)/((s+1)(s+2)(s+5)), which is \(126/30 = 4.2\) times the present 30.

05Closed-loop frequency response and bandwidth

Margins come from the open loop and estimate closed-loop stability. The actual performance is in the closed-loop frequency response \(T(j\omega)\). Its key figure is the bandwidth \(\omega_{BW}\): the highest frequency at which the system tracks a sinusoidal reference satisfactorily. By convention it is where \(|T(j\omega)|\) has fallen to 0.707 of its low-frequency value (−3 dB).

Bandwidth measures speed of response, like rise time in the time domain: a wider bandwidth means a faster system. It is related to the natural frequency of the closed-loop poles, and to the open-loop crossover frequency, because \(|KG| \gg 1\) below \(\omega_c\) (so \(T \approx 1\)) and \(|KG| \ll 1\) above it (so \(T \approx KG\)):

Bandwidth estimates\[ \omega_c \le \omega_{BW} \le 2\omega_c, \qquad \omega_n \le \omega_{BW} \le 2\omega_n \]
\(\omega_c\)
Open-loop crossover frequency, where \(|KG(j\omega)| = 1\).
\(\omega_{BW}\)
Closed-loop bandwidth, where \(|T(j\omega)| = 0.707\) (−3 dB).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency of the dominant closed-loop poles.

Figure 5.1: Here \(\omega_c = 1.83\) rad/s and \(\omega_{BW} = 3.42\) rad/s, inside the band \(\omega_c\) to \(2\omega_c\). The closed loop has a resonant peak just below crossover because the phase margin is moderate; the lower the PM, the higher the peak.

Key idea

Design uses \(\omega_c\) as the handle on bandwidth: push the crossover up for a faster system (shorter \(t_r\), \(t_s\)), and set the phase there for damping (overshoot).

06PD compensation

Design now treats the controller as a compensator \(D_c\) in the loop. The characteristic equation is \(1 + D_cG = 0\) and \(L(s) = D_cG(s)\) is the loop gain. PD control in time-constant form is:

PD compensator\[ D(s) = k_Ds + k_P \;\;\rightarrow\;\; D_c(s) = T_Ds + 1 \]
\(k_D,\,k_P\)
Derivative and proportional gains.
\(T_D\)
Derivative time (s); the zero breaks at \(\omega = 1/T_D\).

Above \(1/T_D\) the magnitude rises at +20 dB/decade and the phase rises towards +90° (FPE Figure 6.51). The added phase lead near crossover increases the phase margin: better stability. The price is that the gain keeps rising with frequency, so the compensator amplifies high-frequency noise.

Reading FPE Ch 6.7.1 to 6.7.2; Lecture 10 slides 7 to 8

07The lead compensator

The lead compensator adds a pole above the zero to stop the gain rising for ever:

Lead compensator (FPE 6.38)\[ KD_c(s) = K\,\frac{T_Ds + 1}{\alpha T_Ds + 1}, \qquad \alpha \lt 1 \]
\(K\)
Gain, chosen for the error or bandwidth requirement.
\(T_D\)
Time constant of the zero (often just \(T\)); zero breaks at \(1/T_D\).
\(\alpha\)
Ratio of zero to pole break frequency; \(1/\alpha\) is the lead ratio. The pole breaks at \(1/(\alpha T_D)\).

Figure 7.1: The gain rises by \(1/\alpha\) (20 dB here) between the two breaks, then levels off, so high-frequency noise is amplified by at most \(1/\alpha\). The phase peaks at \(\phi_{max} = 54.9^\circ\) halfway between the breaks on the log scale.

The maximum phase occurs at the geometric mean of the two break frequencies, and depends only on \(\alpha\):

Maximum phase lead (FPE 6.39, 6.40)\[ \begin{gathered} \omega_{max} = \frac{1}{T\sqrt{\alpha}}, \qquad \sin\phi_{max} = \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 + \alpha} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \alpha = \frac{1 - \sin\phi_{max}}{1 + \sin\phi_{max}}, \\ |D_c(j\omega_{max})| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \end{gathered} \]
\(\omega_{max}\)
Frequency of maximum phase lead (rad/s).
\(T\)
Zero time constant, \(T_D\).
\(\phi_{max}\)
Maximum phase lead.
\(\alpha\)
Zero/pole ratio, less than 1.

Figure 7.2: Each extra degree costs more and more high-frequency gain. Above about 60° (1/α ≈ 14) a single lead stage becomes inefficient; use two in series instead.

The compensator lets you shape three things: the crossover frequency (bandwidth, \(t_r\), \(t_s\)), the phase margin (\(\zeta\), \(M_p\)) and, through \(K\), the low-frequency gain (\(e_{ss}\)).

08Lead design procedure

  1. Find \(K\) to meet the steady-state error (or bandwidth) requirement.
  2. Evaluate the phase margin of \(KG(s)\).
  3. Work out how much phase must be added to meet the PM requirement, plus a safety margin (5° to 12°) because the crossover will move up.
  4. Calculate \(\alpha\) from \(\sin\phi_{max} = (1 - \alpha)/(1 + \alpha)\), or read it from Figure 7.2.
  5. Calculate \(T\) from \(\omega_{max} = 1/(T\sqrt{\alpha})\), placing \(\omega_{max}\) at \(\omega_c\) of \(KG(s)\).
  6. Draw the compensated Bode plot and check the PM.
  7. Iterate if needed. The course focuses on the first iteration.
Worked example 8.1 Lead design for velocity error and phase margin

A unity-feedback plant is \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s(s+2)}\). Design a lead compensator so that \(K_v \ge 20\) s⁻¹ and PM \(\ge 45^\circ\).

1
Gain. \(K_v = \lim_{s\to0} sKD_cG = K/2\) (since \(D_c(0) = 1\)). \(K_v = 20 \Rightarrow K = 40\).
2
Current PM. \(|KG(j\omega)| = 1\) at \(\omega_c = 6.17\) rad/s; \(\angle KG = -90^\circ - \tan^{-1}(\omega_c/2) = -162.0^\circ\), so PM \(= 18.0^\circ\). Far too little.
3
Phase to add. \(45^\circ - 18.0^\circ + 10^\circ\) (safety) \(= 37.0^\circ\).
4
α. \(\alpha = \dfrac{1 - \sin 37.0^\circ}{1 + \sin 37.0^\circ} = 0.248\) (lead ratio 4.03).
5
T. With \(\omega_{max} = \omega_c = 6.17\): \(T = \dfrac{1}{6.17\sqrt{0.248}} = 0.325\) s. So \(KD_c(s) = 40\,\dfrac{0.325s + 1}{0.0808s + 1}\).
6
Check. The compensator's gain moves the crossover up to 10.3 rad/s, where its phase is below the peak: PM \(= 44.7^\circ\), just short of 45°.
7
Iterate. Put \(\omega_{max}\) at the frequency where \(|KG| = \sqrt{\alpha}\) (\(-10\log_{10}(1/\alpha)\) dB), which is where the compensated curve will cross: \(\omega_{max} = 8.85\) rad/s, \(T = 0.227\) s. Now \(\omega_c = 8.85\) rad/s and PM \(= 49.8^\circ\).
\(KD_c(s) = 40\,\dfrac{0.227s + 1}{0.0563s + 1}\) meets both requirements: \(K_v = 20\) s⁻¹ and PM = 49.8°. Overshoot falls from 60 % to 22 % and the bandwidth rises from 9.65 to 14.5 rad/s (Figures 8.1 and 8.2).

Figure 8.1: The lead lifts the phase near crossover while also pushing the crossover to higher frequency, which is why the first iteration, centred on the old crossover, falls slightly short.

The Week 4 class example

The class example (\(G = 10/(s(0.1s+1))\), ramp error constant ≥ 100, overshoot ≤ 25 %) follows the same steps: \(K = 10\), crossover of \(KG\) near 31 rad/s with PM about 18°, a required PM of about 45° from FPE Figure 6.37, then \(\alpha \approx 0.167\) and \(T\) from \(\omega_{max}\). Tutorial 3 does it for \(G = 50/(s(0.2s+1))\), where the first iteration again falls short and the advice is to add the phase at a higher frequency.

Exam

Lead compensation helps the transient (more PM, more bandwidth) but leaves the low-frequency gain, and so the steady-state error, as set by \(K\). If a design also must not increase the bandwidth, a lead is the wrong tool; week 5's lag compensator does the opposite.

09Practice problems

Problem 1. Find the steady-state output of \(G(s) = \dfrac{20}{s + 10}\) for \(u(t) = 3\sin 10t\). (Tutorial 3 problem 1.1 uses this plant.)

\(G(j10) = 20/(10 + 10j)\): \(M = 1.41\), \(\phi = -45^\circ\). \(y_{ss} = 4.24\sin(10t - 45^\circ)\), lagging by \(\pi/40 = 0.0785\) s.

Problem 2. Estimate PM and GM for \(G(s) = \dfrac{10}{(s+1)^2}\) with P control \(k_P = 1\) and with D control \(k_D = 1\).

P: \(|G| = 1\) at \(\omega = 3\) rad/s, \(\angle = -2\tan^{-1}3 = -143^\circ\), PM ≈ 36.9°. The phase only approaches −180°, so GM = ∞. D: \(L = 10s/(s+1)^2\), \(|L| = 10\omega/(1+\omega^2) = 1\) at \(\omega = 0.101\) and \(\omega = 9.90\) rad/s. The crossover is the upper one, where \(|L|\) falls through 1: \(\angle L = 90^\circ - 2\tan^{-1}9.9 = -78.5^\circ\), so PM ≈ 101.5°; GM = ∞. D control gives the larger margin (Tutorial 3 answers ≈ 45° and ≈ 90° from asymptotes).

Problem 3. A lead must add 40° of phase at 10 rad/s. Find \(\alpha\) and \(T\), and the compensator's high-frequency gain increase.

\(\alpha = (1 - \sin40^\circ)/(1 + \sin40^\circ) = 0.217\). \(T = 1/(10\sqrt{0.217}) = 0.214\) s. Gain rises by \(1/\alpha = 4.60\) (13.3 dB) at high frequency.

Problem 4. An overshoot of at most 15 % is required. What phase margin should you design for?

\(\zeta = 0.517\) for 15 % overshoot, so PM ≈ 53.2° from Figure 4.2 (the PM/100 rule gives about 51.7°).

Problem 5. Tutorial 3: \(G = \dfrac{50}{s(0.2s+1)}\), ramp error below 0.005. What is the minimum \(K\), and the PM of \(KG\) with \(K = 5\)?

\(K_v = 50K \ge 200 \Rightarrow K \ge 4\). With \(K = 5\): \(\omega_c = 35.2\) rad/s, PM \(= 8.1^\circ\) (tutorial: 35 rad/s, 8°).

10Formula sheet

Sinusoidal response

\(y_{ss} = AM\sin(\omega_0t + \phi)\)

\(M = |G(j\omega_0)|\), \(\phi = \angle G(j\omega_0)\)

Margins

\(|KG(j\omega_c)| = 1\)

PM \(= 180^\circ + \angle KG(j\omega_c)\)

GM \(= 1/|KG|\) where \(\angle = -180^\circ\)

\(\zeta \approx \text{PM}/100\); aim PM > 30°

Bandwidth

\(|T(j\omega_{BW})| = 0.707\) (−3 dB)

\(\omega_c \le \omega_{BW} \le 2\omega_c\)

Lead

\(KD_c = K\dfrac{Ts+1}{\alpha Ts+1}\), \(\alpha \lt 1\)

\(\omega_{max} = 1/(T\sqrt{\alpha})\)

\(\sin\phi_{max} = (1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)\)

\(|D_c(j\omega_{max})| = 1/\sqrt{\alpha}\)
