State-space models, canonical forms and transformations

State-variable models and their transfer functions, the control, observer and modal canonical forms, state transformations, and the controllability and observability tests that the following weeks build on. Built from the Ch 7.4 state transformation slides and Tutorial 5.

01State-variable models

Weeks 1 to 6 used transfer functions. From week 7 the module moves to modern control, built on the state-variable form of FPE Chapter 7. A system of order \(n\) is written as \(n\) first-order differential equations in the state vector \(\mathbf{x}\), plus an output equation:

State and output model (FPE 7.3)\[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}u, \qquad y = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} + Du \]
\(\mathbf{x}\)
State vector, \(n \times 1\): the smallest set of variables that, with the input, fixes the future of the system.
\(\mathbf{A}\)
System matrix, \(n \times n\).
\(\mathbf{B}\)
Input matrix, \(n \times 1\) for one input.
\(\mathbf{C}\)
Output matrix, \(1 \times n\) for one output.
\(D\)
Direct transmission term, \(1 \times 1\) (usually 0).
\(u,\,y\)
Input and output.

Taking Laplace transforms with zero initial conditions gives the transfer function, and its poles are the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}\):

Transfer function from a state model (FPE 7.45)\[ G(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + D = \frac{\mathbf{C}\,\mathrm{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{B}}{\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})} + D \]
\(\mathbf{I}\)
Identity matrix, \(n \times n\).
\(\mathrm{adj}\)
Adjugate (transpose of the cofactor matrix).
\(\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\)
Characteristic polynomial; its roots are the poles (the eigenvalues of A).
Worked example 1.1 From physics to a state model and back

A mass \(m = 1\) kg on a spring \(k = 6\) N/m with damper \(b = 5\) N s/m is driven by a force \(u\); the output is the position \(y\). Take \(\mathbf{x} = [y \;\; \dot y]^T\). Find \(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, D\), the poles and \(G(s)\).

1
Newton: \(\ddot y = -6y - 5\dot y + u\). With \(x_1 = y\), \(x_2 = \dot y\): \(\dot x_1 = x_2\), \(\dot x_2 = -6x_1 - 5x_2 + u\).
2
\(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}\) (2 × 2), \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (2 × 1), \(\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) (1 × 2), \(D = 0\).
3
\(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 6 & s+5 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det = s^2 + 5s + 6 = (s+2)(s+3)\): poles at −2 and −3.
4
\(\mathrm{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s+5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix}\) (swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal). \(\mathbf{C}\,\mathrm{adj}\,\mathbf{B} = [1\;\;0]\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ s \end{bmatrix} = 1\).
\(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s^2 + 5s + 6}\), DC gain \(1/6\) m/N: a steady force of 1 N stretches the spring by \(1/k\).

02Control canonical form

The same system has many state models: any choice of state variables works. A different selection of states gives a different form of the state and output model. Three canonical forms are useful because each makes a particular calculation easy, and each can be drawn as a block diagram. Start from

Transfer function to realise\[ H(s) = \frac{b_1s^{n-1} + b_2s^{n-2} + \cdots + b_n}{s^n + a_1s^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n} \]
\(a_i\)
Denominator coefficients (the polynomial is monic).
\(b_i\)
Numerator coefficients; a strictly proper \(H\) has \(D = 0\).

In control canonical form the states are the outputs of a chain of integrators, and the input enters only the first one (FPE's integrator-chain convention):

Control canonical form (FPE 7.19)\[ \mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & -a_2 & \cdots & -a_n \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C}_c = [\,b_1 \;\; b_2 \;\; \cdots \;\; b_n\,], \quad D_c = 0 \]
\(\mathbf{A}_c\)
Denominator coefficients, negated, along the top row; ones on the subdiagonal.
\(\mathbf{B}_c\)
Input enters the first integrator only.
\(\mathbf{C}_c\)
Numerator coefficients.

Figure 2.1: The input feeds the first integrator; each integrator output feeds the next. The a-gains feed every state back to the input sum (this is where the poles come from); the b-gains add the states to form the output (this is where the zeros come from).

The numerator coefficients never appear in \(\mathbf{A}\): \(\mathbf{A}\) fixes the poles, which depend only on the denominator. The zeros are set by how the states are combined at the output, through \(\mathbf{C}\).

Worked example 2.1 Control canonical form by inspection

Write the control canonical form of \(H(s) = \dfrac{s + 5}{s^3 + 6s^2 + 11s + 6}\).

1
\(n = 3\); \(a_1 = 6\), \(a_2 = 11\), \(a_3 = 6\). The numerator \(s + 5 = 0s^2 + 1s + 5\), so \(b_1 = 0\), \(b_2 = 1\), \(b_3 = 5\).
2
Fill in the pattern.
\(\mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -6 & -11 & -6 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{C}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\), \(D_c = 0\).

03Observer canonical form

The observer canonical form is the transpose (dual) of the control form:

Observer canonical form (FPE 7.27)\[ \mathbf{A}_o = \mathbf{A}_c^T, \qquad \mathbf{B}_o = \mathbf{C}_c^T, \qquad \mathbf{C}_o = \mathbf{B}_c^T, \qquad D_o = D_c \]
\(\mathbf{A}_o,\,\mathbf{B}_o,\,\mathbf{C}_o\)
Observer canonical matrices.
\(^T\)
Transpose.

Why it works: a scalar transfer function equals its own transpose, so \(H = \mathbf{C}_c(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_c)^{-1}\mathbf{B}_c\), which equals \(\mathbf{B}_c^T(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_c^T)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_c^T\). In the block diagram the integrator chain runs the other way: the output is the last integrator, the a-gains feed the output back into every integrator, and the b-gains distribute the input to every integrator. The 'observer' name comes from week 8 onwards, where this structure makes estimator design easy.

Worked example 3.1 Observer canonical form

Write the observer canonical form of the \(H(s)\) in example 2.1.

1
Transpose: \(\mathbf{A}_o = \mathbf{A}_c^T\), \(\mathbf{B}_o = \mathbf{C}_c^T\), \(\mathbf{C}_o = \mathbf{B}_c^T\).
\(\mathbf{A}_o = \begin{bmatrix} -6 & 1 & 0 \\ -11 & 0 & 1 \\ -6 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B}_o = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{C}_o = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\).

04Modal (normal) canonical form

Expanding \(H(s)\) in partial fractions writes it as a sum of first-order terms, one per pole. Each term is a separate first-order state, so \(\mathbf{A}\) is diagonal with the poles on the diagonal:

Modal canonical form (FPE 7.4.1)\[ H(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_i}{s - p_i} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{A}_m = \mathrm{diag}(p_1, \ldots, p_n), \quad \mathbf{B}_m = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C}_m = [\,r_1 \;\; \cdots \;\; r_n\,] \]
\(p_i\)
Poles of H (distinct).
\(r_i\)
Residues at each pole.
\(\mathbf{B}_m\)
Chosen as all ones; then C holds the residues. (Any split of \(r_i\) between B and C works.)
Worked example 4.1 Modal form by partial fractions

Find the modal form of \(H(s) = \dfrac{s+5}{(s+1)(s+2)(s+3)}\) (example 2.1).

1
Residues by cover-up: at −1, \(\dfrac{4}{(1)(2)} = 2\); at −2, \(\dfrac{3}{(-1)(1)} = -3\); at −3, \(\dfrac{2}{(-2)(-1)} = 1\).
2
\(H(s) = \dfrac{2}{s+1} - \dfrac{3}{s+2} + \dfrac{1}{s+3}\).
\(\mathbf{A}_m = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B}_m = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{C}_m = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Figure 4.1: The modes are decoupled: each state is driven by the input alone and evolves independently, and the output is a weighted sum. That is why it is a set of parallel first-order paths rather than a chain.

Two complications

  • Complex poles. A pair \(\sigma \pm j\omega\) would need complex entries. To stay with real numbers, the pair is replaced by a real \(2 \times 2\) block \(\begin{bmatrix} \sigma & \omega \\ -\omega & \sigma \end{bmatrix}\), whose characteristic polynomial is \((s - \sigma)^2 + \omega^2\). The model is then block-diagonal, not diagonal.
  • Repeated poles. A double pole gives terms \(\dfrac{r_1}{s-p} + \dfrac{r_2}{(s-p)^2}\); the second needs two integrators in series, so the block becomes \(\begin{bmatrix} p & 1 \\ 0 & p \end{bmatrix}\) (a Jordan block) with an off-diagonal 1.

05State transformations

Each canonical form suits a particular calculation. If the model is in no special form, or in the wrong one, transform it. The form of the model depends on the state vector, so to change the form you change the state vector, with a non-singular matrix \(\mathbf{T}\):

State transformation (FPE 7.4.2)\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z} \;\Rightarrow\; \dot{\mathbf{z}} = \bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{z} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}u, \;\; y = \bar{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{z} + \bar Du, \\ \bar{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}, \;\; \bar{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}, \;\; \bar{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}, \;\; \bar D = D \end{gathered} \]
\(\mathbf{x}\)
Original state vector.
\(\mathbf{z}\)
New state vector.
\(\mathbf{T}\)
Transformation matrix, \(n \times n\), \(\det\mathbf{T} \ne 0\).
\(\bar{\mathbf{A}},\,\bar{\mathbf{B}},\,\bar{\mathbf{C}},\,\bar D\)
Matrices of the transformed model.

Derivation: substitute \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}\) into \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}u\) to get \(\mathbf{T}\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{B}u\), then multiply by \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\).

Unchanged by x = TzChanged by x = Tz

\(\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A} = -a_1\) and \(\det\mathbf{A} = (-1)^na_n\)The state trajectories \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) versus \(\mathbf{z}(t)\)

Worked example 5.1 Three realisations, one system

Check that the control, observer and modal realisations of \(H(s) = \dfrac{s+5}{s^3+6s^2+11s+6}\) agree.

1
\(\mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\) at \(s = 2\): all three give \(0.1167\), equal to \(H(2) = 7/60\).
2
Trace and determinant of each \(\mathbf{A}\): \(\mathrm{tr} = -6\), \(\det = -6\) for all three. These are \(-a_1 = -6\) and \((-1)^3a_3 = -6\): the sum and product of the poles −1, −2, −3.
3
Simulate a unit step (Figure 5.1): the state trajectories differ completely, the outputs coincide.
Same transfer function, same poles, same output; different states. The states are an internal choice of coordinates.

06Finding the transformation

To modal form: eigenvectors

\(\mathbf{T}\) whose columns are the eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) (with \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i = p_i\mathbf{v}_i\)) diagonalises \(\mathbf{A}\): \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \mathrm{diag}(p_i)\). A neat way to find eigenvectors by hand is the adjugate:

Eigenvectors from the adjugate\[ \mathbf{M}\,\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{M}) = \det(\mathbf{M})\,\mathbf{I} \;\Rightarrow\; (p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\,\mathrm{adj}(p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0} \]
\(\mathbf{M}\)
Any square matrix; here \(p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}\).
\(p_i\)
An eigenvalue, so \(\det(p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0\).

So every non-zero column of \(\mathrm{adj}(p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\) satisfies \((p_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\): it is an eigenvector. Form \(\mathrm{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\) once as a matrix of polynomials, then substitute each pole. MATLAB does the same with canon(sys,'modal').

Worked example 6.1 Diagonalising the mass-spring model

Transform the model of example 1.1 to modal form.

1
From example 1.1, \(\mathrm{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s+5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix}\).
2
At \(p = -2\): \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -6 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\); first column \([3\;\;-6]^T \propto [1\;\;-2]^T\). At \(p = -3\): \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -6 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\); second column \([1\;\;-3]^T\). Check: \(\mathbf{A}[1\;\;-2]^T = [-2\;\;4]^T = -2[1\;\;-2]^T\) ✓.
3
\(\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det\mathbf{T} = -1\), \(\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\).
4
\(\bar{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\bar{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\bar{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).
\(G(s) = \dfrac{1\cdot1}{s+2} + \dfrac{1\cdot(-1)}{s+3} = \dfrac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)}\), unchanged. Scaling an eigenvector by 2 would halve the matching entry of \(\bar{\mathbf{B}}\) and double that of \(\bar{\mathbf{C}}\): the matrices change but \(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(G\) do not.

To control canonical form: controllability matrices

Under \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}\) the controllability matrix (section 7) transforms as \(\bar{\mathcal{C}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathcal{C}\). If \(\bar{\mathcal{C}}\) is the controllability matrix of the control canonical form (built from the characteristic polynomial), then

Transformation to control canonical form (FPE 7.4.2, p. 495)\[ \mathbf{T} = \mathcal{C}_x\,\mathcal{C}_z^{-1} \]
\(\mathcal{C}_x\)
Controllability matrix of the given model.
\(\mathcal{C}_z\)
Controllability matrix of the target (control canonical) model.
\(\mathbf{T}\)
Transformation with \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}\).

This needs \(\mathcal{C}_x\) to be invertible: a model can be put in control canonical form only if it is controllable.

Worked example 6.2 Mass-spring to control canonical form

Find \(\mathbf{T}\) that takes example 1.1 to control canonical form.

1
Characteristic polynomial \(s^2 + 5s + 6\): \(\mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -5 & -6 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\).
2
\(\mathcal{C}_x = [\mathbf{B}\;\;\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}\); \(\mathcal{C}_z = [\mathbf{B}_c\;\;\mathbf{A}_c\mathbf{B}_c] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).
3
\(\mathbf{T} = \mathcal{C}_x\mathcal{C}_z^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\).
\(\mathbf{T}\) just swaps the two states (\(z_1 = \dot y\), \(z_2 = y\)), and \(\bar{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = [0\;\;1]\), matching \(b_1 = 0\), \(b_2 = 1\).

07Controllability and observability

Controllability and observability tests (FPE 7.5 and 7.7)\[ \mathcal{C} = [\,\mathbf{B} \;\; \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \;\; \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B} \;\; \cdots \;\; \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\mathbf{B}\,], \qquad \mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{n-1} \end{bmatrix} \]
\(\mathcal{C}\)
Controllability matrix, \(n \times n\) for one input. Controllable if \(\det\mathcal{C} \ne 0\).
\(\mathcal{O}\)
Observability matrix, \(n \times n\) for one output. Observable if \(\det\mathcal{O} \ne 0\). (The formula sheet writes its transpose.)

A system is controllable if the input can drive every state (every mode) anywhere; observable if every state can be worked out from the output. A transformation never changes either property, but the states you initially choose can: controllability is a property of the state model, not of the transfer function.

In modal form you can read both off directly: a mode is uncontrollable if its row of \(\mathbf{B}\) is zero (the input cannot reach it), and unobservable if its column of \(\mathbf{C}\) is zero (it never shows in the output). In the transfer function, an uncontrollable or unobservable mode disappears through a pole-zero cancellation.

Worked example 7.1 Running the tests

Test (a) the mass-spring model of example 1.1, (b) \(\mathbf{A} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -4)\), \(\mathbf{B} = [1\;\;0]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [1\;\;1]\), (c) \(\mathbf{A} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -4)\), \(\mathbf{B} = [1\;\;1]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [0\;\;1]\).

1
(a) \(\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det = -1\); \(\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det = 1\). Both 2 × 2 and non-singular: controllable and observable.
2
(b) \(\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det = 0\): uncontrollable (zero in row 2 of B, the mode at −4). \(\det\mathcal{O} = -3\): observable. \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s+1}\): the −4 mode is gone.
3
(c) \(\det\mathcal{C} = -3\): controllable. \(\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det = 0\): unobservable (zero in column 1 of C, the mode at −1). \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s+4}\).
Both failures cancel a pole from the transfer function, so a second-order model behaves like a first-order one from input to output. The hidden mode is still there: if it were unstable, the system would be unstable even though \(G(s)\) looks fine.
Looking ahead

Why this matters next: controllability is the condition for placing all closed-loop poles with state feedback, \(\det[s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{K}] = 0\); observability is the condition for building an estimator of the states from the output, \(\det[s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{C}] = 0\). Both equations are on the formula sheet.

08Matrix inverse by hand, and MATLAB

Hand work needs \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\) and \((s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\). Use the adjugate (Cramer's rule): \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{\det\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}^T\), where \(\mathbf{C}\) is the cofactor matrix, \(C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\), and \(M_{ij}\) is the determinant with row \(i\) and column \(j\) removed. For 2 × 2: swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant. The 3 × 3 formula is on the formula sheet.

P = eig(A)                          % poles
sysG = ss(A,B,C,D);                 % state model
[sysGm,TI] = canon(sysG,'modal');   % modal form, z = TI*x
[Am,Bm,Cm,Dm] = ssdata(sysGm);
T = inv(TI);                        % so that x = T*z
ctrb(A,B), obsv(A,C)                % test matrices
tf(sysG)                            % back to a transfer function
Watch out

MATLAB's canon returns the matrix that maps \(\mathbf{x}\) to \(\mathbf{z}\) (\(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{T}_I\mathbf{x}\)), which is the inverse of the course's \(\mathbf{T}\). It also scales eigenvectors its own way, so its \(\bar{\mathbf{B}}\) and \(\bar{\mathbf{C}}\) may differ from yours by factors that cancel. Tutorial 5 asks for all matrix work by hand first.

09Practice problems

Problem 1. Series RLC: \(L = 0.5\) H, \(R = 3\) Ω, \(C = 0.25\) F, input \(v_{in}\), output \(v_C\), \(\mathbf{x} = [v_C\;\;i]^T\). Find the model, poles and \(G(s)\). (Tutorial 5 problem 1.)

\(C\dot v_C = i\), \(L\dot i = v_{in} - Ri - v_C\): \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ -2 & -6 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B} = [0\;\;2]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [1\;\;0]\). \(\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = s^2 + 6s + 8\), poles −2, −4. \(G = 8/((s+2)(s+4))\), DC gain 1 (the capacitor charges to the source voltage).

Problem 2. Write the control and modal forms of \(H(s) = \dfrac{3s + 1}{s^2 + 3s + 2}\).

Control: \(\mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B}_c = [1\;\;0]^T\), \(\mathbf{C}_c = [3\;\;1]\). Modal: residues \(-2/1 = -2\) at −1 and \(-5/(-1) = 5\) at −2: \(\mathbf{A} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -2)\), \(\mathbf{B} = [1\;\;1]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [-2\;\;5]\).

Problem 3. Why can \(F(s) = \dfrac{s+4}{(s+1)(s^2+4s+13)}\) not have a diagonal modal form with real numbers? Give its real block.

Poles −1 and \(-2 \pm 3j\). The complex pair needs the real block \(\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 3 \\ -3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\) (characteristic polynomial \(s^2 + 4s + 13\)). The residue at −1 is \(3/10 = 0.3\).

Problem 4. \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B} = [0\;\;1]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [1\;\;1]\). Controllable? Observable? \(G(s)\)?

Row 1 of B is zero: the −2 mode is uncontrollable (\(\det\mathcal{C} = 0\)). C has no zero column: observable. \(G = 1/(s+5)\).
