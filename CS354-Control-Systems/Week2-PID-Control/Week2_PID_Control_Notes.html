A first analysis of feedback and PID control

The equations behind stability, sensitivity, tracking and regulation; system type and steady-state error; the P, I and D terms; and Ziegler-Nichols tuning. Built from Lectures 4 to 6 and Tutorial 2, with FPE Chapter 4.1 to 4.3 as the reference.

01The four objectives of feedback

Week 1 listed four requirements that guide every design. Chapter 4 turns each of them into an equation you can test:

RequirementBecomesSection of FPE
Stable operationStability: closed-loop poles in the LHP4.1.1
Reference trackingTracking: output follows the reference4.1.2
Disturbance rejectionRegulation: output unaffected by disturbances4.1.3
Robust performanceSensitivity: response unaffected by plant changes4.1.4

All four are judged on the standard loop in Figure 1.1. The controller in the loop is called \(D_{cl}(s)\); an open-loop controller is \(D_{ol}(s)\).

Figure 1.1: The signals are the reference \(R\), the control \(U\), the disturbance \(W\), the output \(Y\) and the sensor noise \(V\). After FPE Figure 4.2.

Closed-loop output (FPE 4.5)\[ Y_{cl} = \frac{GD_{cl}}{1+GD_{cl}}R + \frac{G}{1+GD_{cl}}W - \frac{GD_{cl}}{1+GD_{cl}}V \]
\(Y_{cl}\)
Closed-loop output.
\(G\)
Plant transfer function.
\(D_{cl}\)
Controller in the closed loop.
\(R\)
Reference.
\(W\)
Disturbance, entering at the plant input.
\(V\)
Sensor noise.

Every term shares the denominator \(1 + GD_{cl}\). That is why one controller decides stability, tracking, regulation and noise response together, and why improving one can hurt another.

02Stability: open versus closed loop

Write the plant and controller as ratios of polynomials, \(G = b(s)/a(s)\) and \(D = c(s)/d(s)\). In open loop the overall transfer function is simply the product:

Open loop\[ T_{ol}(s) = G(s)D_{ol}(s) = \frac{b(s)c(s)}{a(s)d(s)} \]
\(T_{ol}\)
Open-loop transfer function from R to Y.
\(b,\,a\)
Numerator and denominator of the plant.
\(c,\,d\)
Numerator and denominator of the controller.

The poles of \(T_{ol}\) are the roots of \(a(s)\) and \(d(s)\): the controller cannot move the plant's poles. If the plant has an RHP pole the only way to remove it in open loop is to put a zero exactly on top of it, which never works in practice because the pole is never exactly where the model says. Neither \(a(s)\) nor \(d(s)\) may have RHP roots.

In closed loop the denominator becomes \(1 + GD_{cl}\), and setting it to zero gives the characteristic equation:

Characteristic equation (FPE 4.15, 4.16)\[ 1 + \frac{b(s)c(s)}{a(s)d(s)} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a(s)d(s) + b(s)c(s) = 0 \]
\(a(s)d(s) + b(s)c(s)\)
Closed-loop characteristic polynomial; its roots are the closed-loop poles.
Key idea

Feedback changes the characteristic polynomial, so the controller can move the poles. This is how feedback stabilises an unstable plant.

Worked example 2.1 Stabilising an unstable plant

\(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s-2}\). Show that open-loop control cannot stabilise it and find the proportional gains \(D_{cl} = K\) that can.

1
Open loop: \(T_{ol} = K/(s-2)\). The pole stays at +2 for every \(K\): unstable.
2
Closed loop: \(a d + b c = (s-2)(1) + (1)(K) = s - 2 + K\). The closed-loop pole is at \(s = 2 - K\).
3
Stable when \(2 - K \lt 0\).
Any \(K \gt 2\) stabilises the loop; for example \(K = 5\) puts the pole at −3 (time constant 0.33 s).
Watch out

Be careful with pole-zero cancellations. Cancelling an RHP pole with a controller zero (or an RHP zero with a controller pole) hides an unstable mode inside the loop: the transfer function looks fine but an internal signal grows without bound.

03Sensitivity

A design is done on a model, but the real plant is \(G + \delta G\). Sensitivity measures how much the overall transfer function changes, as a fraction, for a fractional change in the plant:

Sensitivity (FPE 4.18)\[ \mathcal{S}_G^T = \frac{\delta T/T}{\delta G/G} \]
\(\mathcal{S}_G^T\)
Sensitivity of T with respect to G (dimensionless).
\(\delta T/T\)
Fractional change in the overall transfer function.
\(\delta G/G\)
Fractional change in the plant.

So an \(x\) % change in \(G\) gives an \(x\mathcal{S}\) % change in \(T\).

Open- and closed-loop sensitivity (FPE 4.20, 4.22)\[ \text{Open loop: } \frac{\delta T_{ol}}{T_{ol}} = \frac{D_{ol}\,\delta G}{D_{ol}G} = \frac{\delta G}{G} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathcal{S} = 1, \qquad \text{Closed loop: } \mathcal{S}_G^{T_{cl}} = \frac{1}{1 + GD_{cl}} \]
\(T_{ol},\,T_{cl}\)
Open- and closed-loop transfer functions.
\(D_{ol},\,D_{cl}\)
Open- and closed-loop controllers.
\(GD_{cl}\)
Loop gain.

With a loop gain of 100 at DC, \(1 + GD_{cl} \approx 100\), so a 10 % change in \(G\) gives about a 0.1 % change in the steady-state gain. The open-loop controller is 100 times more sensitive.

Worked example 3.1 Checking the sensitivity estimate

\(G(s) = \dfrac{4}{s+2}\) (DC gain 2) is controlled by \(D_{cl} = 24\) in unity feedback. The plant gain rises by 5 %. How much does the closed-loop DC gain change?

1
DC loop gain \(GD_{cl}(0) = 24 \times 2 = 48\), so \(\mathcal{S} = 1/49 = 0.0204\).
2
Predicted change: \(5\,\% \times 0.0204 = 0.102\,\%\).
3
Exact: \(T(0) = 48/49 = 0.97959\) before, \(50.4/51.4 = 0.98054\) after, a change of 0.0973 %.
About 0.1 %: a 50-fold reduction compared with open loop, where the same plant change gives a full 5 % error.

04Tracking and regulation

Tracking asks that the output follow the reference as closely as possible. In open loop you could in theory cancel a stable plant with the controller and substitute the transfer function you want, but that relies on a perfect linear model and fails amid real uncertainty and actuator limits.

Regulation asks that the error stay small at a constant set point despite disturbances. An open-loop controller has no influence on the response to \(W\) or \(V\) at all (look at FPE 4.5 with \(D_{cl} = 0\)), so it is useless for regulation.

Both objectives are therefore designed in closed loop, and both are measured by the steady-state error to standard inputs.

05System type and steady-state error

References and disturbances are modelled as polynomial inputs \(1/s^{k+1}\) (step, ramp, parabola). A system is classified by the highest degree \(k\) of polynomial it can follow with a constant, non-zero error: that \(k\) is its type. A Type 1 system follows a step with zero error and a ramp with a constant error. The error is found with the Final Value Theorem:

Steady-state error (FPE 4.28)\[ e_{ss} = \lim_{t\to\infty} e(t) = \lim_{s\to 0} sE(s) \]
\(e_{ss}\)
Steady-state error.
\(E(s)\)
Laplace transform of the error, \(R - Y\) for tracking.

Type for reference tracking

Set \(W = V = 0\) in Figure 1.1. With unity feedback, \(E = R - Y = \dfrac{1}{1 + D_{cl}G}R\), and with \(R = 1/s^{k+1}\):

Tracking error to a polynomial reference\[ e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to 0} \frac{1}{1 + D_{cl}G}\,\frac{1}{s^{k}} \]
\(k\)
Degree of the reference polynomial.
\(D_{cl}G\)
Loop transfer function.

If the loop transfer function has \(n\) poles at the origin (integrators), the type is \(n\). The limits are summarised by the error constants:

TypeStep (k = 0)Ramp (k = 1)Parabola (k = 2)
0\(1/(1+K_p)\)∞∞
10\(1/K_v\)∞
200\(1/K_a\)
Error constants (FPE Table 4.1)\[ K_p = \lim_{s\to 0} D_{cl}G, \qquad K_v = \lim_{s\to 0} sD_{cl}G, \qquad K_a = \lim_{s\to 0} s^2D_{cl}G \]
\(K_p\)
Position error constant (dimensionless).
\(K_v\)
Velocity error constant, in 1/s.
\(K_a\)
Acceleration error constant, in 1/s².
Worked example 5.1 Type and ramp error

\(G(s) = \dfrac{5}{s(s+4)}\) with \(D_{cl} = k_P = 8\) in unity feedback. Find the type and the errors to a unit step and a unit ramp.

1
The loop \(D_{cl}G = \dfrac{40}{s(s+4)}\) has one integrator: Type 1.
2
Step: \(e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to 0} \dfrac{s(s+4)}{s(s+4)+40} = 0\).
3
Ramp: \(K_v = \lim_{s\to 0} s\cdot\dfrac{40}{s(s+4)} = 10\) s⁻¹, so \(e_{ss} = 1/K_v = 0.1\).
Type 1; zero error to a step; error of 0.1 (in units of the reference per unit of ramp slope) to a unit ramp.

Type for disturbance rejection

Now set \(R = V = 0\). The error is \(E = 0 - Y = -\dfrac{G}{1+D_{cl}G}W\) with \(W = 1/s^{k+1}\). The type with respect to a disturbance counts only the integrators between the error and the point where the disturbance enters, that is, in the controller.

Error due to a disturbance\[ e_{ss} = -\lim_{s\to 0} s\,\frac{G}{1 + D_{cl}G}\,\frac{1}{s^{k+1}} \]
\(W\)
Disturbance at the plant input, \(1/s^{k+1}\).
\(G\)
Plant.
\(D_{cl}\)
Controller.
Worked example 5.2 Same loop, different type

For the loop of example 5.1, find the error to a unit-step disturbance at the plant input with (a) \(D_{cl} = k_P\), (b) \(D_{cl} = k_P + k_I/s\).

1
(a) \(e_{ss} = -\lim_{s\to 0} s\cdot\dfrac{5/(s(s+4))}{1 + 5k_P/(s(s+4))}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s} = -\lim_{s\to 0}\dfrac{5}{s(s+4) + 5k_P} = -\dfrac{1}{k_P}\).
2
(b) Replace \(k_P\) by \((k_Ps + k_I)/s\): \(e_{ss} = -\lim_{s\to 0}\dfrac{5s}{s^2(s+4) + 5(k_Ps + k_I)} = 0\).
With P control the loop is Type 1 to the reference but Type 0 to the disturbance (error \(-1/k_P\)), because the plant's integrator sits after the disturbance. Adding an integrator in the controller makes it Type 1 to the disturbance too.
Exam

Always derive the type from first principles with the FVT, as Tutorial 2 asks. A system that reaches zero error only because two gains happen to cancel (a 'calibrated' system, such as Tutorial 2 Figure 1b) loses its type as soon as a parameter drifts. A type that comes from an integrator in the loop is unaffected by parameter changes.

06PID control

PID control grew out of experience and trial and error. Engineers wanted faster responses, so they used proportional control; to remove steady-state error they added integral control; to improve the transient and stability they added derivative control. The three-term controller is still the most widely used in industry, and you will implement it on the PLC in week 3.

The examples in this section use the plant \(G(s) = \dfrac{10}{(s+1)(s+2)(s+5)}\) (DC gain 1) in unity feedback.

Proportional (P)

Proportional control\[ \frac{U(s)}{E(s)} = D_{cl}(s) = k_P \]
\(U(s)\)
Control signal.
\(E(s)\)
Error signal.
\(k_P\)
Proportional gain.

The control is proportional to the error, like an amplifier. Raising \(k_P\) makes the response faster and reduces the steady-state error, but never removes it, and increases overshoot and oscillation. With a Type 0 plant the loop stays Type 0: \(e_{ss} = 1/(1 + k_P G(0))\).

Figure 6.1: Higher gain raises the final value towards 1 and speeds up the response, at the cost of more oscillation. For this plant substituting \(s = j\omega\) into \(s^3 + 8s^2 + 17s + 10 + 10k_P = 0\) gives \(\omega^2 = 17\) from the imaginary part and \(8\omega^2 = 10 + 10k_P\) from the real part, so the poles reach the imaginary axis at \(k_P = 12.6\) and the loop is unstable above it.

Integral (I) and PI

Integral and PI control (FPE 4.63, 4.73)\[ D_{cl}(s) = \frac{k_I}{s}, \qquad D_{cl}(s) = k_P + \frac{k_I}{s} \]
\(k_I\)
Integral gain, in 1/s times the units of \(k_P\).
\(1/s\)
Integrator: the control is proportional to the time integral of the error.

The integrator keeps changing the control as long as any error remains, so it removes the steady-state error to a step for any \(k_I\); the loop becomes Type 1. Too much \(k_I\) increases overshoot and oscillation and can cause instability. Integral control alone is slow, so it is nearly always combined with proportional control (PI): zero steady-state error with a faster response.

Derivative (D) and PID

Derivative and PID control (FPE 4.71)\[ D_{cl}(s) = k_Ds, \qquad D_{cl}(s) = k_P + \frac{k_I}{s} + k_Ds = k_P\left(1 + \frac{1}{T_Is} + T_Ds\right) \]
\(k_D\)
Derivative gain, in s times the units of \(k_P\).
\(T_I\)
Integral (reset) time in s, \(T_I = k_P/k_I\).
\(T_D\)
Derivative time in s, \(T_D = k_D/k_P\).

The derivative term reacts to the rate of change of the error, so it anticipates where the error is going. It adds damping: it can improve stability, reduce overshoot and speed up the response. It is never used alone (it does nothing for a constant error).

Watch out

Two warnings about \(k_D\). It amplifies noise, because high-frequency noise has large derivatives. And it gives a sharp kick when the reference steps suddenly. The usual fix (FPE Figure 4.10a) is to put the D-term in the feedback path, acting on the measured output only, so a reference step does not pass through the derivative.

Figure 6.2: P: \(k_P = 3\). PI: \(k_P = 3,\ k_I = 2\). PID: \(k_P = 6,\ k_I = 4,\ k_D = 1.5\). PI removes the 25 % offset but overshoots more (34 %) and settles slowly (6.4 s, 2 %). Adding D lets the gains rise while cutting overshoot to 19 % and settling to 2.3 s.

IncreaseRise timeOvershootSteady-state errorStability
\(k_P\)ShorterLargerSmaller, not zeroWorse
\(k_I\)ShorterLargerRemovedWorse
\(k_D\)Little changeSmallerNo effectBetter (if noise allows)

07Ziegler-Nichols tuning

Tuning means choosing \(k_P\), \(T_I\) and \(T_D\). You can do it by trial and error, or systematically from a generic model of the response. Callender et al. (1936) proposed finding the gains from plant parameters measured in experiments; Ziegler and Nichols (1942, 1943) extended this into two methods. Both use the form \(D_c(s) = k_P(1 + 1/(T_Is) + T_Ds)\).

The methods rest on the observation that the step response of very many process plants has the same S-shape, the process reaction curve, which is well described by a first-order system with a time delay:

Process reaction curve model\[ \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{Ae^{-sL}}{\tau s + 1}, \qquad R = \frac{A}{\tau} \]
\(A\)
Final value (DC gain) of the open-loop step response.
\(L\)
Time delay (lag) in s.
\(\tau\)
Time constant in s (FPE 3.59).
\(R\)
Reaction rate: slope of the tangent at the steepest point, in units of y per s.

Figure 7.1: Draw the tangent through the steepest point of the curve. L is where the tangent crosses zero, and R is its slope. For a true first-order-plus-delay plant the tangent reaches the final value A at time L + τ.

Method 1: quarter decay ratio

Apply a unit step to the open-loop plant, read \(L\) and \(R\), and use Table 4.2. The gains are chosen so that the closed-loop transient decays to a quarter of its amplitude after one period of oscillation.

ControllerOptimum gain (FPE Table 4.2)
P\(k_P = 1/(RL)\)
PI\(k_P = 0.9/(RL)\), \(T_I = L/0.3\)
PID\(k_P = 1.2/(RL)\), \(T_I = 2L\), \(T_D = 0.5L\)

Method 2: ultimate sensitivity

Close the loop with proportional gain only. Increase \(k_P\) until a small impulse produces oscillations that neither grow nor decay (the limit of stability), without saturating the actuator. That gain is the ultimate gain \(K_u\), and the period of the oscillation is the ultimate period \(P_u\). Use Table 4.3.

ControllerOptimum gain (FPE Table 4.3, as on the slides)
P\(k_P = 0.5K_u\)
PI\(k_P = 0.45K_u\), \(T_I = P_u/1.2\)
PID\(k_P = 1.6K_u\), \(T_I = 0.5P_u\), \(T_D = 0.125P_u\)
Watch out

The slide and the textbook table give \(k_P = 1.6K_u\) for PID. Ziegler and Nichols' original rule is \(k_P = 0.6K_u\), and that is the value in most other references. For the plant in example 7.1, \(1.6K_u\) gives an unstable loop while \(0.6K_u\) gives a stable one. Use the slide value in the test if asked for Table 4.3, but be aware of the difference when you tune the real rig.

Quarter decayUnit stepOpen loopUnit-step responseProcess reaction curve constants R and L
Ultimate sensitivitySmall impulseClosed loop, P onlyNon-decaying oscillation of smallest amplitudeUltimate gain and period \(K_u, P_u\)
Worked example 7.1 Both Ziegler-Nichols methods

A process has \(\dfrac{Y}{U} = \dfrac{e^{-2s}}{10s + 1}\). Find PID gains with each method.

1
Reaction curve: \(A = 1\), \(L = 2\) s, \(\tau = 10\) s, so \(R = A/\tau = 0.1\) s⁻¹ and \(RL = 0.2\).
2
Table 4.2, PID: \(k_P = 1.2/0.2 = 6\), \(T_I = 2L = 4\) s, \(T_D = 0.5L = 1\) s. (P alone: \(k_P = 5\); PI: \(k_P = 4.5\), \(T_I = 6.67\) s.)
3
Ultimate point: the loop with gain \(K\) is at the stability limit where the phase of \(G(j\omega)\) is −180°: \(-2\omega - \tan^{-1}(10\omega) = -\pi\), giving \(\omega_u = 0.844\) rad/s. Then \(K_u = 1/|G(j\omega_u)| = \sqrt{1 + (10\omega_u)^2} = 8.50\) and \(P_u = 2\pi/\omega_u = 7.44\) s.
4
Table 4.3, PID: \(k_P = 1.6K_u = 13.6\), \(T_I = 0.5P_u = 3.72\) s, \(T_D = 0.125P_u = 0.930\) s.
Quarter decay: \(k_P = 6\), \(T_I = 4\) s, \(T_D = 1\) s. Ultimate sensitivity: \(K_u = 8.50\), \(P_u = 7.44\) s. Both are only starting points for iterative tuning on the real system (Figure 7.2).

Figure 7.2: Delay simulated with a sixth-order Padé approximation. Overshoot is P 47 %, PI 54 %, PID 67 %. These are typical Ziegler-Nichols results: fast but lightly damped, which is why the gains are then tuned by hand.

08Practice problems

Problem 1. \(G(s) = \dfrac{2}{s(s+3)}\), unity feedback, \(D_{cl} = k_P\). Find \(k_P\) so that the error to a unit ramp is 0.05.

Type 1. \(K_v = 2k_P/3\). \(1/K_v = 0.05 \Rightarrow K_v = 20 \Rightarrow k_P = 30\).

Problem 2. For \(G(s) = \dfrac{10}{(s+1)(s+10)}\) in unity feedback, which of \(D_1 = k_P\) and \(D_2 = (k_Ps + k_I)/s\) can give a ramp error below 0.1? (Tutorial 2 problem 2 with β = 1.)

\(e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to0} \dfrac{(s+1)(s+10)}{(s+1)(s+10) + 10D}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s}\). With \(D_1\) the loop is Type 0 and the ramp error is infinite. With \(D_2\): \(e_{ss} = 10/(10k_I) = 1/k_I\), so \(k_I \gt 10\). Stability: substitute \(s = j\omega\) into \(s^3 + 11s^2 + (10 + 10k_P)s + 10k_I = 0\). The imaginary part gives \(\omega^2 = 10 + 10k_P\) and the real part \(11\omega^2 = 10k_I\), so poles sit on the imaginary axis when \(10k_I = 11(10 + 10k_P)\). A small \(k_I\) is stable (the poles are then close to those with \(k_I = 0\)), so the loop is stable for \(0 \lt 10k_I \lt 11(10 + 10k_P)\), that is \(k_P \gt k_I/11 - 1\).

Problem 3. A plant's open-loop step response gives \(L = 0.5\) s and \(R = 0.8\) s⁻¹. Find the Ziegler-Nichols PI gains as \(k_P\) and \(k_I\).

\(RL = 0.4\). \(k_P = 0.9/0.4 = 2.25\), \(T_I = 0.5/0.3 = 1.67\) s, \(k_I = k_P/T_I = 1.35\) s⁻¹.

Problem 4. With P control alone the lab rig starts sustained oscillation at \(k_P = 4\) with a period of 0.8 s. Give the Table 4.3 PI and PID settings.

\(K_u = 4\), \(P_u = 0.8\) s. PI: \(k_P = 1.8\), \(T_I = 0.667\) s. PID (slide table): \(k_P = 6.4\), \(T_I = 0.4\) s, \(T_D = 0.1\) s. (Original rule: \(k_P = 2.4\).)

Problem 5. Open-loop control of a plant with DC gain 5 is calibrated so \(T(0) = 1\). The plant gain drops 20 %. Compare with a unity-feedback loop whose DC loop gain is 50.

Open loop: \(T(0)\) drops 20 %. Closed loop: \(T(0) = 50/51 = 0.9804\) before, \(40/41 = 0.9756\) after, a drop of 0.488 % (sensitivity estimate \(20/51 = 0.392\) %).
