Hardware, sample-rate selection and discrete design

Converters, aliasing and the anti-alias prefilter, the computer's delays and arithmetic, how to choose the sample rate, the ZOH equivalent for designing in the z-domain, and a review of the term. Built from the Week 12 folder: the lecture deck, the term summary, the 2020 Ch 8.4-8.5 slides and Tutorial 10.

01From ideal to real hardware

The Week 11 notes (the digital control page, built from FPE 8.1 to 8.3) gave the theory of a digital loop: the z-transform, the map \(z = e^{sT}\), the discrete equivalents (Tustin, zero-order hold, matched pole-zero) and the difference equations they produce, with the rule of thumb to sample at 20 to 25 times the bandwidth. All of that assumed ideal hardware. This week covers the real components (FPE 8.4), how to choose the sample rate properly (8.5), and a first look at designing directly in the z-domain (8.6). It closes the module, so the page ends with a review of the term.

The theme

The sample rate is a compromise. Three requirements push it up: tracking, disturbance rejection and the anti-alias prefilter. Three costs push it down: the price of a faster computer and converters, the lower resolution of fast converters, and arithmetic precision. The aim is the slowest rate that meets every specification.

Converters

The A/D converter turns a voltage into a number and the D/A converter turns a number back into a voltage. Two properties matter for control. Resolution is given in bits: an \(n\)-bit converter divides its full-scale range into \(2^n\) levels. Speed is the highest sample rate, which sets the shortest usable \(T\). The price rises with both, so specify no more bits and no faster a rate than the design needs. When several sensors share one A/D converter, taking turns, each channel gets only a fraction of its speed.

Converter resolution\[ \text{resolution} = \frac{1}{2^n}\ \text{of full scale}, \qquad 2^n \gt \frac{1}{\text{required fraction}} \]
\(n\)
Number of bits of the converter.
\(2^n\)
Number of levels across the full-scale range.

Worked example 2.1 How many bits?

A sensor's full range must be resolved to better than 0.05 % of full scale. How many bits does the A/D converter need?

1
\(0.05\,\% = 5 \times 10^{-4}\), so \(2^n \gt 1/(5\times10^{-4}) = 2000\).
2
\(2^{10} = 1024\) is too few; \(2^{11} = 2048\) is enough.
At least 11 bits. A 12-bit converter is the practical choice and costs little more.

03Aliasing

Aliasing is high-frequency content being turned, by the sampling itself, into a lower frequency where it cannot be told apart from a genuine signal. The textbook case is a 60 Hz oscillation sampled at 50 Hz: join the samples and you get a clean 10 Hz signal that was never there.

Definition

Sampling theorem (Nyquist, Shannon): a signal can be reconstructed from its samples only if it contains no component above \(\omega_s/2\). The highest frequency a sampled system can represent unambiguously is the Nyquist frequency \(\omega_s/2\). This is the periodicity of \(e^{j\omega T}\) in the \(z = e^{sT}\) map: frequencies above \(\omega_s/2\) land on top of lower ones.

Alias frequency\[ f_a = |f - Nf_s|, \qquad N = \text{nearest integer to } f/f_s \]
\(f\)
Frequency of the component being sampled (Hz).
\(f_s\)
Sample frequency (Hz), \(1/T\).
\(f_a\)
Apparent (alias) frequency after sampling, between 0 and \(f_s/2\).

Figure 3.1: The dots are identical for the true signal and the alias. Nothing done to the numbers afterwards can separate them.

This is worse in a digital loop than in an analogue one. In a continuous system, noise far above the control bandwidth has little effect because the system does not respond up there. In a digital system the same noise can be aliased down near the bandwidth, where the loop responds fully, so a poorly designed digital controller can be noisier than the analogue one it replaced. The worst case is \(f\) an exact multiple of \(f_s\): the noise aliases to zero frequency and appears as a constant offset that looks like a real error.

Worked example 3.1 Where noise lands

A loop samples at \(f_s = 200\) Hz. Where do components at 90, 230, 370 and 400 Hz appear?

1
Nyquist frequency: 100 Hz. 90 Hz is below it: no aliasing, it appears at 90 Hz.
2
230 Hz: \(230/200 = 1.15\), \(N = 1\), \(f_a = |230 - 200| = 30.0\) Hz.
3
370 Hz: \(370/200 = 1.85\), \(N = 2\), \(f_a = |370 - 400| = 30.0\) Hz.
4
400 Hz: \(N = 2\), \(f_a = 0\): a constant offset.
90, 30, 30 and 0 Hz. Two different noise sources land on the same 30 Hz, and the 400 Hz one becomes a bias the integrator will try to remove. Always check where a known interference frequency lands.

04The anti-alias prefilter

The remedy is an analogue low-pass filter between the sensor and the A/D converter. It must be analogue and come before the sampler, because once the samples are taken the alias is arithmetically identical to a real signal. A first-order filter often does:

First-order prefilter\[ H_p(s) = \frac{a}{s + a}, \qquad |H_p(j\omega)| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + \omega^2}}, \qquad \angle H_p(j\omega) = -\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{a} \]
\(a\)
Cut-off (break) frequency in rad/s: gain \(1/\sqrt2\) (−3 dB) and 45° of lag there.
\(\omega\)
Frequency in rad/s.
Conservative design
  1. Put the cut-off well above the bandwidth so its lag does not disturb the design already done: \(a = 10\,\omega_{BW}\) gives only 5.7° of lag at \(\omega_{BW}\).
  2. Choose \(\omega_s\) about 10 times the cut-off, so the filter has cut noise at \(\omega_s/2\) by a factor of at least 5.

Together: \(\omega_s \approx 100\,\omega_{BW}\), four times the week 11 rule of thumb. On this route the prefilter, not the control design, sets the sample rate.

The alternative is to allow more prefilter lag at \(\omega_{BW}\), include \(H_p(s)\) in the plant model and add lead in \(D_d(z)\) to make up for it. That permits a much lower sample rate, at the cost of a more complex compensator and an analogue and digital design that no longer separate. The prefilter also cuts broadband sensor noise in general, which matters because digital controllers are strongly affected by noise.

Worked example 4.1 Prefilter and sample rate together

A loop has \(\omega_{BW} = 8\) rad/s. The sensor carries a strong 150 Hz interference. Design the prefilter and sample period by the conservative rule.

1
\(a = 10 \times 8 = 80\) rad/s (12.7 Hz). Lag at \(\omega_{BW}\): \(\tan^{-1}(8/80) = 5.71^\circ\); gain there \(80/\sqrt{80^2 + 8^2} = 0.995\).
2
\(\omega_s \approx 10a = 800\) rad/s, so \(T = 2\pi/800 = 7.85\) ms. Round to \(T = 8\) ms: \(f_s = 125\) Hz, \(\omega_s = 785.4\) rad/s (\(98.2\,\omega_{BW}\)).
3
Nyquist: \(\omega_s/2 = 392.7\) rad/s (62.5 Hz). \(|H_p| = 80/\sqrt{80^2 + 392.7^2} = 0.200\), a factor of 5.01.
4
The 150 Hz noise: \(150/125 = 1.2\), \(N = 1\), alias at 25.0 Hz. At 150 Hz (942 rad/s) the prefilter gives \(|H_p| = 0.0846\) (-21.5 dB).
\(H_p = 80/(s + 80)\), \(T = 8\) ms. The noise still aliases to 25 Hz (157 rad/s, far above the 8 rad/s loop), but only after being cut by a factor of 11.8. Both defences are needed. Had \(T\) been 6.67 ms (150 Hz) it would have aliased to 0 Hz.

05The computer

Microprocessors, microcontrollers, PLCs and PCs all serve, and the elements are the same: a CPU that executes the difference equation, a clock that supplies an interrupt every \(T\) seconds to define the sample instant, memory for the program and past values \(u(k-1),\,e(k-1)\), and a power supply. Timing can be interrupt-driven, which fixes \(T\) precisely, or free-running, where the code loops and \(T\) is its execution time; the latter is only acceptable if no branch changes how much code runs.

  • Computation delay. The time between reading \(y(k)\) and writing \(u(k)\) is pure delay in the loop. If it is not small compared with \(T\), use the modified matched pole-zero equivalent (week 11), which gives the computer a full period to work.
  • Hold delay. The zero-order hold keeps each control value for a whole period, an average delay of \(T/2\).
  • Arithmetic precision. As \(T\) shrinks, the poles and zeros of \(D(z)\) crowd towards \(z = 1\) and the difference-equation coefficients nearly cancel. With fixed-point arithmetic, rounding them moves the compensator's poles and zeros and can cause offsets or limit cycles.
Worked example 5.1 Faster is not always more accurate

\(D(s) = 5\,\dfrac{s+2}{s+10}\) is converted with Tustin's method and stored with 8 fractional bits (steps of \(1/256\)). Compare \(T = 50\), 10 and 1 ms.

1
\(T = 50\) ms: \(D(z) = \dfrac{4.20z - 3.80}{z - 0.600}\). After rounding, the equivalent pole is at \(s = -9.951\) (designed −10) and the zero at \(s = -1.992\) (designed −2).
2
\(T = 10\) ms: coefficients \(4.810,\,-4.714\), pole \(-0.9048\). Rounded: pole \(s = -9.836\), zero \(s = -1.969\).
3
\(T = 1\) ms: coefficients \(4.980,\,-4.970\), pole \(-0.99005\): all close to ±5 and 1. Rounded: pole \(s = -11.79\), zero \(s = -2.356\).
At 1 ms the rounding moves the compensator's pole by 18 % and its zero by 18 %, while at 50 ms the errors are under 2 %. With floating-point arithmetic this hardly matters, which is why many designers simply sample at \(40\,\omega_{BW}\) or faster; with fixed point it is a real argument against sampling faster than needed (Tutorial 10 problem 4).

06Selecting the sample rate

The objective is the slowest sample rate that still meets every specification: a slower computer will do, more control tasks fit on the one you have, and the A/D converter need not be as fast. Three things set a lower bound:

  1. Tracking (bandwidth, rise and settling time). The sampling theorem gives the absolute floor, \(\omega_s \ge 2\,\omega_{BW}\), but that is nowhere near enough: 20 to 40 times \(\omega_{BW}\) keeps the delay between command and response small and smooths the steps from the hold.
  2. Regulation (rejecting random disturbances). The controller can only counter a disturbance it samples often enough to see. It would be foolish to sample fast enough to attenuate very high-frequency noise that a prefilter removes for nothing.
  3. The prefilter. On the conservative route this is usually the binding constraint, at about \(100\,\omega_{BW}\).
\(\omega_s\)Status
\(2\,\omega_{BW}\)Theoretical floor; unusable in practice
\(5\,\omega_{BW}\)Emulation (discrete equivalents) goes unstable
\(20\,\omega_{BW}\)Reasonable
\(25\,\omega_{BW}\)Emulation matches the continuous design
\(100\,\omega_{BW}\)Conservative prefilter design
A false constraint

With discrete equivalents the approximation itself degrades as the sample rate falls and can even destabilise the loop. That limit belongs to the emulation method, not to digital control: the fix is to sample faster or to design directly in the z-domain (section 7).

Worked example 6.1 Emulation at two sample periods

A PI controller \(D(s) = 8\,\dfrac{s+1}{s}\) controls \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{(s+1)(s+4)}\). The zero cancels the slow pole, leaving \(8/(s(s+4))\) and closed-loop poles \(-2 \pm 2j\) (\(\zeta = 0.707\), \(\omega_n = 2.83\) rad/s). Implement it with Tustin's method at \(T = 0.1\) s and \(T = 0.3\) s.

1
\(\omega_{BW} = 2.83\) rad/s. \(T = 0.1\) s gives \(\omega_s = 62.8\) rad/s \(= 22.2\,\omega_{BW}\); \(T = 0.3\) s gives \(20.9\) rad/s \(= 7.40\,\omega_{BW}\).
2
Tustin at \(T = 0.1\) (\(2/T = 20\)): \(D(z) = 8\,\dfrac{21z - 19}{20(z - 1)} = 8.40\,\dfrac{z - 0.9048}{z - 1}\), so \(u(k) = u(k-1) + 8.40e(k) - 7.60e(k-1)\).
3
Simulate the sampled loop with the real hold (Figure 6.1).
Overshoot: continuous 4.3 %, \(T = 0.1\) s 7.6 %, \(T = 0.3\) s 17 %. Emulation ignores the hold's \(T/2\) delay, which costs phase margin; even at 22 times the bandwidth it nearly doubles the overshoot, and the cost shrinks only as the sample rate rises further.

07Designing directly in the z-domain

Emulation designs a continuous controller and then approximates it, so its accuracy always depends on sampling fast. The alternative is to model the sampled plant and design in the z-domain from the outset. The plant together with its zero-order hold, exact at the sample instants, is the ZOH equivalent:

ZOH equivalent of the plant\[ G_{ho}G(z) = (1 - z^{-1})\,\mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{G(s)}{s}\right\} \]
\(G(s)\)
Continuous plant.
\(G(s)/s\)
Plant step response in the s-domain; take its z-transform from the table.
\(1 - z^{-1}\)
Accounts for the hold: a step on, then a step off one period later.
Worked example 7.1 ZOH equivalent of a plant

Find \(G_{ho}G(z)\) for \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{(s+1)(s+4)}\) with \(T = 0.1\) s.

1
Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{G(s)}{s} = \dfrac{1}{s(s+1)(s+4)} = \dfrac{1/4}{s} - \dfrac{1/3}{s+1} + \dfrac{1/12}{s+4}\).
2
Table: \(\dfrac{1}{s} \to \dfrac{z}{z-1}\), \(\dfrac{1}{s+a} \to \dfrac{z}{z - e^{-aT}}\). Multiply by \((1 - z^{-1}) = (z-1)/z\): \(G_{ho}G(z) = \dfrac14 - \dfrac13\,\dfrac{z-1}{z - e^{-T}} + \dfrac{1}{12}\,\dfrac{z-1}{z - e^{-4T}}\).
3
\(e^{-0.1} = 0.9048\), \(e^{-0.4} = 0.6703\). Combining over the common denominator:
\(G_{ho}G(z) = \dfrac{0.004248\,(z + 0.8466)}{(z - 0.9048)(z - 0.6703)}\). The poles are exactly \(e^{p_iT}\); the zero near −0.85 comes from the hold, not the plant. (Checked against MATLAB-equivalent c2d(G,T,'zoh').)

The design tools then carry over: root locus is drawn in the z-plane with the same rules, but the target region is inside the unit circle and the constant-\(\zeta\) and constant-\(\omega_n\) loci are the spirals of \(z = e^{sT}\). Because there is no approximation to break down, direct design permits sample rates far below what emulation allows. In MATLAB: Gz = c2d(Gs,T,'zoh'), then rlocus(Gz) and zgrid.

Exam

Direct digital design (root locus in z) is not examinable. The term summary marks the ZOH equivalent model as examinable.

08Term 4 in review

The term summary works one plant through the whole term. Here is the same chain on a different plant, \(\ddot y + 4\dot y + 3y = u\), with only \(y\) measured: \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B} = [0\;\;1]^T\), \(\mathbf{C} = [1\;\;0]\), \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{(s+1)(s+3)}\).

Worked example 8.1 The whole term on one plant

Design for \(\zeta = 0.707\) and a 1 % settling time near 1.5 s, zero steady-state error, then choose a sample period.

1
Poles. \(\sigma = 4.6/1.5 \approx 3\) and \(\zeta = 0.707\): \(-3 \pm 3j\), \(\alpha_c = s^2 + 6s + 18\). \(\det\mathcal{C} = -1\), \(\det\mathcal{O} = 1\): both hold.
2
Control law (week 8). Companion form: \(\det[s\mathbf{I} - (\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{K})] = s^2 + (4 + K_2)s + (3 + K_1)\), so \(\mathbf{K} = [15\;\;2]\).
3
Estimator (week 9), poles 4 times faster at −12 (twice): \(s^2 + (4 + l_1)s + (3 + 4l_1 + l_2) = s^2 + 24s + 144\), so \(\mathbf{L} = [20\;\;61]^T\).
4
Reference gain (week 9): \(\bar N = \alpha_c(0)/b(0) = 18/1 = 18\).
5
Integral control (week 10), adding a pole at −6: \((s+6)(s^2 + 6s + 18) = s^3 + 12s^2 + 54s + 108\). Augmented companion form gives \(K_I = 108\), \(\mathbf{K}_0 = [51\;\;8]\).
6
Sample rate (this week). \(\omega_{BW} \approx \omega_n = 4.24\) rad/s (computed: 4.24 rad/s). Tracking rule \(25\,\omega_{BW}\): \(\omega_s \approx 106\) rad/s, \(T \approx 0.059\) s; the conservative prefilter rule \(100\,\omega_{BW}\) gives \(T \approx 0.015\) s.
\(\mathbf{K} = [15\;\;2]\), \(\mathbf{L} = [20\;\;61]^T\), \(\bar N = 18\) or \(K_I = 108\), \(\mathbf{K}_0 = [51\;\;8]\); sample at about \(T = 50\) ms for tracking, or about 15 ms if the prefilter is designed conservatively.

09Practice problems

Problem 1. Tutorial 10 problem 1: a loop samples at 250 Hz. Where do components at 180, 300, 320, 250 and 500 Hz appear?

Nyquist 125 Hz. 180 → 70 Hz, 300 → 50 Hz, 320 → 70 Hz, 250 → 0 Hz, 500 → 0 Hz. The last two become constant offsets.

Problem 2. Tutorial 10 problems 2 and 3: \(\omega_{BW} = 15\) rad/s. Give the conservative prefilter, its lag at \(\omega_{BW}\), its attenuation at 300 Hz, and the sample period.

\(a = 150\) rad/s (23.9 Hz); lag \(\tan^{-1}0.1 = 5.71^\circ\). At 1885 rad/s: \(0.0793\) (−22.0 dB). \(\omega_s \approx 1500\) rad/s gives \(T = 4.19\) ms, rounded to 4 ms (250 Hz, Nyquist 785 rad/s, prefilter attenuation there 0.188, a factor of 5.3).

Problem 3. Tutorial 10 problem 4: discretise \(D(s) = \dfrac{12(s+3)}{s+15}\) with Tustin at \(T = 4\) ms.

\(2/T = 500\): zero at 0.9881, pole at 0.9417, gain 11.7204. \(u(k) = 0.9417u(k-1) + 11.7204e(k) - 11.5806e(k-1)\).

Problem 4. Inverted pendulum (term summary): \(\ddot\theta + 3\dot\theta - 2\theta = u\) about upright. Is it stable? Find \(\mathbf{K}\) for poles \(-2 \pm 2j\).

Poles \((-3 \pm \sqrt{17})/2 = 0.562,\,-3.562\): unstable. \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det\mathcal{C} = -1\). \(s^2 + (3 + K_2)s + (K_1 - 2) = s^2 + 4s + 8\): \(\mathbf{K} = [10\;\;1]\).

Problem 5. Why can an alias not be removed by a digital filter after sampling?

After sampling, the aliased component has exactly the same sample values as a genuine low-frequency signal, so no operation on the numbers can tell them apart. Only an analogue filter ahead of the sampler can remove it.
