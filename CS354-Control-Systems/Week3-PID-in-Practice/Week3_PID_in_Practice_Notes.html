Putting PID control on the lab rig

The angular position rig and its motor-gearbox model, turning a continuous controller into a difference equation with Tustin's method, and measuring a Bode plot. Built from Lectures 7 and 8 (no tutorial this week).

01Laboratory practical 1: the PID design project

Week 3 moves from theory to the bench. Laboratory practical 1 asks you to design a PID controller for the angular position system and to measure its frequency response. Part 1 of the brief is on STEMLearn.

The workstation has a bench power supply, an oscilloscope, a pressurised-air outlet, a Siemens PLC with an interface unit, and the angular position system inside a disc enclosure. The test rig is a DC motor assembly driving a disc (the load) through a gearbox, with a signal conditioning box.

The application is a water cannon whose nozzle angle \(\theta\) is controlled. The design project is open-ended: you set your own requirements from the operating context, and there are no 'right' answers, only practical results judged with engineering sense.

Before the lab

Preparation before the session:

  • Read the safety appendix (Appendix B of the brief).
  • Define your design requirements. What specifications suit a water cannon nozzle? Which inputs (step, ramp) must it follow accurately?
  • Use system type (week 2) to reason about which gains are needed in theory.
  • Derive the difference equations that implement the controller on the PLC (section 4).

02Modelling the motor and gearbox

FPE Chapter 2.3 models a DC motor and gears. On the rig the motor drives gear 1 (radius \(R_{G1}\)), which meshes with gear 2 (radius \(R_{G2}\)) on the disc shaft. Each gear has its own torque balance, with \(F_t\) the tangential contact force between the teeth:

Gear torque balances\[ J_{G1}\ddot\theta_m + B_{G1}\dot\theta_m = T_m - R_{G1}F_t, \qquad J_{G2}\ddot\theta_L + B_{G2}\dot\theta_L = R_{G2}F_t - T_L \]
\(J_{G1},\,J_{G2}\)
Moments of inertia of the gears (kg m²).
\(B_{G1},\,B_{G2}\)
Viscous friction coefficients (N m s).
\(\theta_m,\,\theta_L\)
Motor and load angles (rad); \(\theta_L = \theta_m/GR\).
\(T_m\)
Motor torque (N m).
\(T_L\)
Load torque (N m).
\(R_{G1},\,R_{G2}\)
Gear radii (m).
\(F_t\)
Tangential force between the gear teeth (N).

Eliminating \(F_t\), adding the motor's electrical equation and referring everything to the motor shaft gives one equivalent inertia and damping. The load inertia is divided by the square of the gear ratio, and the motor's back-emf adds electrical damping:

Position transfer function (Lecture 7)\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\Theta_m(s)}{V_a(s)} = \left(\frac{1}{R_a\alpha_r}\right)\frac{1}{J_es^2 + B_es}, \\ J_e = J_m + J_G + \frac{J_L}{GR^2}, \qquad B_e = B_m + B_G + \frac{1}{R_a\alpha_r^2} \end{gathered} \]
\(\Theta_m\)
Motor shaft angle (rad).
\(V_a\)
Armature voltage (V).
\(R_a\)
Armature resistance (Ω).
\(\alpha_r\)
Motor constant as written on the slides; \(1/\alpha_r\) is the torque constant \(K_t\) (N m/A), equal to the back-emf constant \(K_e\) (V s/rad).
\(J_e,\,B_e\)
Equivalent inertia (kg m²) and damping (N m s) at the motor shaft.
\(J_m,\,J_G,\,J_L\)
Motor, gearbox and load inertias.
\(B_m,\,B_G\)
Motor and gearbox friction.
\(GR\)
Gear ratio, motor speed over load speed.

The speed transfer function follows by dividing by \(s\) (one integration less): \(\dfrac{\Omega_m}{V_a} = \dfrac{1}{R_a\alpha_r}\cdot\dfrac{1}{J_es + B_e}\). Speed is a first-order system; position adds an integrator, so the position plant is already Type 1.

Notation

FPE writes the motor with \(K_t\) and \(K_e\). The slides use \(\alpha_r\) such that \(K_t = K_e = 1/\alpha_r\); then \(K_tK_e/R_a = 1/(R_a\alpha_r^2)\), the electrical damping term in \(B_e\). Check your brief for the rig's parameter names.

Worked example 2.1 Equivalent inertia and time constant

A motor has \(J_m = 2\times10^{-6}\) kg m², gearbox \(J_G = 1\times10^{-6}\) kg m², disc \(J_L = 4\times10^{-4}\) kg m², \(GR = 20\), \(B_m + B_G = 1\times10^{-6}\) N m s, \(R_a = 4\) Ω and \(\alpha_r = 50\). Find \(J_e\), \(B_e\), the speed time constant and the DC speed gain.

1
\(J_L/GR^2 = 4\times10^{-4}/400 = 1\times10^{-6}\), so \(J_e = 0.00000400\) kg m².
2
Electrical damping \(1/(R_a\alpha_r^2) = 1/(4 \times 2500) = 1\times10^{-4}\); \(B_e = 0.000101\) N m s. The back-emf dominates the friction.
3
\(\Omega_m/V_a = \dfrac{1/(R_a\alpha_r)}{J_es + B_e}\): time constant \(\tau = J_e/B_e = 0.0396\) s; DC gain \(\dfrac{1/(R_a\alpha_r)}{B_e} = 49.5\) rad/s per volt at the motor.
\(\tau \approx 0.0396\) s and 49.5 rad/s per V at the motor, or 2.48 rad/s per V at the disc after the 20:1 gearbox. Even a disc 200 times heavier than the motor only doubles \(J_e\), because of the \(1/GR^2\) factor.

03From analogue to digital controllers

So far signals have lived in the time domain \(t\) and the continuous \(s\) domain. That suits analogue controllers built from resistors, capacitors and op-amps: they handle continuous signals with all values over time, but are hard to design and change and are susceptible to noise.

The lab uses a digital controller: a PLC (programmable logic controller) with a processor and memory that runs the program you write. It reads the inputs and writes the outputs only at sample instants \(t = kT\), so the controller must be written as a difference equation in \(k\), not a differential equation in \(t\). The route mirrors the inverse Laplace transform:

Analogue (continuous)Digital (discrete)
Time domain \(f(t)\)Discrete-time domain \(f(k) = f(kT)\)
Laplace transform \(F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}dt\)z-transform \(F(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty f(k)z^{-k}\)
Derivative: \(\mathcal{L}\{\dot f\} = sF(s)\)Delay: \(\mathcal{Z}\{f(k-1)\} = z^{-1}F(z)\)
Differential equationDifference equation
z-transform and shift property\[ \mathcal{Z}\{f(k)\} = F(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} f(k)z^{-k}, \qquad \mathcal{Z}\{f(k-1)\} = z^{-1}F(z) \]
\(f(k)\)
Value of the sampled signal at sample \(k\), that is at time \(kT\).
\(F(z)\)
Its z-transform.
\(z^{-1}\)
A delay of one sample period \(T\).

The plan is: start with the designed controller \(D(s)\), convert it to an approximate \(D(z)\), then read off the difference equation using the shift property. Example: sampling \(y(t) = \sin t\) gives the sequence \(y(k) = \sin(kT)\); the PLC only ever sees these values.

04Tustin's method and difference equations

Tustin's method (the bilinear transform) replaces every \(s\) in \(D(s)\) with a function of \(z\). It comes from approximating integration with the trapezoidal rule:

Tustin's method (on the formula sheet)\[ s \;\rightarrow\; \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{z-1}{z+1} \]
\(T\)
Sample period in s.
\(z\)
z-transform variable; \(z^{-1}\) is a one-sample delay.

The recipe has three steps: substitute, rearrange into powers of \(z^{-1}\), and replace each \(z^{-n}X(z)\) by \(x(k-n)\).

Worked example 4.1 Lead compensator to difference equation

Implement \(D(s) = \dfrac{U(s)}{E(s)} = 5\,\dfrac{0.2s + 1}{0.05s + 1}\) with a sample period \(T = 0.01\) s.

1
\(2/T = 200\). Substitute: \(D(z) = 5\,\dfrac{0.2\cdot 200\frac{z-1}{z+1} + 1}{0.05\cdot 200\frac{z-1}{z+1} + 1} = 5\,\dfrac{40(z-1) + (z+1)}{10(z-1) + (z+1)} = \dfrac{205z - 195}{11z - 9}\).
2
Divide top and bottom by \(z\): \(\dfrac{U(z)}{E(z)} = \dfrac{205 - 195z^{-1}}{11 - 9z^{-1}}\), so \(11U(z) - 9z^{-1}U(z) = 205E(z) - 195z^{-1}E(z)\).
3
Apply the shift property.
\(11u(k) = 9u(k-1) + 205e(k) - 195e(k-1)\), or \(u(k) = 0.8182u(k-1) + 18.64e(k) - 17.73e(k-1)\). Each sample the PLC needs only the new error and the previous error and output. Figure 4.1 checks it against the continuous response.

Figure 4.1: The difference equation (amber dots, held between samples) follows the continuous curve closely because the sample period, 0.01 s, is short compared with the compensator's time constants of 0.05 s and 0.2 s.

The Lecture 8 example

The slide converts \(D(s) = 10\,\dfrac{0.5s+1}{0.1s+1}\) to \(9u(k) = 7u(k-1) + 410e(k) - 390e(k-1)\). Working backwards, those coefficients correspond to \(2/T = 80\), that is a sample period of \(T = 0.025\) s, which the slide does not state.

A PID difference equation

For the lab the controller is \(D(s) = k_P + k_I/s + k_Ds\). Applying Tustin to each term and putting them over the common denominator \(z^2 - 1\) gives a second-order difference equation:

Tustin PID\[ \begin{gathered} u(k) = u(k-2) + a_0e(k) + a_1e(k-1) + a_2e(k-2), \\ a_0 = k_P + \frac{k_IT}{2} + \frac{2k_D}{T},\; a_1 = k_IT - \frac{4k_D}{T},\; a_2 = -k_P + \frac{k_IT}{2} + \frac{2k_D}{T} \end{gathered} \]
\(u(k)\)
Control output at sample \(k\).
\(e(k)\)
Error at sample \(k\).
\(k_P,\,k_I,\,k_D\)
PID gains.
\(T\)
Sample period (s).
\(a_0,\,a_1,\,a_2\)
Difference-equation coefficients.
Watch out

Tustin applied to the pure derivative \(k_Ds\) puts a pole at \(z = -1\), which can make the D-term chatter from sample to sample. Practical PLC code (including many templates) therefore implements each term separately: P directly, I by a running sum (trapezoidal or rectangular), and D by a backward difference, \(k_D[e(k) - e(k-1)]/T\), often with a filter. Use the structure in your TIA Portal template's Controller block and make sure the sample time \(T\) in your equations matches the cycle time of the PLC.

05Bode plots from measurements

The practical asks for a Bode plot of the closed loop. A Bode plot shows the frequency response: how a linear system responds to sinusoidal inputs over a range of frequencies. The top graph is the magnitude, the ratio of output amplitude to input amplitude (amplification or attenuation), usually in dB. The bottom graph is the phase, how far the output is shifted behind (lag) or ahead of (lead) the input. The link to the transfer function is \(s = j\omega\).

For a stable linear system, a sinusoidal input produces, once the transient has died out, a sinusoid of the same frequency:

Sinusoidal steady state\[ \begin{gathered} u(t) = A\sin\omega_0t \;\Rightarrow\; y_{ss}(t) = AM\sin(\omega_0t + \phi), \\ M = |T(j\omega_0)|, \;\; \phi = \angle T(j\omega_0) = -\omega_0\,\delta t \end{gathered} \]
\(A\)
Input amplitude.
\(\omega_0\)
Input frequency (rad/s), \(\omega_0 = 2\pi f\).
\(M\)
Magnitude ratio, output amplitude over input amplitude.
\(\phi\)
Phase shift (rad or degrees); negative means the output lags.
\(\delta t\)
Time by which the output peaks lag the input peaks (s).

Figure 5.1: At \(\omega_0 = 4\) rad/s, \(M = 0.975\) and \(\phi = -69.4^\circ\), so the output peaks lag the input peaks by \(\delta t = 0.303\) s.

Worked example 5.1 One Bode point from a scope trace

The rig is driven with a 2 Hz sine of amplitude 25°. The measured angle settles to an amplitude of 18°, and its peaks occur 0.05 s after the reference peaks. Find the magnitude (ratio and dB) and phase.

1
\(M = 18/25 = 0.72\); in decibels \(20\log_{10}(0.72) = -2.85\) dB.
2
\(\omega_0 = 2\pi(2) = 12.57\) rad/s. \(\phi = -\omega_0\delta t = -12.57 \times 0.05 = -0.628\) rad, or \(-360^\circ \times f\,\delta t = -36^\circ\).
One point on the Bode plot: -2.85 dB and -36° at 2 Hz (12.57 rad/s). Repeat over a range of frequencies (the lab gives sine inputs from about 0.1 Hz upwards) to build the whole plot.

Figure 5.2: The amber dot on the phase plot is the 4 rad/s point of Figure 5.1. The bandwidth, where the magnitude falls 3 dB below its low-frequency value, is the approximate figure the practical asks you to estimate. Week 4 explains what it tells you.

Watch out

Units: magnitude in dB is \(20\log_{10}M\) (an amplitude ratio). \(-3\) dB corresponds to \(M = 0.707\). Frequency in rad/s is \(2\pi\) times frequency in Hz; mixing them up shifts the whole plot sideways.

06Practical procedure and deliverables

  1. Initial gains. Use the data provided (an open-loop step response) and the Ziegler-Nichols quarter-decay method from week 2 to get starting values of \(k_P\), \(T_I\) and \(T_D\).
  2. Iterative tuning. Implement the controller in the Controller program block in TIA Portal (version 18). Apply steps, observe the traces, and adjust the gains until your requirements are met.
  3. Frequency response. Apply sinusoidal references at several frequencies, record amplitude and phase, and draw the closed-loop Bode plot. Estimate the bandwidth.

07Practice problems

Problem 1. Convert \(D(s) = \dfrac{U}{E} = \dfrac{4}{0.1s + 1}\) to a difference equation with Tustin's method and \(T = 0.02\) s.

\(2/T = 100\): \(D(z) = \dfrac{4(z+1)}{10(z-1) + (z+1)} = \dfrac{4z + 4}{11z - 9}\). So \(11u(k) = 9u(k-1) + 4e(k) + 4e(k-1)\). DC check: \(z = 1\) gives \(8/2 = 4\) ✓.

Problem 2. Find the Tustin PID coefficients for \(k_P = 2\), \(k_I = 5\) s⁻¹, \(k_D = 0.1\) s and \(T = 0.01\) s.

\(a_0 = 2 + 0.025 + 20 = 22.025\), \(a_1 = 0.05 - 40 = -39.95\), \(a_2 = -2 + 0.025 + 20 = 18.025\). \(u(k) = u(k-2) + 22.025e(k) - 39.95e(k-1) + 18.025e(k-2)\). (Checked numerically against the bilinear transform.)

Problem 3. A 0.5 Hz test gives input amplitude 40°, output amplitude 41°, output peaks 0.15 s behind. Give magnitude in dB and phase.

\(M = 1.025\), 0.21 dB. \(\phi = -360 \times 0.5 \times 0.15 = -27^\circ\).

Problem 4. Why does a heavy disc behind a 20:1 gearbox barely change the motor's time constant?

Inertia referred to the motor shaft is \(J_L/GR^2\), so the gearbox divides the disc's inertia by 400.
