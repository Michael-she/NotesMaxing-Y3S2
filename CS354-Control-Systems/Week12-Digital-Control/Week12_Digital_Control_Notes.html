Digital Control: from D(s) to a difference equation

Study notes built from the three Week 12 slide sets (Ch 8.1 to 8.2, Ch 8.3, Ch 8.4 to 8.5). Every number, figure and worked example below was computed and checked numerically. The worked examples are new problems, not copies of the slides.

01Why go digital

Everything up to now designed a controller \(D_c(s)\) in continuous time. You could build it with op-amps, but in practice it runs on a microcontroller, PLC or PC. Chapter 8 is about getting from \(D_c(s)\) to code that a processor runs once every sample period \(T\).

What you gainThe controller is software, so it is reprogrammable and cheap to change. The same hardware can run many different controllers, logging, comms and safety logic.
What it costsSampling and holding introduce delay (about \(T/2\)), which eats phase margin. The ADC and DAC quantise signals to a finite number of bits. High-frequency noise can alias down into the control band.

The course takes the emulation route (slide 30), which has three steps:

  1. Design \(D_c(s)\) in continuous time with root locus, frequency response or state space, as before.
  2. Digitise: find a discrete \(D_d(z)\) (and so a difference equation) that approximates \(D_c(s)\) together with the sampling and ZOH behaviour.
  3. Evaluate: analyse, simulate and test the sampled-data loop to check the approximation did not break the design.

Direct design in the z-domain (section 8.6) is shown briefly in the slides but is not examined in this module.

02The sampled loop and the zero-order hold

The zero-order hold (ZOH)

A ZOH takes each output sample \(u(kT)\) and holds it constant until the next sample arrives:

\[ u(t) = u(kT), \qquad kT \le t \lt (k+1)T \]
\(u(t)\)
Control signal in continuous time: what the hold (DAC) actually outputs.
\(u(kT)\)
Control value the processor computed at sample \(k\).
\(k\)
Sample index, an integer.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(kT \le t \lt (k+1)T\)
The interval between sample \(k\) and the next one, over which the output is held constant.

The result is a staircase. If you draw a smooth line through the middle of each step, it is the intended signal shifted right by half a sample. That is the single most useful fact about a ZOH:

ZOH as a delay\[ G_{\text{ZOH}}(s) = \frac{1 - e^{-sT}}{s} \;\approx\; T\,e^{-sT/2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{extra phase lag} = \frac{\omega T}{2}\ \text{rad} \]
\(G_{\text{ZOH}}(s)\)
Transfer function of the zero-order hold.
\(s\)
Laplace variable.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(e^{-sT}\)
A delay of one full sample period.
\(T\,e^{-sT/2}\)
The approximation: a gain of \(T\) and a delay of half a sample.
\(\omega\)
Frequency at which the lag is evaluated, in rad/s.
\(\omega T/2\)
Phase lag in radians that the half-sample delay adds at \(\omega\).

The factor \(T\) in front of \(e^{-sT/2}\) is cancelled by the \(1/T\) gain of the ideal sampler, so in loop calculations you only keep the delay. Section 6 turns this into a phase-margin rule.

03The z-transform

In continuous time \(s\) describes frequency and the Laplace transform turns differential equations into algebra. In discrete time the sequence \(f(k) = f(kT)\) is described by \(z\), and the z-transform turns difference equations into algebra.

Definition (one-sided)\[ \mathcal{Z}\{f(k)\} = F(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} f(k)\,z^{-k} = f(0) + f(1)z^{-1} + f(2)z^{-2} + \cdots \]
\(\mathcal{Z}\{\cdot\}\)
The z-transform operator.
\(f(k)\)
Value of the sequence at sample \(k\).
\(F(z)\)
The z-transform of \(f(k)\).
\(z\)
Complex variable of the z-domain.
\(z^{-k}\)
A delay of \(k\) samples; it tags the value that occurs at sample \(k\).
\(k\)
Sample index, summed from 0 upwards (one-sided).

Read the definition literally: the coefficient of \(z^{-k}\) is the value of the signal at sample \(k\). So \(z^{-1}\) marks "one sample later". This gives the most used property:

Shift (delay) property\[ \mathcal{Z}\{f(k-1)\} = z^{-1}F(z) \qquad\text{(for } f(k)=0,\ k\lt 0) \]
\(f(k-1)\)
The sequence delayed by one sample.
\(z^{-1}\)
One-sample delay operator.
\(F(z)\)
z-transform of the undelayed sequence.
\(f(k)=0,\ k\lt 0\)
The sequence starts at \(k=0\), so no initial-condition term appears.

\(z^{-1}\) is a one-sample delay, the discrete counterpart of \(e^{-sT}\).

The transforms you need (from Table 8.1)

\(F(s)\)\(f(kT)\)\(F(z)\)

nonepulse at \(k = k_0\)\(z^{-k_0}\)
\(\dfrac{1}{s}\)unit step \(1(kT)\)\(\dfrac{z}{z-1}\)
\(\dfrac{1}{s^2}\)ramp \(kT\)\(\dfrac{Tz}{(z-1)^2}\)

The last row is the one added in red on slide 12 and it does almost all the work. The step is \(b = 1\), the sampled exponential is \(b = e^{-aT}\).

Worked example 3.1 z-transform from the definition

Find \(F(z)\) for \(f(k) = 0.6^k\), then for the sampled exponential \(f(t) = e^{-3t}\) with \(T = 0.1\) s.

1
Substitute into the definition: \(F(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty 0.6^k z^{-k} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (0.6\,z^{-1})^k\).
2
This is a geometric series with ratio \(0.6z^{-1}\), which converges for \(|z| \gt 0.6\): \[ F(z) = \frac{1}{1-0.6z^{-1}} = \frac{z}{z-0.6}. \]
3
For the exponential: \(f(kT) = e^{-3(0.1)k} = (e^{-0.3})^k = 0.7408^k\). So it is the same form with \(b = 0.7408\): \[ F(z) = \frac{z}{z-0.7408}. \]
Note the pole: the continuous pole at \(s=-3\) became a z-pole at \(e^{-3T} = 0.7408\). This is the \(z = e^{sT}\) rule of section 4 appearing on its own.

Inverse z-transform

Two methods are useful:

  • Partial fractions (closed form). Because every table entry has a \(z\) in the numerator, expand \(F(z)/z\) instead of \(F(z)\), then multiply \(z\) back in (the hint on slide 13).
  • Long division (numerical check). Divide numerator by denominator in powers of \(z^{-1}\); the coefficients are the samples \(f(0), f(1), f(2), \ldots\)
Worked example 3.2 inverse by partial fractions, checked by long division

Find \(y(k)\) for \( Y(z) = \dfrac{2z}{(z-1)(z-0.6)} \).

1
Divide by \(z\) first: \[ \frac{Y(z)}{z} = \frac{2}{(z-1)(z-0.6)} = \frac{A}{z-1} + \frac{B}{z-0.6} \]
2
Cover-up: \(A = \dfrac{2}{1-0.6} = 5\), \(\;B = \dfrac{2}{0.6-1} = -5\).
3
Multiply \(z\) back in and read off the table (\(z/(z-b) \leftrightarrow b^k\)): \[ Y(z) = \frac{5z}{z-1} - \frac{5z}{z-0.6} \quad\Rightarrow\quad y(k) = 5 - 5(0.6)^k. \]
4
Check by long division. \(Y(z) = \dfrac{2z}{z^2 - 1.6z + 0.6} = 2z^{-1} + 3.2z^{-2} + 3.92z^{-3} + \cdots\) Formula: \(y(1) = 5-3 = 2\), \(y(2) = 5 - 1.8 = 3.2\), \(y(3) = 5 - 1.08 = 3.92\). They agree.
\(y(k) = 5\,[1 - 0.6^k]\), which rises from 0 towards 5.

Difference equations and discrete transfer functions

A difference equation is what the processor actually runs. Taking z-transforms with the shift property turns it into a transfer function, and back again.

Worked example 3.3 difference equation to transfer function and step response

A sampled system obeys \( y(k) = 0.8\,y(k-1) + 0.4\,u(k-1) \). Find \(H(z)=Y(z)/U(z)\) and the response to a unit step \(u(k)=1,\ k\ge 0\).

1
Transform each term: \(Y = 0.8z^{-1}Y + 0.4z^{-1}U\), so \[ H(z) = \frac{0.4z^{-1}}{1-0.8z^{-1}} = \frac{0.4}{z-0.8}. \]
2
Run the recursion directly (this is exactly what a microcontroller would do):

04Mapping between the s-plane and the z-plane

Compare the transforms of \(f(t) = e^{-at}\) (slides 14 to 15):

\[ F(s) = \frac{1}{s+a}\ \ (\text{pole at } s = -a) \qquad F(z) = \frac{z}{z-e^{-aT}}\ \ (\text{pole at } z = e^{-aT}) \]
\(F(s)\)
Laplace transform of the continuous signal \(e^{-at}\).
\(a\)
Decay rate in 1/s; the s-plane pole sits at \(s=-a\).
\(F(z)\)
z-transform of the same signal sampled every \(T\) seconds.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(e^{-aT}\)
The z-plane pole: the factor by which the signal shrinks each sample.

The pole moves from \(s\) to \(e^{sT}\). This holds for every pole, real or complex:

The mapping\[ z = e^{sT} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad s = \frac{1}{T}\ln z \]
\(z\)
A point in the z-plane (a discrete pole or zero).
\(s\)
The matching point in the s-plane.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(\ln z\)
Natural logarithm of the complex number \(z\).

For a complex pole \(s = -\sigma \pm j\omega_d\) (where \(\sigma = \zeta\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\)):

\[ z = e^{-\sigma T}\,e^{\pm j\omega_d T} \quad\Rightarrow\quad |z| = e^{-\sigma T} = e^{-\zeta\omega_n T}, \qquad \angle z = \pm\,\omega_d T \ \text{rad} \]
\(\sigma\)
Decay rate of the pole, \(\sigma=\zeta\omega_n\): the distance of the s-plane pole from the imaginary axis.
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency in rad/s: the imaginary part of the s-plane pole.
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency in rad/s.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(|z|\)
Radius of the z-plane pole; less than 1 means stable.
\(\angle z\)
Angle of the z-plane pole in radians: how far the oscillation turns per sample.
\(j\)
The imaginary unit.
How to read it Radius is set by the decay rate \(\sigma\): faster decay means a smaller radius. Angle is set by the oscillation frequency \(\omega_d\): it is the number of radians the oscillation turns through in one sample.

Final value theorem (discrete)

In continuous time \(x_{ss} = \lim_{s\to0} sX(s)\). The discrete version (slide 21) replaces \(s\) by \((1 - z^{-1})\) and \(s \to 0\) by \(z \to 1\):

Discrete FVT\[ x_{ss} = \lim_{k\to\infty} x(k) = \lim_{z\to 1}\,(1 - z^{-1})\,X(z) \]
\(x_{ss}\)
Steady-state value of the sequence.
\(x(k)\)
The sequence in the sample domain.
\(X(z)\)
Its z-transform.
\((1-z^{-1})\)
Cancels the pole at \(z=1\); the discrete counterpart of the \(s\) in the continuous theorem.
\(z\to 1\)
Corresponds to \(s\to 0\), that is DC.
Only if it settlesThe theorem is valid only when \((1-z^{-1})X(z)\) has all its poles strictly inside the unit circle. Apply it to an unstable or oscillating sequence and it returns a number that means nothing.
Worked example 4.3 steady-state value and DC gain

Using \(H(z) = 0.4/(z-0.8)\) from example 3.3 with a unit step input \(U(z) = z/(z-1)\):

1
\( (1 - z^{-1})\,Y(z) = \dfrac{z-1}{z}\cdot\dfrac{z}{z-1}\cdot\dfrac{0.4}{z-0.8} = \dfrac{0.4}{z-0.8} \), pole at 0.8, inside the circle, so the theorem applies.
2
Let \(z \to 1\): \(y_{ss} = 0.4/0.2 = 2\). This matches the table in example 3.3.
Shortcut worth remembering: the DC gain of a discrete system is \(H(1)\), just as the DC gain of a continuous one is \(H(0)\). This is the condition used to match gains in the MPZ method below.

05Discrete equivalents of a continuous controller

Given \(D_c(s)\) and \(T\), find \(D_d(z)\). Each method makes a different assumption about what the signal does between samples. That assumption is the thing to remember; the formulas follow from it.

Tustin's method (slide 31)

Start from the simplest controller, an integrator \(D(s) = U/E = 1/s\), so \(u(t) = \int e\,dt\). Between two samples, add the area under \(e\) using a trapezoid:

\[ u(k) = u(k-1) + \frac{T}{2}\,\big[e(k) + e(k-1)\big] \]
\(u(k)\)
Integrator output (running area) at sample \(k\).
\(u(k-1)\)
Integrator output one sample earlier.
\(e(k),\ e(k-1)\)
Input to the integrator now and one sample earlier.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(\tfrac{T}{2}[\,\cdot\,]\)
Area of the trapezoid between the two input samples.

Take z-transforms: \(U(1 - z^{-1}) = \tfrac{T}{2}E(1 + z^{-1})\), so

\[ \frac{U(z)}{E(z)} = \frac{T}{2}\,\frac{1+z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}} \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{s} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{\,s = \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}} = \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{z-1}{z+1}\,} \]
\(U(z),\ E(z)\)
z-transforms of the integrator output and input.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(z^{-1}\)
One-sample delay operator.
\(1/s\)
The continuous integrator that the trapezoid rule stands in for.
\(s = \frac{2}{T}\frac{z-1}{z+1}\)
The Tustin substitution: replace every \(s\) in \(D_c(s)\) with this.

Since any \(D_c(s)\) can be built from integrators, substituting this for every \(s\) gives the Tustin equivalent.

Frequency warping (beyond the slides, useful to know)Tustin squashes the whole \(j\omega\) axis onto the unit circle, so frequencies get compressed. The digital filter's response at \(\omega\) equals the analog response at \(\omega_a = \frac{2}{T}\tan\frac{\omega T}{2}\). At low \(\omega T\) the two agree. Example: \(T = 0.05\) s, \(\omega = 20\) rad/s gives \(\omega_a = 40\tan(0.5) = 21.9\) rad/s, a 9% shift. If one frequency must be exact (a notch, a crossover), use the prewarped substitution \(s = \dfrac{\omega_0}{\tan(\omega_0 T/2)}\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}\).

ZOH equivalent (slides 32 to 33)

Assume the input to \(D_c(s)\) is held constant between samples, exactly as a ZOH would hold it. A staircase is a sum of steps: each step is a positive unit step minus the same step delayed by one sample. The response of \(D_c\) to a step is \(D_c(s)/s\), so

ZOH (step-invariant) equivalent\[ D_d(z) = \mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{D_c(s)}{s}\right\} - z^{-1}\mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{D_c(s)}{s}\right\} = (1-z^{-1})\,\mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{D_c(s)}{s}\right\} \]
\(D_d(z)\)
The discrete equivalent controller.
\(D_c(s)\)
The continuous controller being converted.
\(D_c(s)/s\)
Laplace transform of the step response of \(D_c\).
\(\mathcal{Z}\{\cdot\}\)
z-transform of the sampled time signal whose Laplace transform is inside the braces.
\((1-z^{-1})\)
Divides out the unit step \(z/(z-1)\) that was applied.
\(z^{-1}\)
One-sample delay operator.

By construction, the step response of \(D_d(z)\) equals the step response of \(D_c(s)\) sampled at \(t = kT\). The same formula is how you discretise a plant driven by a real ZOH, and for a plant it is exact.

Matched pole-zero (MPZ) and modified MPZ (slides 34 to 36)

Map every finite pole and zero with \(z = e^{sT}\), then fix the gain:

\[ D_c(s) = K_c\,\frac{s+a}{s+b} \quad\longrightarrow\quad D_d(z) = K_d\,\frac{z-e^{-aT}}{z-e^{-bT}} \]
\(D_c(s)\)
Continuous lead or lag compensator.
\(K_c\)
Its gain.
\(a\)
Zero location: zero at \(s=-a\).
\(b\)
Pole location: pole at \(s=-b\).
\(D_d(z)\)
Matched pole-zero discrete equivalent.
\(K_d\)
Discrete gain, chosen so the DC gains match.
\(e^{-aT},\ e^{-bT}\)
The zero and pole mapped through \(z=e^{sT}\).
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
  1. Match poles and zeros: \(s = -a \mapsto z = e^{-aT}\).
  2. Fix the order. MPZ: if \(D_c\) has zeros at infinity (more poles than zeros), add factors \((z+1)\) to the numerator until numerator and denominator have equal order. The point \(z = -1\) is where \(\omega = \pi/T\), the highest frequency a sampled system can represent, so it stands in for "infinity". Modified MPZ: add one fewer \((z+1)\), so the numerator is one order lower than the denominator.
  3. Match DC gains: \(\lim_{s\to0}D_c(s) = \lim_{z\to1}D_d(z)\), i.e. \(D_c(0) = D_d(1)\).
Gain for a first-order lead/lag, no (z+1) factors\[ K_c\frac{a}{b} = K_d\,\frac{1-e^{-aT}}{1-e^{-bT}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad K_d = K_c\,\frac{a}{b}\,\frac{1-e^{-bT}}{1-e^{-aT}} \]
\(K_c\,a/b\)
DC gain of the continuous compensator (set \(s=0\)).
\(K_d\frac{1-e^{-aT}}{1-e^{-bT}}\)
DC gain of the discrete compensator (set \(z=1\)).
\(K_d\)
The discrete gain being solved for.
\(a,\ b\)
Zero and pole locations of the continuous compensator.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
Watch the (z+1) factorsThe \(K_d\) formula on slide 34 has no \((z+1)\) in it. Every \((z+1)\) you add is worth \(2\) at \(z = 1\), so divide \(K_d\) by \(2^m\) for \(m\) added factors. The slide 37 table shows it: for \(5/(s+5)\), MPZ gives \(0.0945\frac{z+1}{z-0.811}\) and MMPZ gives \(0.189\frac{1}{z-0.811}\). The MPZ gain is exactly half.

Why modify? With equal orders, the difference equation contains a term in \(e(k)\): \(u(k) = \cdots + \alpha\,e(k) + \cdots\). The processor must read \(e(k)\), compute, and output \(u(k)\) in the same instant, which is impossible in practice. Making the numerator one order lower removes the \(e(k)\) term, so \(u(k)\) only needs past errors and a full sample period is available for computation (slides 35 to 36).

Lead and lag compensatorsA lead or lag has one finite zero and one pole, so the orders are already equal and there are no zeros at infinity. MPZ and MMPZ give the same answer, and \(u(k)\) necessarily depends on \(e(k)\) with every method. The (z+1) business only matters for strictly proper \(D_c(s)\), like a low-pass filter (example 5.2).
Worked example 5.1 one lead compensator, three discrete equivalents

Digitise with \(T = 0.05\) s (\(\omega_s = 2\pi/T = 125.7\) rad/s, about \(22\,\omega_{BW}\)).

Table: \(\mathcal{Z}\{\cdot\} = 32\left[\tfrac{1}{8}\tfrac{z}{z-1} + \tfrac{7}{8}\tfrac{z}{z-p}\right]\) with \(p = e^{-8T} = e^{-0.4} = 0.6703\).
3
Multiply by \((1 - z^{-1}) = \frac{z-1}{z}\): \[ D_d(z) = 32\left[\frac18 + \frac78\,\frac{z-1}{z-p}\right] = 4\,\frac{(z-p) + 7(z-1)}{z-p} = 32\,\frac{z - \frac{p+7}{8}}{z-p} \]
4
\(\dfrac{p+7}{8} = \dfrac{7.6703}{8} = 0.9588\), so \(D_d(z) = 32\,\dfrac{z-0.9588}{z-0.6703}\). DC check: \(32 \times 0.0412/0.3297 = 4.00\).

The ZOH method makes the step response match at the samples, not the frequency response.
Worked example 5.2 where the (z+1) factors and the notch come from

Discretise the low-pass filter \(G_c(s) = \dfrac{25}{s^2 + 5s + 25}\) (\(\omega_n = 5\), \(\zeta = 0.5\), two zeros at infinity) with \(T = 0.2\) s by MPZ and MMPZ.

1

06Choosing the sample rate

The goal (slide 60) is the slowest sample rate that still meets every performance spec, because faster sampling costs processor time, converter speed and money. The rule of thumb for emulation design (slide 39), with \(\omega_s = 2\pi/T\) and \(\omega_{BW}\) the closed-loop bandwidth:

\(\omega_s / \omega_{BW}\)What happens to the emulated design
below 5Likely unstable
below 10Damping degrades noticeably
about 20Reasonable
above 25Use with confidence

Why: the ZOH delay costs \(\omega_c T/2\) radians of phase at crossover. That loss is small only if \(T\) is small relative to the loop's timescale.

Phase margin lost to the hold\[ \Delta\phi \approx \frac{\omega_c T}{2}\ \text{rad} = \frac{\omega_c T}{2}\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \]
\(\Delta\phi\)
Phase margin lost because of the hold.
\(\omega_c\)
Gain crossover frequency in rad/s.
\(T\)
Sample period in seconds: the time between two samples.
\(180^\circ/\pi\)
Converts radians to degrees.

Choose \(T \approx 0.05\) s (20 Hz). The continuous design overshoots 4.3%. Figure 6.1 confirms the trend: the damping erodes as \(\omega_s/\omega_{BW}\) falls, as the slide 39 rule predicts. The rule is a guideline rather than a sharp boundary: at \(3.7\times\) this particular loop is still stable, but with 50% overshoot it is no longer the design you made.

Other factors that set a lower limit on \(\omega_s\) (slide 60):

  • Tracking: the closed loop must follow the reference; \(\omega_s\) is tied to \(\omega_{BW}\) as above.
  • Regulation: disturbances arriving between samples go uncorrected until the next sample; slow sampling lets them grow.
  • Prefilter: the anti-alias filter adds its own phase lag, and its cutoff must sit well below \(\omega_s/2\) but well above \(\omega_c\). That squeeze pushes \(\omega_s\) up.

07Hardware: converters, aliasing and prefilters

A/D and D/A converters

Converters turn voltages into digital words and back. They are specified by resolution (bits) and speed (samples per second). Cost rises with both (slide 55).

Quantisation step\[ q = \frac{V_{\text{range}}}{2^n}, \qquad \text{rms quantisation noise} \approx \frac{q}{\sqrt{12}} \]
\(q\)
Quantisation step: the voltage of one least significant bit.
\(V_{\text{range}}\)
Full-scale input range of the converter in volts.
\(n\)
Number of bits.
\(2^n\)
Number of levels the converter can represent.
\(q/\sqrt{12}\)
rms value of an error spread uniformly over one step.
Worked example 7.1 ADC resolution
1
A 12-bit ADC on a 0 to 3.3 V input (a typical STM32): \(q = 3.3/4096 = 0.806\) mV.
2
A pressure sensor gives 0.3 to 3.0 V over 0 to 10 bar, i.e. 0.27 V/bar, which fits the 3.3 V input range. One LSB is \(0.806\ \text{mV} / 270\ \text{mV/bar} = 0.0030\) bar, so the resolution is 3 mbar before any noise.
If your controller has high gain on the derivative path, a single-LSB flicker is amplified by that gain. Resolution matters most for derivative action and high-gain loops.

Aliasing

A sampler cannot tell apart two sinusoids whose frequencies differ by a multiple of \(f_s\). A high-frequency signal therefore shows up in the samples as a low-frequency one (slide 56 uses 60 Hz sampled at 50 Hz, which looks like 10 Hz).

Apparent frequency\[ f_{\text{alias}} = \big|\,f - n f_s\,\big|, \quad n \text{ the integer that brings the result into } [0,\ f_s/2] \]
\(f_{\text{alias}}\)
Frequency the sampled signal appears to have.
\(f\)
True frequency of the signal.
\(f_s\)
Sample rate.
\(n\)
Integer number of whole sample rates subtracted.
\(f_s/2\)
The Nyquist frequency, the highest frequency that can be represented.
Worked example 7.2 aliasing on a pump station

A level sensor on a pump has 470 Hz vibration pickup. The controller samples at 200 Hz.

1
\(|470 - 2(200)| = 70\) Hz, which is below the 100 Hz Nyquist frequency, so it is the alias.
2
The controller sees a 70 Hz oscillation that does not exist in the level. Worse, a component at \(400 \pm 0.5\) Hz would alias to 0.5 Hz, right inside the control bandwidth, and the controller would try to "correct" it.
3
Fix: a first-order RC prefilter before the ADC. To cut 470 Hz by 10× (20 dB): \(1/\sqrt{1+(470/f_c)^2} = 0.1 \Rightarrow f_c \approx 47\) Hz.
4
Check the cost to the loop: if crossover is 1 Hz, the filter adds \(\arctan(1/47) = 1.2^\circ\) of lag. Acceptable.
Once aliased, noise cannot be removed digitally, because it now sits at a legitimate frequency. The prefilter must be analog and placed before the A/D converter (slides 57 to 58).

The computer

Microprocessor, microcontroller, PLC or PC; the elements are a CPU, clock, memory and power supply (slide 59). The clock is what makes \(T\) constant. A variable loop period breaks every formula in this chapter, so run the control law from a timer interrupt, not from a loop with a delay in it.

08Designing directly in the z-domain

Slides 61 to 62 show that root-locus rules work unchanged in the z-plane: the locus starts at open-loop poles, ends at zeros, and the angle and magnitude conditions are the same. The only change is the stability boundary, which is now the unit circle instead of the imaginary axis. This is not examined in this module, but one example shows why it matters.

Worked example 8.1 sampling can destabilise a loop that is always stable in continuous time

Plant \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s(s+1)}\) with proportional control \(K\). In continuous time \(s^2 + s + K = 0\) is stable for every \(K \gt 0\). Now sample at \(T = 0.2\) s with a ZOH.

1
ZOH equivalent of the plant (section 5 formula applied to \(G\)): \[ G(z) = \frac{(T - 1 + e^{-T})z + (1 - e^{-T} - Te^{-T})}{(z-1)(z-e^{-T})} = \frac{0.01873z + 0.01752}{(z-1)(z-0.8187)} \]
2
Characteristic equation \(1 + KG(z) = 0\): \(z^2 + (0.01873K - 1.8187)z + (0.8187 + 0.01752K) = 0\).
3
For a complex pair, \(|z|^2\) equals the constant term. It reaches the unit circle when \(0.8187 + 0.01752K = 1\), i.e. \(K = 0.1813/0.01752 = 10.34\).
The sampled loop goes unstable at \(K \approx 10.34\), even though the continuous loop never does. The zero at \(z = -0.936\) and the circle boundary are what shape the locus in figure 8.1.

09From D(z) to code

A first-order \(D_d(z) = \dfrac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1}}\) becomes \(u(k) = -a_1u(k-1) + b_0e(k) + b_1e(k-1)\). Using the Tustin result from example 5.1:

// Runs from a 20 Hz timer interrupt (T = 0.05 s)
static float e_prev = 0.0f, u_prev = 0.0f;
const float b0 = 27.333f, b1 = -26.000f, a1 = -0.6667f;

void control_isr(void) {
    float y = adc_read_scaled();      // sample y(kT)
    float e = r - y;                  // error e(k)
    float u = -a1*u_prev + b0*e + b1*e_prev;
    u = clamp(u, U_MIN, U_MAX);       // actuator limits
    dac_write(u);                     // ZOH holds it until next tick
    e_prev = e;  u_prev = u;          // shift the delay line
}

Two practical notes. First, the time between reading \(y\) and writing \(u\) is an extra delay the analysis ignores; keep the code between those two lines short (that is the motivation for MMPZ). Secondly, store the clamped \(u\) as u_prev so the controller knows what the actuator actually did.

10Practice problems

Try each before opening the solution.

P1. Find \(F(z)\) for \(f(k) = 3(0.5)^k - 2\).

Linearity and the geometric row: \(F(z) = \dfrac{3z}{z-0.5} - \dfrac{2z}{z-1}\).

P2. Invert \(Y(z) = \dfrac{z}{(z-0.2)(z-0.7)}\).

\(Y/z = \dfrac{1}{(z-0.2)(z-0.7)} = \dfrac{-2}{z-0.2} + \dfrac{2}{z-0.7}\). So \(y(k) = 2(0.7)^k - 2(0.2)^k\). Check: \(y(0) = 0\), \(y(1) = 1.4 - 0.4 = 1\), matching the long division \(z^{-1} + 0.9z^{-2} + \cdots\)

P3. Map \(s = -1 \pm j2\) to the z-plane with \(T = 0.25\) s.

\(|z| = e^{-0.25} = 0.7788\), \(\angle z = 2(0.25) = 0.5\) rad \(= 28.6^\circ\). \(z = 0.6835 \pm j0.3734\).

P4. Tustin equivalent of \(D_c(s) = \dfrac{s+3}{s+15}\) with \(T = 0.02\) s.

\(2/T = 100\): \(D_d = \dfrac{100(z-1) + 3(z+1)}{100(z-1) + 15(z+1)} = \dfrac{103z - 97}{115z - 85} = 0.8957\,\dfrac{z - 0.9417}{z - 0.7391}\). DC check: \(D_d(1) = 6/30 = 0.2 = D_c(0)\).

P5. MPZ equivalent of the same \(D_c\) and \(T\).

Zero \(e^{-0.06} = 0.9418\), pole \(e^{-0.3} = 0.7408\). \(K_d = \dfrac{3}{15}\cdot\dfrac{1-0.7408}{1-0.9418} = 0.890\). \(D_d = 0.890\,\dfrac{z-0.9418}{z-0.7408}\). Note how close it is to P4.

P6. A closed loop has \(\omega_{BW} = 12\) rad/s. What is the longest sample period for "reasonable" performance?

\(\omega_s = 20 \times 12 = 240\) rad/s, so \(f_s = 38.2\) Hz and \(T = 2\pi/240 = 26\) ms.

P7. 50 Hz mains pickup is sampled at 60 Hz. What frequency appears? And at 1 kHz sampling?

At 60 Hz: \(|50 - 60| = 10\) Hz. At 1 kHz: 50 Hz is below Nyquist (500 Hz) so it appears as 50 Hz, unaliased, and can be filtered digitally.

P8. Steady-state value of \(x(k)\) for \(X(z) = \dfrac{0.3z}{(z-1)(z-0.7)}\)?

\((1 - z^{-1})X = \dfrac{0.3}{z-0.7}\), stable, so \(x_{ss} = 0.3/0.3 = 1\).

P9. With \(\omega_c = 8\) rad/s and \(T = 0.04\) s, how much phase margin does the ZOH cost?

\(\omega_c T/2 = 0.16\) rad \(= 9.2^\circ\).

P10. Why does MPZ put zeros at \(z = -1\) rather than at \(z = 0\)?

A zero at infinity in \(s\) means the gain falls to zero as \(\omega \to \infty\). The highest frequency a sampled system can represent is \(\pi/T\), which maps to \(z = e^{j\pi} = -1\). A zero at \(z = 0\) would only add a pure advance \(z\), with no effect on magnitude.
