    S1Second-order response and a zero[12]

    A closed loop has \(T(s) = \dfrac{16}{s^2 + 4s + 16}\).

    (a)

    Find \(\omega_n\), \(\zeta\) and the poles.

    [3]
    (b)

    Estimate \(t_r\), \(t_p\), \(M_p\) and the 2 % \(t_s\).

    [5]
    (c)

    A zero is added, keeping the DC gain: \(T_z(s) = \dfrac{8(s+2)}{s^2 + 4s + 16}\). Predict how the step response changes and explain why. A simulation gives \(M_p = 70\) %; is that plausible?

    [4]
    Memo

    Approach. Read \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) off the standard form \(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2\), then use the formula-sheet estimates. For the zero, write \(T_z\) in terms of \(T\).

    (a)Natural frequency, damping and poles[3]

    1. Match the constant term:

      \[ \omega_n^2 = 16 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_n = 4 \text{ rad/s} \]
      1

    2. Match the \(s\) term:

      \[ 2\zeta\omega_n = 4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \zeta = \frac{4}{2 \times 4} = 0.5 \]
      1

    3. Poles at \(s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\):

      \[ \sigma = 0.5 \times 4 = 2, \qquad \omega_d = 4\sqrt{1 - 0.25} = 3.46 \text{ rad/s} \]
      1
    \(\omega_n = 4\) rad/s, \(\zeta = 0.5\), poles \(s = -2 \pm 3.46j\).

    (b)Time-domain estimates[5]

    1. Rise time: \(t_r \approx 1.8/\omega_n = 1.8/4 = 0.45\) s.

      1

    2. Peak time: \(t_p = \pi/\omega_d = \pi/3.46 = 0.907\) s.

      1

    3. Overshoot:

      \[ M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} = e^{-\pi(0.5)/0.866} = e^{-1.814} = 0.163 \]
      2

    4. Settling time: \(t_s = 4/\sigma = 4/2 = 2.0\) s.

      1
    \(t_r \approx 0.45\) s, \(t_p = 0.907\) s, \(M_p = 16.3\) %, \(t_s = 2.0\) s.

    Check. A simulation gives 0.41 s, 0.907 s, 16.3 % and 2.02 s. The rise-time rule is the roughest of the four.

    (c)Effect of the zero[4]

    1. The DC gain is unchanged: \(T_z(0) = 8 \times 2/16 = 1\). Split the numerator:

      \[ T_z(s) = \frac{8s + 16}{s^2 + 4s + 16} = T(s) + \tfrac12 sT(s) \]
      1

    2. Multiplying by \(s\) differentiates, so \(y_z(t) = y(t) + \tfrac12\dot y(t)\): the zero adds a multiple of the derivative of the original response.

      1

    3. \(\dot y\) is large and positive during the rise, so the response rises much faster and overshoots much more. The zero is at \(-2\), so \(\alpha = 2/(\zeta\omega_n) = 1\) in FPE's zero plot: as close to the poles as the plot goes, where the effect is largest.

      1

    4. 70 % is plausible (simulation: 69.9 %, \(t_r = 0.12\) s). The settling time hardly changes, because the poles have not moved.

      1
    Faster rise, about four times the overshoot, the same settling time. 70 % is plausible.

    S2Steady-state error and PI control[14]

    \(G(s) = \dfrac{10}{(s+2)(s+5)}\) is in a unity feedback loop.

    (a)

    With \(D = k_P\), find \(k_P\) for a 5 % steady-state error to a unit step.

    [3]
    (b)

    Find \(\zeta\) and \(M_p\) with that gain. Is the design acceptable?

    [4]
    (c)

    Use \(D(s) = k_P\dfrac{s+2}{s}\) instead. Explain why the step error is now zero, choose \(k_P\) for \(\zeta = 0.707\), and give \(k_I\), \(M_p\) and \(t_s\).

    [7]
    Memo

    Approach. Find the system type, then use the error constant for the step error. For the PI controller, cancel the pole, reduce the loop to second order and match \(\zeta\).

    (a)Gain for 5 % error[3]

    1. \(DG\) has no pole at \(s = 0\), so the loop is Type 0. The error is \(E = R/(1 + DG)\); the FVT with \(R = 1/s\) gives derive

      \[ e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to0} s\,\frac{1}{1+DG}\,\frac1s = \frac{1}{1 + K_p}, \qquad K_p = \lim_{s\to0} DG \]
      1

    2. \(K_p = k_P\,G(0) = k_P \times 10/10 = k_P\).

      1

    3. \(\dfrac{1}{1+k_P} = 0.05\) gives \(1 + k_P = 20\).

      1
    \(k_P = 19\).

    (b)Damping with that gain[4]

    1. Characteristic equation \(1 + 19G = 0\):

      \[ (s+2)(s+5) + 190 = s^2 + 7s + 200 = 0 \]
      1

    2. \(\omega_n = \sqrt{200} = 14.1\) rad/s and \(\zeta = \dfrac{7}{2 \times 14.1} = 0.247\).

      1

    3. \(M_p = e^{-\pi(0.247)/\sqrt{1 - 0.061}} = e^{-0.802} = 0.448\).

      1

    4. 45 % overshoot is far too oscillatory: high proportional gain buys accuracy at the cost of damping.

      1
    \(\zeta = 0.247\), \(M_p = 44.8\) %: not acceptable.

    (c)PI with a cancelling zero[7]

    1. The integrator puts a pole at \(s = 0\) in \(DG\): the loop is Type 1, \(K_p = \infty\) and \(e_{ss} = 1/(1 + \infty) = 0\) for a step.

      1

    2. The zero cancels the plant pole at \(-2\):

      \[ DG = k_P\frac{s+2}{s}\cdot\frac{10}{(s+2)(s+5)} = \frac{10k_P}{s(s+5)} \]
      1

    3. Closed loop:

      \[ T(s) = \frac{10k_P}{s^2 + 5s + 10k_P} \]
      1

    4. Match \(2\zeta\omega_n = 5\) with \(\zeta = 0.707\): \(\omega_n = 5/1.414 = 3.54\) rad/s, so \(\omega_n^2 = 12.5 = 10k_P\) and \(k_P = 1.25\).

      2

    5. \(D = k_P + 2k_P/s\), so \(k_I = 2k_P = 2.5\) s⁻¹.

      1

    6. \(\zeta = 0.707\) gives \(M_p = e^{-\pi} = 4.3\) %. \(\sigma = \zeta\omega_n = 2.5\), so \(t_s = 4/2.5 = 1.6\) s.

      1
    \(k_P = 1.25\), \(k_I = 2.5\) s⁻¹, \(M_p = 4.3\) %, \(t_s \approx 1.6\) s (simulation 1.69 s).

    S3Ziegler-Nichols tuning[10]

    \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{(s+1)^3}\) is in a unity feedback loop with \(D = K\).

    (a)

    Find the ultimate gain \(K_u\) and period \(P_u\) by substituting \(s = j\omega\).

    [4]
    (b)

    Give the Ziegler-Nichols PI settings as \(k_P\), \(T_I\) and \(k_I\).

    [3]
    (c)

    The tuned loop has a phase margin of 15° and 56 % overshoot. Explain why, and what you would do next.

    [3]
    Memo

    Approach. The ultimate gain puts closed-loop poles on the imaginary axis, so substitute \(s = j\omega\) and set the real and imaginary parts to zero. Then apply the ultimate-sensitivity table.

    (a)Ultimate gain and period[4]

    1. Characteristic equation: \((s+1)^3 + K = s^3 + 3s^2 + 3s + 1 + K = 0\).

      1

    2. With \(s = j\omega\): \(-j\omega^3 - 3\omega^2 + 3j\omega + 1 + K = 0\). Imaginary part: \(-\omega^3 + 3\omega = 0\), so \(\omega = \sqrt3 = 1.73\) rad/s.

      1

    3. Real part: \(-3\omega^2 + 1 + K = 0\), so \(K_u = 3(3) - 1 = 8\).

      1

    4. \(P_u = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/1.732 = 3.63\) s.

      1
    \(K_u = 8\), \(P_u = 3.63\) s.

    (b)PI settings[3]

    1. Table 4.3 (ultimate sensitivity; quote it, it is not on the formula sheet): \(k_P = 0.45K_u = 0.45 \times 8 = 3.6\).

      1

    2. \(T_I = P_u/1.2 = 3.63/1.2 = 3.02\) s.

      1

    3. \(k_I = k_P/T_I = 3.6/3.02 = 1.19\) s⁻¹.

      1
    \(k_P = 3.6\), \(T_I = 3.02\) s, \(k_I = 1.19\) s⁻¹.

    (c)Why the margin is small[3]

    1. Ziegler-Nichols settings aim for a quarter decay ratio, roughly \(\zeta \approx 0.2\): fast but poorly damped by design, so a small margin and large overshoot are expected.

      1

    2. The rules come from one test on the plant; they are a starting point, not a design to a specification.

      1

    3. Reduce \(k_P\) (or lengthen \(T_I\)) and retune until the phase margin is about 45° to 60° and the overshoot meets the specification.

      1

    S4Margins and bandwidth[14]

    A unity feedback loop has \(KG(s) = \dfrac{40}{s(s+2)(s+10)}\).

    (a)

    Find the phase crossover frequency and the gain margin. By how much could the gain rise before instability?

    [5]
    (b)

    Find the gain crossover frequency and the phase margin.

    [5]
    (c)

    Estimate \(\zeta\), \(M_p\) and the closed-loop bandwidth. A simulation gives \(M_p = 25\) % and \(\omega_{BW} = 2.66\) rad/s; comment.

    [4]
    Memo

    Approach. Write the magnitude and phase of \(KG(j\omega)\). Phase crossover is where the phase is −180°; gain crossover is where the magnitude is 1.

    (a)Phase crossover and gain margin[5]

    1. \(\angle KG = -90^\circ - \tan^{-1}(\omega/2) - \tan^{-1}(\omega/10)\). It equals −180° when \(\tan^{-1}(\omega/2) + \tan^{-1}(\omega/10) = 90^\circ\).

      1

    2. Two angles sum to 90° when the product of their tangents is 1: \((\omega/2)(\omega/10) = 1\), so \(\omega^2 = 20\) and \(\omega = 4.47\) rad/s.

      1

    3. Magnitude there:

      \[ |KG| = \frac{40}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\sqrt{\omega^2+100}} = \frac{40}{\sqrt{20}\sqrt{24}\sqrt{120}} = \frac{40}{240} = \frac16 \]
      2

    4. GM \(= 1/|KG| = 6\), that is 15.6 dB.

      1
    Phase crossover 4.47 rad/s, GM = 6 (15.6 dB): the gain could rise six times, to 240.

    (b)Gain crossover and phase margin[5]

    1. Solve \(|KG| = 1\): \(\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+4}\sqrt{\omega^2+100} = 40\).

      1

    2. First guess from the asymptotes: below 2 rad/s, \(|KG| \approx 40/(20\omega) = 2/\omega\), which is 1 at \(\omega = 2\). The true curve is lower near the break, so the crossing is a little below 2.

      1

    3. Trial: \(\omega = 1.5\) gives 37.9; \(\omega = 1.6\) gives 41.5; \(\omega = 1.56\) gives 40.0. So \(\omega_c = 1.56\) rad/s.

      1

    4. \(\angle KG = -90^\circ - \tan^{-1}0.779 - \tan^{-1}0.156 = -90^\circ - 37.9^\circ - 8.9^\circ = -136.8^\circ\).

      1

    5. PM \(= 180^\circ - 136.8^\circ = 43.2^\circ\).

      1
    \(\omega_c = 1.56\) rad/s, PM = 43.2°.

    (c)Damping and bandwidth estimates[4]

    1. \(\zeta \approx \mathrm{PM}/100 = 0.43\) derive (from the formula-sheet figure; valid below about 60°).

      1

    2. \(M_p = e^{-\pi(0.43)/\sqrt{1-0.185}} = e^{-1.50} = 0.22\), so about 22 %.

      1

    3. The bandwidth lies between \(\omega_c\) and \(2\omega_c\): 1.6 to 3.1 rad/s.

      1

    4. The simulation (25 %, 2.66 rad/s) agrees. The third pole at −10 is far enough out for the dominant-pair estimates to hold; PM/100 slightly overestimates \(\zeta\).

      1

    S5Lead design for a Type 1 plant[16]

    \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s(s+1)}\) is in unity feedback with \(D(s) = K\dfrac{Ts+1}{\alpha Ts+1}\), \(\alpha \lt 1\). Requirements: \(K_v \ge 10\) and PM \(\ge 50^\circ\).

    (a)

    Find \(K\).

    [2]
    (b)

    Find the crossover frequency and phase margin of \(KG\).

    [4]
    (c)

    Design the lead with a 10° allowance: give \(\alpha\), the new crossover, \(T\) and \(D(s)\).

    [8]
    (d)

    Check the achieved phase margin.

    [2]
    Memo

    Approach. Set \(K\) from the error constant, find the margin of \(KG\), then add the missing phase (plus the allowance) at the frequency where the lead's gain lifts the magnitude to 1.

    (a)Gain from \(K_v\)[2]

    1. The lead factor is 1 at \(s = 0\), so derive

      \[ K_v = \lim_{s\to0} sDG = \lim_{s\to0} \frac{sK}{s(s+1)} = K \]
      1

    2. \(K_v \ge 10\) needs \(K = 10\).

      1
    \(K = 10\).

    (b)Margin of \(KG\)[4]

    1. \(|KG| = \dfrac{10}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+1}} = 1\), so \(\omega^4 + \omega^2 - 100 = 0\).

      1

    2. \(\omega^2 = \dfrac{-1 + \sqrt{401}}{2} = 9.51\), so \(\omega_c = 3.08\) rad/s.

      1

    3. \(\angle KG = -90^\circ - \tan^{-1}3.08 = -90^\circ - 72.0^\circ = -162.0^\circ\).

      1

    4. PM \(= 18.0^\circ\): short of 50° by 32°.

      1
    \(\omega_c = 3.08\) rad/s, PM = 18.0°.

    (c)Lead design[8]

    1. Phase to add: \(\phi_{max} = 50^\circ - 18^\circ + 10^\circ = 42^\circ\).

      1

    2. From \(\sin\phi_{max} = \dfrac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\) derive:

      \[ \alpha = \frac{1 - \sin42^\circ}{1 + \sin42^\circ} = \frac{1 - 0.669}{1 + 0.669} = 0.198 \]
      2

    3. At \(\omega_{max}\) the lead raises the gain by \(1/\sqrt\alpha\), so the new crossover is where \(|KG| = \sqrt\alpha = 0.445\) (−7.0 dB).

      1

    4. \(\dfrac{10}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+1}} = 0.445\) gives \(\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+1} = 22.5\), so \(\omega^4 + \omega^2 - 505 = 0\), \(\omega^2 = 21.97\) and \(\omega_c = 4.69\) rad/s.

      2

    5. Put \(\omega_{max} = 1/(T\sqrt\alpha)\) at \(\omega_c\): \(T = \dfrac{1}{4.69 \times 0.445} = 0.479\) s and \(\alpha T = 0.0950\) s.

      1

    6. Substitute into \(D\).

      1
    \(D(s) = 10\,\dfrac{0.479s + 1}{0.0950s + 1}\): zero at −2.09 rad/s, pole at −10.5 rad/s.

    (d)Check[2]

    1. At 4.69 rad/s: \(\angle KG = -90^\circ - \tan^{-1}4.69 = -168.0^\circ\), and the lead adds its full \(42.0^\circ\): total −126.0°.

      1

    2. PM \(= 54.0^\circ \ge 50^\circ\): met.

      1

    Check. The 10° allowance was more than needed here; the simulated overshoot is 18 %.

    S6Lag design for steady-state accuracy[16]

    \(G(s) = \dfrac{1}{(s+1)(0.1s+1)}\) is in unity feedback with the unity DC gain lag \(D(s) = K\dfrac{Ts+1}{\alpha Ts+1}\), \(\alpha \gt 1\). Requirements: at most 2 % step error and PM \(\ge 50^\circ\).

    (a)

    Find \(K\).

    [3]
    (b)

    Find the phase margin of \(KG\).

    [4]
    (c)

    Design the lag with a 5° allowance and the zero a decade below crossover.

    [7]
    (d)

    State the achieved margin and the cost of the lag.

    [2]
    Memo

    Approach. Set \(K\) for the error, then move crossover down to where the plant alone has the phase you need, and let the lag remove the extra gain there.

    (a)Gain for 2 % error[3]

    1. Type 0 loop: \(K_p = \lim_{s\to0} DG = K\,G(0) = K\).

      1

    2. \(e_{ss} = \dfrac{1}{1 + K_p}\) derive (FVT on \(E = R/(1+DG)\)).

      1

    3. \(\dfrac{1}{1+K} \le 0.02\) needs \(K \ge 49\).

      1
    \(K = 49\).

    (b)Margin of \(KG\)[4]

    1. \(|KG| = 1\): \((1+\omega^2)(1+0.01\omega^2) = 49^2 = 2401\), so \(0.01\omega^4 + 1.01\omega^2 - 2400 = 0\).

      1

    2. \(\omega^2 = \dfrac{-1.01 + \sqrt{1.02 + 96}}{0.02} = 442\), so \(\omega_c = 21.0\) rad/s.

      1

    3. \(\angle KG = -\tan^{-1}21.0 - \tan^{-1}2.10 = -87.3^\circ - 64.6^\circ = -151.8^\circ\).

      1

    4. PM \(= 28.2^\circ\): too small.

      1
    \(\omega_c = 21.0\) rad/s, PM = 28.2°.

    (c)Lag design[7]

    1. Needed plant phase at the new crossover: \(-180^\circ + 50^\circ + 5^\circ = -125^\circ\) (the 5° covers the lag's own phase there).

      1

    2. \(\tan^{-1}\omega + \tan^{-1}0.1\omega = 125^\circ\). Taking tangents, \(\dfrac{1.1\omega}{1 - 0.1\omega^2} = \tan125^\circ = -1.428\), so \(0.1428\omega^2 - 1.1\omega - 1.428 = 0\) and \(\omega_c = 8.83\) rad/s.

      2

    3. \(|KG(j8.83)| = \dfrac{49}{\sqrt{1+78.0}\sqrt{1+0.780}} = \dfrac{49}{8.89 \times 1.334} = 4.13\). The lag's high-frequency gain is \(1/\alpha\), so \(\alpha = 4.13\).

      2

    4. Zero a decade below crossover: \(1/T = 0.883\) rad/s, \(T = 1.13\) s; then \(\alpha T = 4.68\) s (pole at 0.214 rad/s).

      1

    5. Substitute into \(D\).

      1
    \(D(s) = 49\,\dfrac{1.13s + 1}{4.68s + 1}\)

    (d)Result and cost[2]

    1. The lag adds −4.3° at 8.86 rad/s, inside the allowance: PM = 50.6° with a 2 % step error.

      1

    2. Crossover falls from 21 to 8.9 rad/s, so the loop is slower, and the slow pole-zero pair adds a long tail to the step response.

      1

    S7Root locus with complex poles[14]

    Sketch the root locus of \(1 + KL(s) = 0\), \(L(s) = \dfrac{1}{s(s^2 + 4s + 8)}\), for \(K \gt 0\).

    (a)

    Give the real-axis segment and the asymptotes.

    [3]
    (b)

    Find the departure angle from the pole at \(-2 + 2j\).

    [4]
    (c)

    Is there a break-away point?

    [3]
    (d)

    Find the gain and frequency at the imaginary-axis crossing, and sketch the locus.

    [4]
    Memo

    Approach. Work through the rules in order: real axis, asymptotes, departure angles, break-away, imaginary-axis crossing. Then join the pieces.

    (a)Real axis and asymptotes[3]

    1. Poles at 0 and \(-2 \pm 2j\), no zeros: three branches, all going to infinity.

      1

    2. A real-axis point is on the locus when an odd number of real poles and zeros lie to its right: \((-\infty, 0]\). (Complex pairs do not count.)

      1

    3. Centroid \(\alpha = \dfrac{0 - 2 - 2}{3} = -1.33\); angles \(\dfrac{180^\circ + 360^\circ l}{3} = 60^\circ, 180^\circ, 300^\circ\).

      1
    Real axis \((-\infty, 0]\); asymptotes from −1.33 at ±60° and 180°.

    (b)Departure angle[4]

    1. Angle condition at a point just next to \(p_1 = -2 + 2j\): the sum of angles from all poles is \(180^\circ + 360^\circ l\) (no zeros).

      1

    2. From the pole at 0: \(\angle(-2 + 2j) = 135^\circ\). From \(-2 - 2j\): \(\angle(4j) = 90^\circ\).

      1

    3. \(-\phi_{dep} - 135^\circ - 90^\circ = 180^\circ\) gives \(\phi_{dep} = -405^\circ \equiv -45^\circ\).

      1

    4. The branch leaves heading down and to the right, then bends towards the 60° asymptote. The lower pole departs at +45° by symmetry.

      1
    \(\phi_{dep} = -45^\circ\).

    (c)Break-away[3]

    1. Break-away points satisfy \(dK/ds = 0\) with \(K = -(s^3 + 4s^2 + 8s)\): \(3s^2 + 8s + 8 = 0\).

      1

    2. \(s = \dfrac{-8 \pm \sqrt{64 - 96}}{6} = -1.33 \pm 0.94j\): complex, so not on the real-axis segment.

      1

    3. There is no break-away: the branch from 0 runs straight left along the real axis.

      1

    (d)Imaginary-axis crossing and sketch[4]

    1. Characteristic equation \(s^3 + 4s^2 + 8s + K = 0\) with \(s = j\omega\): \(-j\omega^3 - 4\omega^2 + 8j\omega + K = 0\).

      1

    2. Imaginary part: \(-\omega^3 + 8\omega = 0\), so \(\omega = \sqrt8 = 2.83\) rad/s.

      1

    3. Real part: \(K = 4\omega^2 = 32\).

      1

    4. Sketch: the complex branches leave at ∓45°, cross the axis at ±2.83j and follow the ±60° asymptotes; the real branch runs from 0 to −∞.

      1
    Crossing at \(\pm 2.83j\) with \(K = 32\): stable for \(0 \lt K \lt 32\).

    S8Canonical forms and a hidden mode[14]

    \(G(s) = \dfrac{2s + 6}{s^3 + 6s^2 + 11s + 6}\).

    (a)

    Write the control canonical form.

    [4]
    (b)

    Test controllability and observability of that realisation.

    [5]
    (c)

    Explain the result by factorising \(G\).

    [3]
    (d)

    Give a second-order realisation that is both controllable and observable.

    [2]
    Memo

    Approach. Read the canonical form straight off the coefficients, build the controllability and observability matrices, and look for a pole-zero cancellation to explain any loss of rank.

    (a)Control canonical form[4]

    1. The denominator is monic: \(a(s) = s^3 + 6s^2 + 11s + 6\), \(b(s) = 2s + 6\).

      1

    2. First row of \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(-a_1, -a_2, -a_3\), with ones below the diagonal; \(\mathbf{B}\) is the first unit vector:

      \[ \mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -6 & -11 & -6 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
      2

    3. \(\mathbf{C}\) holds the numerator coefficients: \(\mathbf{C}_c = [0 \;\; 2 \;\; 6]\), \(D = 0\).

      1

    (b)Controllability and observability[5]

    1. \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = [-6 \;\; 1 \;\; 0]^T\), \(\mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B} = [25 \;\; -6 \;\; 1]^T\). \(\mathcal{C} = [\mathbf{B} \;\; \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \;\; \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B}]\) is upper triangular with ones on the diagonal: \(\det\mathcal{C} = 1\), controllable (control form always is).

      1

    2. \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} = [2 \;\; 6 \;\; 0]\) and \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^2 = [-6 \;\; -22 \;\; -12]\):

      \[ \mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 6 \\ 2 & 6 & 0 \\ -6 & -22 & -12 \end{bmatrix} \]
      1

    3. Expand along the first row: \(\det\mathcal{O} = -2(2 \times -12 - 0) + 6(2 \times -22 - 6 \times -6) = 48 - 48 = 0\).

      2

    4. \(\det\mathcal{O} = 0\): not observable.

      1
