ADC, DAC, audio and calibration

How a microcontroller reads and produces analog voltages: the ADC and DAC conversion formulas, quantisation and the error sources, the main converter types, the STM32F407 ADC's modes, audio over I2S, op-amp gain and offset, and calibrating a sensor reading against measurements.

01Analog-to-digital conversion

Reading Lecture 22, slides 2 to 17

Most sensors produce an analog voltage: thermocouples, magnetic field sensors, MEMS accelerometers and gyros, current sensors, touch screens. (A sensor with an I2C or SPI output has already done the conversion inside itself.) To use such a voltage as a number rather than as a 0 or 1, the microcontroller needs an analog-to-digital converter (ADC).

Example: the MCP9700 temperature sensor has three pins (\(V_{DD}\), GND and \(V_{OUT}\)) and outputs \(V_{OUT} = 0.5\text{ V} + 0.01\text{ V/°C} \times T_A\). At 20 °C that is 0.7 V. If the microcontroller can measure \(V_{OUT}\), it can compute the temperature and display it, log it, or switch a heater or fan.

The conversion formula

An ideal ADC is linear: the output number is proportional to where the input sits between two reference voltages. The output \(R\) is an integer of \(N\) bits, so the exact value is truncated: this is quantisation.

ADC output\[ R = \left\lfloor (2^N - 1)\,\frac{V_{IN} - V_{REF-}}{V_{REF+} - V_{REF-}} \right\rfloor \]
\(R\)
Converted value, an integer from 0 to \(2^N - 1\).
\(N\)
Resolution in bits.
\(V_{IN}\)
Input voltage, which must lie between the references (V).
\(V_{REF-}\)
Lower reference, the voltage that gives 0 (V). Often 0 V.
\(V_{REF+}\)
Upper reference, the voltage that gives \(2^N - 1\) (V).

With \(V_{REF-} = 0\) this reduces to \(R = (2^N - 1) V_{IN}/V_{MAX}\). Going backwards, \(V_{IN} = V_{REF-} + R\,(V_{REF+} - V_{REF-})/(2^N - 1)\).

Figure 1.1: With \(N = 3\) there are 8 codes over 0 to 3.3 V. Truncation puts each step at or below the ideal line (dashed): every input in one LSB-wide band gives the same code. Code 7 is reached only at exactly 3.3 V. Real ADCs have thousands of steps, but the shape is the same.

Worked example 1.1 both directions

(a) A 12-bit ADC with references 0 V and 3.3 V reads 1.25 V. What is \(R\)? What would a 10-bit ADC give? (b) A 12-bit ADC with references −2.5 V and +2.5 V outputs 3000. What is \(V_{IN}\)?

1
(a) \(R = \lfloor 4095 \times 1.25/3.3 \rfloor = \lfloor 1551.1 \rfloor = 1551\). With 10 bits: \(\lfloor 1023 \times 1.25/3.3 \rfloor = \lfloor 387.5 \rfloor = 387\).
2
(b) \(V_{IN} = -2.5 + 3000 \times 5/4095 = -2.5 + 3.663 = 1.163\) V.
(a) 1551 (12-bit), 387 (10-bit). (b) 1.163 V.

Resolution and errors

The number of bits is the resolution. Because of quantisation the smallest change the ADC can see, one LSB, is:

Size of one LSB\[ \Delta V = \frac{V_{REF+} - V_{REF-}}{2^N - 1} \]
\(\Delta V\)
Voltage step per code, the precision (V/LSB).
\(V_{REF+} - V_{REF-}\)
Input range (V).
\(N\)
Resolution (bits).

A 12-bit ADC over 0 to 5 V resolves 1.22 mV/LSB; over 0 to 3.3 V, 0.806 mV/LSB. Nothing finer can be measured.

  • Offset error and gain error shift and tilt the transfer line, as in an amplifier.
  • Integral non-linearity (INL): the largest deviation of the actual transfer characteristic from a straight line, in LSB.
  • Differential non-linearity (DNL): the largest deviation of any one step from its ideal size of 1 LSB.

Watch out

Two slide statements need care. Slide 12 says a flash ADC needs \(2^n\) comparators; an \(n\)-bit flash converter needs \(2^n - 1\) (one per threshold between codes: 255 for 8 bits). Slide 13 says a smaller resistor gives a slower discharge and higher resolution; the discharge time constant is \(RC\), so a larger resistor discharges more slowly, gives more counts (higher resolution) and a longer conversion, and a smaller resistor the reverse.

Figure 1.2: The DAC output for code \(k\) is \(k \times 3.3/15\) V. Testing 1000 gives 1.76 V, below 2.0 V, so b3 stays. 1100 gives 2.64 V, too high, so b2 is cleared. 1010 gives 2.20 V, cleared. 1001 gives 1.98 V, kept. The result 1001 = 9 matches \(\lfloor 15 \times 2.0/3.3 \rfloor = 9\).

Conversion takes time, so the input must not change during it. A sample-and-hold circuit (a FET switch, a capacitor and a unity-gain buffer) does this. Sample: the switch closes and the capacitor charges to the input. Hold: the switch opens and the buffer keeps the output equal to the capacitor voltage.

Figure 1.3: At each sample instant (dots) the held value (amber) jumps to the input (teal) and then stays flat for the conversion.

Sampling modes and specifications

One-shot conversions suit occasional readings (the current temperature). Continuous conversion samples at a fixed rate (audio, signal processing), with the Nyquist limit: sample faster than twice the highest frequency. A timer supplies the sample clock and DMA usually stores the results in an array. One ADC can read several channels through a multiplexer, one channel at a time.

Datasheets give the resolution, the maximum conversion rate (samples per second, SPS) and the non-linearity in LSB. High-speed ADCs exceed 10 MSPS; high-precision ADCs exceed 16 bits.

02The STM32F407 ADC

Reading Lecture 22, slides 18 to 23

  • Three 12-bit SAR ADCs, 19 multiplexed channels: 16 external, the internal temperature sensor, internal \(V_{REF}\) and \(V_{BAT}\).
  • Resolution 12, 10, 8 or 6 bits; single or continuous mode; automatic scan of a channel sequence; trigger from timers or external sources; DMA request after each conversion.
  • External channels: 0 to 7 on PA0 to PA7, 8 and 9 on PB0 and PB1, 10 to 15 on PC0 to PC5.
  • The ADC clock comes from APB2, divided by 2, 4, 6 or 8. Conversion time is a selectable sampling time plus a fixed number of cycles set by the resolution. The minimums are 3 + 12 = 15 ADCCLK cycles (12 bits), 13 (10 bits), 11 (8 bits) and 9 (6 bits).
Conversion time\[ t_{conv} = \frac{n_{sample} + N}{f_{ADCCLK}}, \qquad f_{ADCCLK} = \frac{f_{APB2}}{2, 4, 6 \text{ or } 8} \]
\(t_{conv}\)
Time for one conversion (s).
\(n_{sample}\)
Sampling time in ADC clock cycles, at least 3.
\(N\)
Resolution in bits, which is also the number of SAR cycles.
\(f_{ADCCLK}\)
ADC clock (Hz).
\(f_{APB2}\)
APB2 peripheral clock (Hz).

Channel selection is a sequence of up to 16 entries. With SCAN = 0 only the first entry is converted; with SCAN = 1 the whole sequence. There is only one data register, so scan mode almost always uses DMA so results are not overwritten.

CONT = 1 starts a new conversion (or sequence) as soon as the last finishes, as fast as the ADC can go, so the program can read the latest value without waiting. An injected group can interrupt the regular sequence for a one-off conversion. Without CONT, a conversion starts from software (a bit in the control register) or from a timer event (update/TRGO or a compare channel), which gives a fixed sample rate.

Worked example 2.1 maximum sample rate

APB2 is 84 MHz and the ADC prescaler is 4. Find the shortest 12-bit conversion time and the sample rate, and the rate at 6 bits.

1
\(f_{ADCCLK} = 84/4 = 21\) MHz.
2
12 bits: \(15/21\text{ MHz} = 714\) ns, so 1.4 MSPS.
3
6 bits: \(21\text{ MHz}/9 = 2.33\) MSPS.
714 ns and 1.4 MSPS at 12 bits; 2.33 MSPS at 6 bits. Lower resolution buys speed.

03Digital-to-analog conversion

Reading Lecture 23, slides 2 to 9

A DAC does the reverse: a number in, a voltage out. A stream of numbers becomes a time-varying signal, used for audio output, software-defined radio and variable-speed motor control. The ideal DAC has the same linear relationship:

DAC output\[ V_{OUT} = V_{REF-} + \frac{R\,(V_{REF+} - V_{REF-})}{2^N - 1} \]
\(V_{OUT}\)
Output voltage (V).
\(R\)
Digital input value, 0 to \(2^N - 1\).
\(V_{REF-}, V_{REF+}\)
Output for \(R = 0\) and for \(R = 2^N - 1\) (V).
\(N\)
Resolution (bits).

Binary-weighted and R-2R

The binary-weighted resistor DAC uses an inverting summing op-amp. Each input's gain is \(-R_f/R_i\), so choosing the resistors as \(R, 2R, 4R, \ldots\) weights each bit by its binary value. Each bit switches its input between 0 V and \(V_{ref}\).

Figure 3.1: The MSB feeds through \(R\), so it contributes the most current into the virtual-earth node; each lower bit's resistor is double the one above, halving its contribution. All the currents flow through \(R_f\), so the output is their weighted sum, inverted.

Binary-weighted DAC\[ V_{out} = -R_f\left(\frac{V_1}{R} + \frac{V_2}{2R} + \frac{V_3}{4R} + \cdots + \frac{V_n}{2^{n-1}R}\right) \]
\(V_{out}\)
Output voltage (V), negative because the amplifier inverts.
\(R_f\)
Feedback resistor (Ω).
\(V_1 \ldots V_n\)
Bit voltages, 0 or \(V_{ref}\); \(V_1\) is the MSB (V).
\(R\)
Resistor of the MSB input (Ω).

With \(R_f = R/2\) this gives \(V_{out} = -V_{ref} \times \text{value}/2^N\).

Worked example 3.1 a 4-bit binary-weighted DAC

\(V_{ref} = 3.3\) V, \(R_f = 0.5R\). Find \(V_{out}\) for the input 11012 = 13.

1
\(V_1 = 3.3, V_2 = 3.3, V_3 = 0, V_4 = 3.3\) V.
2
\[ V_{out} = -0.5R\left(\frac{3.3}{R} + \frac{3.3}{2R} + \frac{3.3}{8R}\right) = -0.5 \times 3.3 \times 1.625 \]
3
\(V_{out} = -2.681\) V. Check: \(-3.3 \times 13/16 = -2.681\) V.
−2.681 V.
Watch out

The binary-weighted circuit divides by \(2^N\) (full scale is \(15/16\) of \(V_{ref}\) for 4 bits), while the ideal DAC formula divides by \(2^N - 1\). Use the formula that matches the question: circuit questions use the summing formula.

Drawbacks: many different resistor values over a wide range (each double the last), and resistor tolerances cause inaccuracy. The R-2R ladder still uses an inverting summing amplifier but needs only two values, \(R\) and \(2R\), with the same end result.

PWM DAC

Not every microcontroller has a DAC, but most have timers with PWM (Week 6). Set the duty cycle to the value to convert (CCR = the number), and the average of the PWM output is the analog value. For a changing signal, load a new CCR every time the timer overflows, using DMA. A low-pass filter averages the PWM into a smooth voltage: a simple RC filter works, but a higher-order filter is better.

Worked example 3.2 PWM DAC resolution against frequency

A timer at 84 MHz (no prescaler) makes a 12-bit PWM DAC (ARR + 1 = 4096). What is the PWM frequency, and is an RC filter with \(R = 1.6\) kΩ, \(C = 100\) nF suitable?

1
\(f_{PWM} = 84\text{ MHz}/4096 = 20.5\) kHz.
2
\(f_c = 1/(2\pi R C) = 1/(2\pi \times 1600 \times 100 \times 10^{-9}) = 995\) Hz.
20.5 kHz PWM, filtered at about 1 kHz: the carrier is 20 times the cutoff, so a first-order filter passes slow signals but leaves some ripple; an 8-bit DAC (256 steps) would run at 328 kHz and filter far more easily.

04Audio, I2S and PCM

Reading Lecture 23, slides 10 to 16

Sound is a pressure wave in a medium; an audio signal is that wave as a voltage. Audible frequencies run from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, so by Nyquist good digital audio is sampled above 40 kHz. Mono has one channel, stereo two (left and right), with samples interleaved. The chain is microphone, signal conditioning, ADC, microcontroller or DSP, DAC (or I2S to an audio DAC), amplifier, loudspeaker.

PCM (pulse code modulation) samples at uniform intervals and quantises each sample to an integer: for example 16-bit 44.1 kHz, 24-bit 96 kHz or 24-bit 192 kHz. Audio PCM values are signed, two's complement, centred on zero.

I2S (Integrated Inter-IC Sound), also from Philips/NXP, is a serial bus for digital audio between audio ADCs, DACs and processors. It is not I2C. It has three lines:

  • SCK, the serial clock (on STM32 the SPI SCK pin);
  • WS, word select: 0 = left, 1 = right. It runs \(n\) times slower than SCK, where \(n\) is the bits per sample (on STM32 the hardware NSS pin);
  • SD, serial data, MSB first; the sample length is not fixed by the standard (on STM32 the MOSI pin).

Because it is so like SPI, ST uses the same peripheral for both.

Figure 4.1: WS (amber) is low for the left sample and high for the right. The data (SD) changes one clock after WS, so the MSB of each word follows the WS edge by one SCK period, and the LSB of the previous word sits in the first clock after the edge.

I2S clock rates\[ f_{WS} = f_s \qquad f_{SCK} = n_{ch}\, n\, f_s \]
\(f_{WS}\)
Word select frequency, one cycle per stereo frame (Hz).
\(f_s\)
Sample rate (Hz).
\(f_{SCK}\)
Serial clock, also the bit rate (Hz).
\(n_{ch}\)
Channels, 2 for stereo.
\(n\)
Bits per sample.

CD audio (16-bit, 44.1 kHz stereo): \(f_{SCK} = 2 \times 16 \times 44\,100 = 1.4112\) MHz.

05Signal conditioning

Reading Lecture 23, slides 17 to 20

A microcontroller ADC only accepts 0 V to \(V_{DD}\): it cannot convert negative voltages, and a small signal uses only a few LSB of the range, giving poor precision (the same applies to a DAC's output). Signal conditioning scales and shifts the signal to fill the range, usually with an op-amp giving \(V_{out} = m V_{in} + b\).

The slides use TI's SLOA097 recipe for positive \(m\) and positive \(b\):

Figure 5.1: \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) mix \(V_{in}\) and \(V_{ref}\) at the non-inverting input; \(R_f\) and \(R_g\) set the non-inverting gain \(1 + R_f/R_g\). Other combinations (negative gain, negative offset) use different circuits and formulas in the same application note.

Gain and offset, positive m and b\[ R_2 = \frac{V_{ref}\, R_1\, m}{b} \qquad R_g = \frac{R_2\, R_f}{m\,(R_1 + R_2) - R_2} \]
\(R_1\)
Input resistor, chosen first (Ω).
\(R_2\)
Resistor from the reference (Ω).
\(V_{ref}\)
Reference voltage used for the offset (V).
\(m\)
Desired gain.
\(b\)
Desired offset (V).
\(R_f\)
Feedback resistor, chosen (Ω).
\(R_g\)
Gain resistor to ground (Ω).
Worked example 5.1 mapping 0 to 1 V onto 0.5 to 3.0 V

A sensor gives 0 to 1 V. Map it to 0.5 to 3.0 V for a 3.3 V ADC using \(V_{ref} = 3.3\) V, \(R_1 = R_f = 10\) kΩ.

1
\(m = (3.0 - 0.5)/(1 - 0) = 2.5\); \(b = 0.5\) V.
2
\(R_2 = 3.3 \times 10\text{ k} \times 2.5/0.5 = 165\) kΩ.
3
\(R_g = 165\text{ k} \times 10\text{ k}/(2.5 \times 175\text{ k} - 165\text{ k}) = 6.055\) kΩ.
4
Check: \(V_+ = V_{in}\,\frac{165}{175} + 3.3 \times \frac{10}{175}\); times \(1 + 10/6.055 = 2.652\) gives 0.5 V at \(V_{in} = 0\) and 3.0 V at \(V_{in} = 1\) V.
\(R_2 = 165\) kΩ, \(R_g = 6.055\) kΩ (use the nearest standard value and check the end points again).
Watch out

Not all op-amps are equal. A negative output needs a dual-rail supply, and outputs become non-linear near the supply rails, so design the gain and offset to keep the output away from them (which is why the example aims for 0.5 to 3.0 V, not 0 to 3.3 V). Read the datasheet and simulate in SPICE.

06ADC calibration

Reading Lecture 23, slides 21 to 24

Combine the ADC formula with the sensor's transfer function to get the measured quantity from \(R\). For the MCP9700, \(T_A = (V_{OUT} - V_{0°C})/T_C\):

MCP9700 through an ADC\[ T_A = \frac{R\,(V_{REF+} - V_{REF-})}{(2^N - 1)\,T_C} + \frac{V_{REF-} - V_{0°C}}{T_C} \]
\(T_A\)
Ambient temperature (°C).
\(R\)
ADC reading.
\(V_{REF+}, V_{REF-}\)
ADC references (V).
\(N\)
ADC resolution (bits).
\(T_C\)
Sensor temperature coefficient, 0.01 V/°C.
\(V_{0°C}\)
Sensor output at 0 °C, 0.5 V.

With 3.3 V, 0 V and 12 bits: \(T_A = 0.0806R - 50\).

Real measurements are also affected by things outside the ADC: \(V_{REF} = V_{DD}\) may not be exactly 3.3 V, resistors have tolerances, there is offset, and each sensor differs a little. Calibration finds the equation and parameters that relate \(R\) to the true quantity. Measuring \(V_{ADC}\) with a voltmeter only fixes half the chain. Better: record many accurate measurements of the quantity itself together with the ADC reading, over the whole range, and fit the model to them (least squares).

Figure 6.1: In the lecture's data the theoretical line (amber dashed) had a 5.15 °C (1σ) error. A first-order least-squares fit gave \(T_A = 0.06982R - 37.62854\) (teal), reducing the error to 1.23 °C (1σ). At \(R = 1200\) the two lines give 46.70 °C and 46.16 °C. A higher-order polynomial could do better still, and sometimes each device needs its own coefficients.

Exam

Expect to convert between \(R\) and voltage in both directions, to build a sensor equation like \(T_A = 0.0806R - 50\) from datasheet values, and to explain why and how a calibration fit improves on it.
