Pull-ups, transmission lines and system design

The electrical side of interfacing: how to choose I2C pull-up resistors between a minimum and a maximum, why differential signalling resists noise, and when a wire becomes a transmission line that needs termination. Then the engineering process for a whole embedded system: requirements, design alternatives, concept design and verification.

01I2C pull-up resistors

Reading Lecture 26, slides 3 to 14

The I2C lines are open drain (Week 7): devices can only pull them low, and a pull-up resistor \(R_P\) pulls them high. \(R_P\) has both a lower and an upper limit.

Minimum: sink current

When a device pulls the line low, current \(I = V_{DD}/R_P\) flows through its transistor. Too small an \(R_P\) and the port cannot sink enough current to bring the line down to a valid low, or is damaged (the same problem as push-pull contention). The limit comes from \(V_{OL(max)}\), the highest output voltage still recognised as a 0, at the rated sink current \(I_{OL}\):

Minimum pull-up\[ R_{P(min)} = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{OL(max)}}{I_{OL}} \]
\(R_{P(min)}\)
Smallest allowed pull-up (Ω).
\(V_{DD}\)
Bus supply (V).
\(V_{OL(max)}\)
Maximum low output voltage at the rated current (V).
\(I_{OL}\)
Low-level sink current (A).
Case\(V_{OL}\)\(I_{OL}\)\(R_{P(min)}\)
STM32F407 GPIO, normal0.4 V8 mA362.5 Ω (slide: 360 Ω)
STM32F407 GPIO, relaxed \(V_{OL}\)1.3 V20 mA100 Ω
I2C specification (standard and fast mode)0.4 V3 mA966.7 Ω (slide: 966 Ω)

The microcontroller coping with 100 Ω does not mean other devices on the bus will see 1.3 V as a low (their \(V_{IL}\) may be lower). Either calculate for every device and take the largest \(R_{P(min)}\), or design to the I2C specification, which all I2C devices must meet.

Why not always use about 1 kΩ? Because a smaller resistor draws more current whenever the line is low: more power, which matters on batteries. Use the largest value that still works.

Maximum: rise time

Wires and tracks have capacitance: wire capacitance, cross-channel capacitance between SDA and SCL, and every pin's input capacitance, together the bus capacitance \(C_B\). When the line is released, \(R_P\) charges \(C_B\) exponentially, so the rise takes time. If \(R_P\) is too large the line does not reach a logic 1 within the allowed rise time, and faster modes allow shorter rise times.

Rise of the line\[ V(t) = V_{DD}\left(1 - e^{-t/R_P C_B}\right) \qquad t_r = t_2 - t_1 = 0.8473\,R_P C_B \qquad R_{P(max)} = \frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_B} \]
\(V(t)\)
Line voltage after release (V).
\(C_B\)
Total bus capacitance (F).
\(t_r\)
Rise time from \(V_{IL} = 0.3V_{DD}\) to \(V_{IH} = 0.7V_{DD}\) (s). Specification maximum: 1000 ns standard mode, 300 ns fast mode.
\(t_1, t_2\)
Times at which the line passes \(0.3V_{DD}\) and \(0.7V_{DD}\) (s).
0.8473
\(\ln(0.7/0.3)\), from solving \(V(t)\) at the two thresholds.
\(R_{P(max)}\)
Largest allowed pull-up (Ω).

Figure 1.1: With 2.2 kΩ and 150 pF (\(RC = 330\) ns) the line crosses \(V_{IL}\) at \(t_1 = 0.3567RC\) and \(V_{IH}\) at \(t_2 = 1.2040RC\). The difference, 280 ns, is the rise time the specification limits.

The slides' example: standard mode, \(C_B = 200\) pF gives \(R_{P(max)} = 1000\text{ ns}/(0.8473 \times 200\text{ pF}) = 5.9\) kΩ. \(C_B\) is found by adding the input capacitance of every pin (from datasheets) and the PCB trace and wire capacitance. In practice the value is often found by trial and error, always checking the signal on an oscilloscope. Several pull-ups on one line act in parallel.

Figure 1.2: Any \(R_P\) between the dashed line (966 Ω, from the 3 mA sink current) and the curve for the chosen mode works. The fast-mode curve (amber) is 0.3 times the standard one (teal), so a heavily loaded fast-mode bus quickly runs out of room.

Worked example 1.1 choosing a fast-mode pull-up

A 3.3 V fast-mode bus (400 kHz) has \(C_B = 150\) pF. Find the allowed range of \(R_P\), choose a standard value, and find the current and power while the line is low.

1
\(R_{P(min)} = (3.3 - 0.4)/3\text{ mA} = 966.7\) Ω.
2
\(R_{P(max)} = 300\text{ ns}/(0.8473 \times 150\text{ pF}) = 2360\) Ω.
3
Choose 2.2 kΩ: \(t_r = 0.8473 \times 2200 \times 150\text{ pF} = 280\) ns, inside 300 ns.
4
Low: \(I = 3.3/2200 = 1.5\) mA, \(P = 3.3^2/2200 = 4.95\) mW per line.
966.7 Ω to 2360 Ω; 2.2 kΩ gives a 280 ns rise, 1.5 mA and 4.95 mW while low. Check it on a scope.

02Differential signalling

Reading Lecture 26, slides 15 to 19

Differential signalling sends each signal on two conductors: the signal and its inverse, balanced (equal and opposite about a common-mode voltage). The receiver responds only to the difference between them. It costs a second wire per signal, but:

  • Balanced signals have ideally no return current, so a common ground may not be needed.
  • Immunity to interference and crosstalk: noise couples equally into both conductors and cancels in the difference.
  • Less emitted interference: the two conductors radiate equal and opposite fields that largely cancel, provided they run close together (twisted pairs, adjacent PCB tracks).
  • Lower voltages: the difference swing is twice each wire's swing, doubling the signal-to-noise ratio for the same common-mode voltage, so lower levels can be used, giving less EMI, less power and higher frequencies.

Figure 2.1: Both wires (teal and amber) carry the same low-frequency noise, which on a single-ended wire could cross a threshold. Their difference (ink) is clean, with twice the swing of either wire.

Watch out

The two conductors must be the same length. If one is longer, its signal arrives later and the crossover point where the difference changes sign shifts, distorting the timing.

Microcontrollers need an external transceiver for differential signals. Examples: LVDS (Low Voltage Differential Signalling), RS-422 (differential UART), RS-485 (RS-422 with tri-state transmitters, so many devices can share two wires as a bus), Ethernet, CAN and USB.

03Transmission lines

Reading Lecture 26, slides 20 to 31

An ideal conductor has the same voltage everywhere at once. Real signals travel along conductors at close to the speed of light, so if a signal changes fast or the conductor is long, it must be modelled as a transmission line and the impedances at its ends matter.

Picture two infinitely long parallel wires with no resistance, switched onto a voltage. The insulation between them forms a distributed capacitance that must charge, and the current that charges it is limited by the wires' distributed inductance. A voltage and current wavefront travels down the line, charging each section as it passes. An infinite line draws a constant current, so it looks like a resistor: its characteristic impedance.

Characteristic impedance (lossless line)\[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
\(Z_0\)
Characteristic impedance (Ω).
\(L\)
Inductance per unit length (H/m).
\(C\)
Capacitance per unit length (F/m).

\(Z_0\) is not the wire's resistance (good lines have almost none); it is set by the geometry of the two conductors. Typical values: 50 or 75 Ω coaxial cable, about 100 Ω twisted pair. On a PCB it depends on trace width and thickness, the board material and the distance to the ground plane.

Reflections and critical length

Wherever the impedance changes along the way, part of the wave is reflected back. At the load:

Load reflection coefficient\[ \Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} \]
\(\Gamma\)
Fraction of the incident wave reflected (−1 to 1).
\(Z_L\)
Load impedance (Ω).
\(Z_0\)
Characteristic impedance of the line (Ω).

A matched load (\(Z_L = Z_0\)) gives \(\Gamma = 0\): no reflection. An open end (\(Z_L \to \infty\)) gives \(+1\), a short gives \(-1\). A digital input is close to an open circuit, so an unterminated line reflects almost everything.

Reflections distort the logic levels if the propagation delay along the conductor is more than about 20 % of the signal's rise or fall time. The wave travels at a fraction of the speed of light, the velocity factor (50 % to 75 %).

Critical length\[ \ell_{crit} \approx 0.2\, t_r\, v_f\, c \]
\(\ell_{crit}\)
Length above which reflections become a problem (m).
\(t_r\)
Signal rise (or fall) time (s).
\(v_f\)
Velocity factor, 0.5 to 0.75.
\(c\)
Speed of light, \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s.

Slide example: a 1 MHz signal with 30 ns rise time and 50 % velocity factor: \(0.2 \times 30\text{ ns} \times 1.5 \times 10^8 = 0.9\) m. It is the rise time that matters, not the signal frequency.

Termination

Fixes: lay out PCB traces to match the cable impedance, and terminate the line with a resistor.

Figure 3.1: (a) A resistor \(Z_0\) to ground at the receiver absorbs the wave, so nothing reflects. (b) A resistor \(Z_0\) in series at the driver (whose own impedance is near 0) halves the launched wave; the open receiver reflects it to full height, and the reflection is absorbed at the source.

Parallel (at receiver)Series (at driver)
Resistor\(Z_L = Z_0\) to ground\(Z_S = Z_0\) in series
PowerContinuous dissipation while the line is highNo DC dissipation
CatchDriver must supply \(V_{DD}/Z_0\)Points along the line see \(V_{DD}/2\), an illegal level, until the reflection returns; only the far end is clean
Exam

The lecture's summary is the checklist: calculate \(R_P\) from device data and the I2C specification and check with a scope; use differential signalling to reduce EMI susceptibility and emissions; treat conductors as transmission lines when edges are fast or conductors long; match impedances or terminate when needed.

04Microcontroller system design

Reading Lecture 27

An embedded system is a microprocessor or microcontroller with its software and supporting hardware, designed to perform a dedicated function: a digital watch, a medical imager, a satellite controller. Here it means the microcontroller, its peripherals, the supporting electronics and the software, built for one purpose, and that purpose is described by requirements.

Systems engineering is the multidisciplinary engineering and management process for this. On large, costly projects it is formal and paper-heavy, but every embedded project benefits from a trimmed-down version of its life cycle.

Figure 4.1: Down the left, requirements are refined into a design; at the bottom it is built; up the right, each level is integrated and verified against the specification on the same level (dashed arrows). The test for each requirement is planned on the way down, not after building.

Requirements

  • User requirements: what the user or customer (a client, your boss, yourself) wants. Often vague, a wish list, not necessarily measurable; the customer may not know the technical implications.
  • System requirements: the exact, unambiguous, testable description of the system, refined from the user requirements by assumptions, decisions and analysis (your engineering knowledge). The refinement is iterative: check back with the customer.

User requirements (beehive monitor): sense when the hive is moved or knocked over; monitor temperature inside; no external power; data "via the cloud"; notify the user within 1 minute of movement; log temperature to the cloud every hour; 1000 devices in 6 months at no more than R300 each.

System requirements (hive sensor unit, HSU): shall register accelerations over 0.1 g as movement; shall measure temperature to 1 °C; shall report movement with under 10 s latency; shall report temperature every hour with under 10 s latency and under 5 s drift; shall have a rechargeable battery and solar panel; shall use an STM32F4.

Design alternatives and concept

The designer chooses between alternatives by trade-off: cost, complexity, availability and development time. For the HSU data link:

OptionComponents per HSUMonthly subscriptionGateway (once-off)CoverageDevelopment time
Cellphone GPRSR200R15noneGoodHigh

Cellular needs a GPRS modem, SIM and subscription. SigFox is a proprietary low-power long-range network with low data rates, good urban coverage but maybe not remote. LoRa is also low-power wide-area; The Things Network is open, but coverage depends on gateways, so a remote site probably needs its own gateway with an internet connection.

Worked example 4.1 first-year cost per unit

Using the table, compare the cost per HSU over the first 12 months for 1000 units.

1

2
SigFox: \(250 + 12 \times 10 = \) R370.
3

Concept design identifies the components and their dependencies in a block diagram: each component is a block, each interface a line labelled with the signal type and peripheral, and arrows show the flow of data (not necessarily current).

Figure 4.2: The STM32F407 talks to the SigFox modem over UART, to a BME280 temperature and moisture sensor over I2C, and reads an analog accelerometer (0 to 1 g = 0 to 10 mV) through signal conditioning into its ADC. A 7.4 V LiPo battery, charged from a solar panel, feeds a 3.3 V regulator; the modem needs both 3.3 V and the raw 7.4 V. The antenna is passive.

Worked example 4.2 does the accelerometer need conditioning?

The accelerometer gives 10 mV/g. The requirement is to detect 0.1 g. Can a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC see it directly? What gain fills the ADC range at 1 g?

1
0.1 g gives 1 mV. One LSB is \(3.3/4095 = 0.806\) mV, so 0.1 g is barely more than 1 LSB: not reliably detectable.
2
Gain to map 10 mV onto 3.3 V: \(3.3/0.01 = 330\).
3
Then 0.1 g gives 0.33 V, \(\lfloor 4095 \times 0.33/3.3 \rfloor = 409\) counts.
A gain of about 330 is needed (signal conditioning, Week 9); 0.1 g then spans about 409 counts instead of 1.

Verification and testing

"I've tested it, it works" is not verification. Verification shows that a component or system complies with a requirement, specification or imposed condition. It uses four methods: inspection, demonstration, test and analysis. A requirements verification matrix records how each requirement will be verified, and is decided before implementation. This is why requirements must be exact.

A test must check the specifics of the requirement (1 °C, 0.1 g, 10 s). It lists the exact equipment, gives a procedure detailed enough for someone else to repeat, records the results, and interprets them to justify that the requirement is met.

Bad test: measure the 3.3 V supply; it reads 3.2 V; "it works".

Good test: the 3.3 V load is 100 + 5 + 5 = 110 mA. Power off; connect a 30 Ω, 500 mW load (3.3/30 = 110 mA, 0.363 W) to the stated terminals; connect the scope to the stated test points; power on; record minimum, maximum and average voltage; tabulate; conclude the supply holds 3.3 V at full load within 0.1 V peak-to-peak.

05Practical 9: debugging terminal

Reading CS245-P9.pdf

Practical 9 builds a text "debugger" sent over the USB Virtual COM Port to Tera Term: your name and student number, a tick count, the LED state toggled by the push button, the accelerometer's raw and scaled readings, and intensity bars for X, Y and Z. It starts from the Practical 7 memo, which scales the LIS3DSH's 16-bit readings to g with a ±2 g range:

LIS3DSH_ReadXYZ(a_Raw);
ax = 2 * ((float) a_Raw[0]) / 32768;     // raw 16384 gives 1.000 g
sprintf(buffer, "Linear accel SCALED x: %6.3f g ...", ax, ...);

The bars use %.*s, which prints only the first n characters of a string of #, with n = raw/1000 (up to 32); a negative reading fills the left bar, right-aligned with %32.*s, and a positive one the right bar with %-32.*s. Preparation: printf/sprintf formats for strings, signed and unsigned integers and floats.

Worked example 5.1 one line of the display

The X reading is −12 000. What scaled value and how many bar characters appear, and on which side?

1
\(a_x = 2 \times (-12\,000)/32\,768 = -0.732\) g.
2
Integer division: \(-12\,000/1000 = -12\), absolute value 12; the reading is negative, so negLxScaled = 12 and posLxScaled = 0.
"−0.732 g" with 12 # on the left (negative) bar and none on the right.

06Practice problems

Problem 6.1 heavy standard-mode bus

A standard-mode bus has \(C_B = 400\) pF. What is \(R_{P(max)}\)? Is 4.7 kΩ acceptable? What about two 4.7 kΩ pull-ups on the same line (one on each of two boards)?

1
\(R_{P(max)} = 1000\text{ ns}/(0.8473 \times 400\text{ pF}) = 2951\) Ω.
2
4.7 kΩ is too large. Two in parallel: 2.35 kΩ, inside 966.7 Ω to 2951 Ω.
2.95 kΩ maximum; one 4.7 kΩ fails, two in parallel (2.35 kΩ) work.
Problem 6.2 reflection coefficients

A 50 Ω line ends in 100 Ω, then in 25 Ω. Find \(\Gamma\) each time.

1
\((100 - 50)/(100 + 50) = 1/3\).
2
\((25 - 50)/(25 + 50) = -1/3\).
+0.333 (reflection in phase, overshoot) and −0.333 (inverted reflection, undershoot).
Problem 6.3 fast edges

An SPI clock has 5 ns edges on a PCB with velocity factor 0.6. Above what length should the trace be treated as a transmission line?

1
\(0.2 \times 5\text{ ns} \times 0.6 \times 3 \times 10^8 = 0.18\) m.
About 18 cm (a propagation delay of 1 ns, 20 % of the edge).
Problem 6.4 cost of parallel termination

A 3.3 V line is parallel-terminated with 50 Ω. What current and power while high?

1
\(I = 3.3/50 = 66\) mA; \(P = 3.3^2/50 = 0.218\) W.
66 mA, 218 mW: far beyond a GPIO pin's 8 to 20 mA, which is why series termination suits microcontroller outputs.
Problem 6.5 minimum pull-up from the MCU

Using the STM32F407's normal rating (0.4 V at 8 mA) on a 3.3 V bus, find \(R_{P(min)}\). Why might the I2C-spec value still be the one to use?

1
\((3.3 - 0.4)/0.008 = 362.5\) Ω.
362.5 Ω, but other devices on the bus may only sink 3 mA, so the bus minimum is the largest of all devices' minimums, 966.7 Ω by the specification.
