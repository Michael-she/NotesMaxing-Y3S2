Actuation, displays and external memory

How a low-current microcontroller switches real loads (transistors, relays, H-bridges) and drives motors; how displays from a single LED to an LCD-TFT panel are connected and refreshed; and how parallel NOR, NAND and SDRAM memories extend the microcontroller's memory. Lecture 23 (DAC) is also in this week's folder; it is covered on the Week 9 page.

01Switching loads

Reading Lecture 24, slides 2 to 8

Microcontrollers move robot arms, switch fans and heaters, control the speed of quadcopter motors and drive electromagnetic locks, usually with some feedback (position, speed, temperature). These loads need high current, and often a higher voltage, than a GPIO pin can supply. So the microcontroller outputs only on/off GPIO or PWM signals, and a power switch, motor driver or H-bridge does the work.

  • Low-side switch: between the load and ground.
  • High-side switch: between the supply \(V_{CC}\) and the load.

Figure 1.1: In both, the GPIO only controls the switch; the load current flows from \(V_{CC}\), not from the microcontroller. The low-side switch is easiest to build, but leaves the load's low end slightly above 0 V.

BJT switches

A BJT switch runs only in cut-off (off) and saturation (on). A low-side switch uses an NPN transistor; a high-side switch uses a PNP. To saturate it, the collector current must reach the load current and the base current must be at least \(I_{LOAD}/\beta\):

BJT switch design\[ I_{LOAD} = \frac{V_{CC}}{R_{LOAD}} \qquad I_B \ge \frac{I_{LOAD}}{\beta} \qquad R_B \lt \frac{V_{DD}\,\beta}{I_{LOAD}} \]
\(I_{LOAD}\)
Load current when on, equal to the collector current (A).
\(V_{CC}\)
Load supply voltage (V).
\(R_{LOAD}\)
Load resistance (Ω).
\(I_B\)
Base current from the GPIO pin (A).
\(\beta\)
Current gain of the transistor.
\(R_B\)
Base resistor (Ω).
\(V_{DD}\)
GPIO high voltage (V).

The slides' example: 1 A load (10 V, 10 Ω), \(\beta = 100\): \(I_B \ge 10\) mA and, with 3.3 V, \(R_B \lt 330\) Ω. Power BJTs rarely have \(\beta\) above 50, so with a 20 mA GPIO limit the largest switchable load is about 1 A. For more, use a Darlington pair. BJTs also dissipate power because of \(V_{CE(sat)}\).

Watch out

The slide formula \(I_B = V_{DD}/R_B\) ignores the 0.7 V base-emitter drop. Including it, \(I_B = (V_{DD} - V_{BE})/R_B\), which gives a smaller limit on \(R_B\) (260 Ω instead of 330 Ω for 10 mA from 3.3 V). Design with the drop included and leave margin, since \(\beta\) varies between transistors.

MOSFET switches

  • Switched by the gate-source voltage \(V_{GS}\), not by current: almost no current flows from the GPIO pin, so much larger currents can be switched.
  • Low-side uses an n-channel, high-side a p-channel MOSFET.
  • Instead of a saturation voltage they have an on-resistance \(R_{DS}\) that depends on \(V_{GS}\); power MOSFETs dissipate far less than BJTs.
  • The gate-source capacitance must be charged by the small GPIO current, so switching takes time. This can be a problem with fast PWM.
Watch out

Slide 6 says the MOSFET's "switching time is lower than with BJT". The point being made is the opposite: the gate charges slowly from a GPIO pin, so switching is slower (the switching time is longer). Read it as "switching speed is lower".

Low-side or high-side? A low-side switch leaves \(V_{CE(sat)}\) or \(V_{DS}\) across the transistor, so the load's low terminal is not at 0 V. If that matters, use a high-side switch (PNP or p-channel). The logic is inverted for a high-side p-channel switch, and the load supply cannot be higher than the GPIO voltage, or the switch never turns off: then an extra driver stage is needed.

Relays

A relay has physical contacts moved by an electromagnet, so it can switch almost any current and voltage, for example 220 V AC. The microcontroller controls the coil, still through a transistor switch. A flyback diode across the coil is essential: switching off an inductor's current causes a voltage spike that can arc or create EM noise, and the diode gives the coil current a path to die away. Latching relays only need current to change state. Relays wear out and cannot be used with PWM.

Figure 1.2: The diode is reverse biased while the coil is on. When the transistor turns off, the coil's current continues through the diode instead of forcing the collector voltage far above 12 V.

Worked example 1.1 BJT or MOSFET for a 0.5 A load

A 24 Ω heater on 12 V is switched from a 3.3 V GPIO. (a) With a BJT of \(\beta = 50\), find \(I_B\) and \(R_B\). (b) Compare the switch dissipation for \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0.2\) V with a MOSFET of \(R_{DS(on)} = 0.05\) Ω.

1
\(I_{LOAD} = 12/24 = 0.5\) A; \(I_B \ge 0.5/50 = 10\) mA.
2
Slide formula: \(R_B \lt 3.3/0.01 = 330\) Ω. With \(V_{BE}\): \(R_B \lt 2.6/0.01 = 260\) Ω. Choose 220 Ω: \(I_B = 2.6/220 = 11.8\) mA, below the 20 mA pin limit.
3
BJT: \(P = 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1\) W. MOSFET: \(P = 0.5^2 \times 0.05 = 0.0125\) W.
\(R_B = 220\) Ω gives 11.8 mA. The MOSFET dissipates 12.5 mW against 100 mW for the BJT, and draws no steady gate current.

02H-bridges and motors

Reading Lecture 24, slides 9 to 19

An H-bridge is four switches around a load so current can flow through it in either direction, which motor control needs. It is built from BJTs or MOSFETs (NPN with PNP, or n- with p-channel), and is available as ICs, either just the transistors and diodes or a full motor driver.

Figure 2.1: A and C connect the motor's ends to \(V_{motor}\); B and D connect them to ground. Closing A and D drives current left to right (amber); closing B and C reverses it. Closing both switches of one side (A with B, or C with D) shorts the supply: "smoke comes out".

Brushed DC motors

Permanent magnets in the housing form the stator; the rotor (armature) carries windings and turns the shaft. The terminals connect to stationary brushes that touch the commutator on the rotor. Current in the windings makes a field that turns the rotor towards alignment; just before it gets there, the commutator switches to the next winding and reverses the field, so the rotor keeps turning. Voltage sets speed or torque; reversing the voltage reverses the direction.

ABCDResult
ClosedOpenOpenClosedSpin in one direction
OpenClosedClosedOpenSpin in the opposite direction
OpenOpenOpenOpenRotor coasts
ClosedOpenClosedOpenActive brake (both ends to \(V_{motor}\))
OpenClosedOpenClosedActive brake (both ends to ground)
Watch out

On slide 14 the first braking row reads A, C and D closed. With C and D both closed the right-hand leg shorts the supply. The highlighted cells (A and C only) show the intended state: A and C closed, B and D open, as in the table above.

Protection diodes across each switch do the job of a flyback diode when the current is switched off or reversed. The microcontroller drives A to D through GPIO, PWM or a motor driver IC. PWM gives variable speed, acceleration and torque: the motor's coils are inductors and smooth the pulses. Motors need their own supply so that their noise, glitches and back EMF do not disturb the microcontroller, and they usually run at a higher voltage.

Brushless, servo and stepper motors

  • Brushless DC (BLDC): magnets on the rotor, coils on the stator, and commutation done electronically. The driver must know the rotor's position, from Hall-effect sensors, to know which coil to power next. More efficient but harder to drive: one half-bridge per phase (switches SW1 to SW6), GPIO/PWM outputs and Hall-sensor inputs with interrupts. It is timing-intensive, so often a dedicated microcontroller or BLDC driver IC does it.
  • Feedback: Hall sensors give coarse speed, which can be integrated to estimate angle. For accurate angle and rate use an optical encoder read through GPIO external interrupts (linear encoder strips do the same for linear motion).
  • Servo: DC motor, driver electronics, gearbox and shaft encoder in one unit, often with limited rotation (RC aircraft flaps, RC car steering).
  • Stepper: a brushless motor that moves in discrete steps as pulses are applied to alternate inputs. Precise position control and high torque; driving two phases at once increases torque. Driven through H-bridges (probably without PWM) or a dedicated stepper controller, often with a gearbox.

Servo signal timing with a timer is worked on the Week 6 page (Problem 4.4).

Strip LEDs

The slide names the part WS2818B; the common 24-bit strip LED is the WS2812B. The bit encoding described is the same.

LCD-TFT and frame buffers

An "LED TV" is still an LCD with an LED backlight; OLED is different, with each pixel an organic LED and no backlight. For an LCD-TFT the MCU computes the image (text, sprites, background) and writes it into a frame buffer (graphics RAM, GRAM). A display controller continuously refreshes the panel from the frame buffer. The frame buffer and controller can be in the display, both in the MCU, or the controller in the MCU with the frame buffer in external RAM (a GPU makes it more complex still).

Frame buffer size and colours\[ \text{size} \ge \frac{W \times H \times \text{bpp}}{8} \text{ bytes} \qquad n_{colours} = 2^{\text{bpp}} \]
size
Frame buffer memory for one frame (bytes).
\(W, H\)
Horizontal and vertical resolution (pixels).
bpp
Colour depth, bits per pixel. 24 bpp is 8 bits each of R, G, B.
\(n_{colours}\)
Number of colours a pixel can show; 16 777 216 at 24 bpp.

The refresh rate is usually 60 Hz or more; slower and the display flickers.

ST's LTDC (LCD-TFT Display Controller) peripheral works like a DMA: it fetches pixel data from memory over AHB in the background and writes it to a parallel display interface. It can fetch two rectangular layers and blend them. Its signals:

  • VSYNC: pulses once per frame. HSYNC: once per line.
  • PCLK (DOTCLK): one cycle per pixel.
  • DE (data enable): high when RGB data is valid. The clock periods with DE low around each line are the back porch and front porch.

Figure 3.1: After the HSYNC pulse the pixel clock keeps running through the back porch, then DE goes high for the active pixels, then low again for the front porch. Porches are why the pixel clock must be a little faster than \(W \times H \times f_{refresh}\).

Double buffering: tearing and flicker happen when the CPU draws into the memory that is being displayed. One fix is to wait for VSYNC and draw only on parts already refreshed. The usual fix is two frame buffers: the controller displays the front buffer while the CPU (or the DMA2D graphics accelerator) draws the back buffer, and at the end of a refresh they swap. Other display signals: a reset line (GPIO), I2C or SPI for touch, brightness and contrast, and a backlight enable (GPIO).

Which STM32

The LTDC and DMA2D are on larger STM32 parts such as the F429; the course board's STM32F407 does not have them, but has the FSMC memory controller (section 4). The slides cite ST application note AN4861.

Worked example 3.1 frame buffer for a small TFT

A 480 × 272 panel uses 16 bpp at 60 Hz. Find the frame buffer size, the double-buffered size, the number of colours and the minimum pixel rate.

1
\(480 \times 272 \times 16/8 = 261\,120\) bytes (255 KiB).
2
Double buffered: \(522\,240\) bytes.
3
\(2^{16} = 65\,536\) colours.
4
Active pixels per second: \(480 \times 272 \times 60 = 7.83\) MHz; porches push the pixel clock higher.
261 120 B per frame, 522 240 B double-buffered: more than the STM32F407's on-chip SRAM, which is exactly the case for an external SDRAM frame buffer.

04External memory

Reading Lecture 25, slides 15 to 27

Usually the MCU's own SRAM and flash are enough (choose a variant that has enough). External memory is needed for data files that will not fit in flash, data logging, an SDRAM frame buffer for double buffering, camera images or video, programs or data that must be erased and written separately from the main program, and FPGAs connected through a memory-mapped interface. SD cards and USB use serial interfaces (Week 8); this lecture covers permanent parallel memories: NOR flash, NAND flash and SDRAM. They need an external memory controller peripheral (ST's FSMC/FMC, Silicon Labs' EBI), found mostly on larger devices because the parallel buses need many pins.

NOR and NAND flash

Flash stores bits in floating-gate transistor cells.

NOR flashNAND flash
Cell arrangementCells in parallel, each between the source line and a bit line, like a NOR gateAbout 8 cells in series, like a NAND gate
AccessRandom access through parallel address linesNo random access; I/O-mapped (indirect) interface
SpeedFast readSlower read, faster write and erase
DensityLowerHigher; made with imperfections, so needs ECC
Used forProgram codeMass storage

NAND interface (legacy, asynchronous): an 8-bit bidirectional data bus DQ[7:0] carries commands, addresses and data. CE# selects the chip; ALE and CLE say whether a bus cycle is an address or a command; RE# and WE# strobe reads and writes (on their rising edges); WP# blocks programming and erasing; RY/BY# shows the device is busy. ONFI and Toggle NAND add signals for more throughput.

NOR interface: the same as asynchronous SRAM, with parallel address and data buses (8, 16 or 32 bits wide) so any word can be read directly. Some parts multiplex the low address bits onto the data bus and need an address-latch signal.

NOR address lines\[ 2^N = \frac{\text{capacity (bits)}}{\text{data bus width (bits)}} \]
\(N\)
Number of address lines.
capacity
Total memory size in bits.
data bus width
Bits per word, 8, 16 or 32.

Figure 4.1: The address bus (amber) and control lines go out from the MCU; the data bus (teal) goes both ways; RY/BY# comes back. To read: MCU drives the address, CE# and OE# active, WE# inactive, and the memory drives the data. To write: address out, CE# and WE# active, OE# inactive, and the MCU drives the data. No clock is passed to the memory. RESET#, WP# and BYTE# (8 or 16-bit mode) complete the interface.

SDRAM

SDRAM stores each bit as charge on a capacitor, in a matrix of rows and columns, sometimes in several banks. To save pins, row and column addresses share the same address lines: RAS (Row Address Strobe) and CAS (Column Address Strobe) say which is on the lines. Opening a row takes time, but once open, many columns in it can be read quickly, so burst accesses keep the row and step the column. SDRAM is synchronous (it has a clock), and timing is crucial: CAS latency is the clock cycles from column address to data, and the read cycle time is the time between successive reads to an open row. All of these are set in the memory controller's registers from the datasheet.

Programming: set up the FMC registers (memory type, number of address and data lines, timings, device commands), using HAL and the device configuration tool. After that the external memory is used through ordinary memory reads and writes at its mapped addresses.

05Practical 8: audio synthesiser

Reading CS245-P8.pdf

The Discovery board's CS43L22 audio DAC is connected to the I2S3 peripheral (shared with SPI3) for samples, and set up over I2C for data format (16, 24 or 32-bit), volume, bass, treble and filtering; ST's libraries do the setup. The practical uses 16-bit, 44.1 kHz audio (audio, PCM and I2S are on the Week 9 page). Its goal: generate a 1 kHz tone and view it on a scope, amplitude-modulate it with the accelerometer, then step the tone up in 1 kHz increments with the push button.

Figure 5.1: One period needs 44.1 samples, which is not a whole number, so the look-up table uses 441 samples: exactly 10 periods of 1 kHz, and a whole number of periods of every harmonic (2 kHz gives 20, 3 kHz 30). With left and right interleaved the buffer is 441 × 2 = 882 16-bit values (1764 bytes), and circular DMA sends it 100 times a second, paced by the I2S requests.

06Practice problems

Problem 6.1 multiplexed 7-segment display

A 4-digit 7-segment display is multiplexed. How many GPIO pins, against driving each segment directly? If the whole display refreshes at 100 Hz, how long is each digit on per cycle?

1
Multiplexed: 7 segment lines + 4 digit enables = 11 pins. Direct: \(4 \times 7 = 28\).
2
Period 10 ms shared by 4 digits: 2.5 ms each.
11 pins instead of 28; each digit lit for 2.5 ms of every 10 ms.
Problem 6.2 RGB strip data

A strip has 60 LEDs. How many PWM periods (compare values in the DMA array) does one update need? If each bit takes 1.25 µs (a typical figure; check the datasheet), how long does the update take?

1
\(60 \times 24 = 1440\) bits, one PWM period each.
2
\(1440 \times 1.25\) µs \(= 1.8\) ms.
1440 compare values; 1.8 ms per update.
Problem 6.3 address lines

How many address lines do a 16 Mbit NOR flash with a 16-bit bus and a 64 Mbit one with an 8-bit bus need?

1
\(16 \times 2^{20}/16 = 2^{20}\) words: \(N = 20\).
2
\(64 \times 2^{20}/8 = 2^{23}\): \(N = 23\).
20 and 23 address lines.
Problem 6.4 24-bit display

An 800 × 480 display uses 24 bpp. How big is one frame, and how many colours?

1
\(800 \times 480 \times 3 = 1\,152\,000\) bytes.
2
\(2^{24} = 16\,777\,216\) colours.
1 152 000 bytes per frame; 16 777 216 colours.
Problem 6.5 H-bridge states

The motor spins forward with A and D closed. Which switches give reverse, coast and brake, and which combination must never happen?

1
Reverse: B and C. Coast: all open. Brake: A and C, or B and D.
2
Never A with B, or C with D: that shorts \(V_{motor}\) to ground.
Reverse B+C; coast none; brake A+C or B+D; never A+B or C+D. Add a dead time between turning one switch off and its partner on (Week 6, advanced timers).
Problem 6.6 stepper positioning

A stepper has 200 steps per revolution. How many steps turn it 90°, and what speed does 400 steps/s give?

1
Step angle \(360/200 = 1.8°\); \(90/1.8 = 50\) steps.
2
\(400/200 = 2\) rev/s = 120 rpm.
50 steps; 120 rpm.
