SPI, SD cards and direct memory access

The third standard serial link, SPI, from its shift-register core to clock modes and slave selection; how an SD card is driven over SPI and organised into a file system; and how the DMA controller moves data between peripherals and memory so the CPU does not have to.

01SPI

Reading Lecture 19, slides 3 to 12

The Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) is a four-wire, full-duplex, synchronous serial bus designed by Motorola in the 1980s. It is built from simple shift registers.

SignalNameDriven by
SCKSerial clockMaster
MOSIMaster Out, Slave InMaster
MISOMaster In, Slave OutSelected slave
SS (or CS)Slave Select, active lowMaster

The master controls the clock. To write, it puts data on MOSI. If the slave must reply, the master keeps clocking and the slave puts data on MISO. Every clock moves one bit each way at the same time, so SPI is full-duplex: after 8 clocks the master and slave have exchanged a byte.

Figure 1.1: On each clock the master shifts one bit out on MOSI (teal) into the slave, and the slave shifts one bit out on MISO (amber) into the master. After eight clocks the two registers have swapped contents. To read only, the master sends dummy bytes; to write only, it ignores what comes back.

Multiple slaves and daisy chains

Each slave has its own SS line, which acts as its enable. SS is active low (0 V = selected). SCK, MOSI and MISO are shared. The SS lines can come from the SPI peripheral or from ordinary GPIO pins.

Figure 1.2: Every slave sees the clock and MOSI, but only the selected one drives MISO (amber). Selecting two at once is bus contention: both drive MISO. With \(n\) slaves the master needs \(3 + n\) signals.

SPI signal count\[ N_{signals} = 3 + n \qquad N_{signals,\,decoder} = 3 + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil \]
\(N_{signals}\)
Number of master pins used by the bus.
\(n\)
Number of slaves.
\(N_{signals,\,decoder}\)
Pins needed when a decoder drives the SS lines from a binary slave number.

For many slaves a decoder (for example 3-to-8) turns a few pins into many SS lines, and also guarantees only one slave is selected at a time. The decoder form is not on the slides as a formula; it follows from a \(k\)-to-\(2^k\) decoder.

In a daisy chain, each slave's data out (DOUT) goes to the next slave's data in (DIN). One SS and one clock go to all of them. Data ripples through: the first byte sent ends up in the last slave. With 3 slaves the master clocks 24 times to get a byte to the last one. The slaves must support this, and it is mostly used for output-only chains such as addressable LED strips.

Figure 1.3: The chain behaves as one long shift register of 24 bits. When SS rises, each slave latches the byte now sitting in it.

Implementation and speed

  • No frame overhead: no start or stop bits, just a continuous bit stream, always in multiples of 8 bits.
  • The shift register may be 8, 16 or 32 bits; how many bytes are sent depends on the application.
  • Bit order (MSB or LSB first) is configurable: check the slave's datasheet.
  • Voltage levels depend on the device, typically TTL levels.
  • Speeds up to 100 MHz are possible; 16 MHz is typical. The master sets the speed; the slave usually states a maximum clock rate, not one fixed value.
  • At high rates, transmission-line effects matter (Week 11).

Clock polarity and phase

CPOL (clock polarity) sets the level of SCK when idle. CPHA (clock phase) sets which edge samples the data: CPHA = 0 samples on the first edge after SS falls, CPHA = 1 on the second. The slave's datasheet decides the mode, and the master's SPI peripheral is set to match.

ModeCPOLCPHAIdle clockSample onShift out on
000LowRising edgeFalling edge
101LowFalling edgeRising edge
210HighFalling edgeRising edge
311HighRising edgeFalling edge
Watch out

The table on slide 8 lists mode 2 as CPOL = 1, CPHA = 1 and mode 3 as CPOL = 1, CPHA = 0, while its edge descriptions match the usual numbering above. The table here uses the standard numbering (also used by STM32 and most datasheets): mode = 2·CPOL + CPHA. If an exam question gives CPOL and CPHA, work from those two bits rather than the mode number.

Figure 1.4: nCS falls to select the slave. The clock idles low (CPOL = 0). Data is set up on the falling edges and sampled on the rising edges (CPHA = 0, dashed lines). MOSI carries 0x3C = 0011 1100 and MISO 0xD2 = 1101 0010, both MSB first. Outside the transfer, MISO is high-impedance.

Programming SPI

  • RXNE interrupt: data has arrived in the receive data register; read DR to get it.
  • TXE interrupt: DR has moved into the output shift register; it is safe to write more.
  • SS control: software (configure a GPIO output and drive it low yourself) or hardware (the SPI peripheral drives SS).
  • DMA removes the per-byte interrupts entirely (section 3).

As with I2C, STM32CubeMX configures pins and mode, and HAL offers blocking, interrupt and DMA functions. The ST LIS3DSH 3-axis accelerometer on the Discovery board is an example slave; on that board it can only be used in SPI mode.

02SD cards

Reading Lecture 19, slides 13 to 28

An SD (Secure Digital) card is non-volatile flash memory, standardised by the SD Association since January 2000 in standard, mini and micro sizes.

Capacity classSize
SDSC (Standard)up to 2 GB
SDHC (High)over 2 GB to 32 GB
SDXC (Extended)over 32 GB to 2 TB
SDUC (Ultra)over 2 TB to 128 TB
Bus speedRate
Standard12.5 MB/s
High-speed25 MB/s
UHS-I50 or 104 MB/s
UHS-II156 MB/s full-duplex, 312 MB/s half
UHS-III312 MB/s full-duplex, 624 MB/s half
Express≥ 985 MB/s full-duplex

A card can be used through its native SD interface (4 parallel bidirectional data lines, faster, needs a dedicated host peripheral) or through SPI, which every card supports. The SPI signals share pins with the native interface, and commands put the card in one mode or the other. SPI is slower but works with any microcontroller that has SPI, which matters because not every microcontroller has an SD host controller. The command set and read/write sequences are fairly complicated (ST application note AN5595 covers SPI mode).

Besides its memory, a card has registers that can be queried: the CSD (Card Specific Data) and the CID (Card Identification Data, with manufacturer information). Others exist, but the card can be used without them.

Blocks and file systems

The card's memory is a flat array of 512-byte blocks. Low-level reads and writes just name an address and a length: "read 512 bytes from address 1024" or "write 1024 bytes from address 2048". Files and folders are a file system built on top of this.

A file spans several blocks, so a structure is needed to say where each file starts and ends, plus its name and date. The slides' example record:

uint32_t Date_created;   // 4 bytes
char     Filename[12];   // 12 bytes
uint32_t Start_address;  // 4 bytes
uint32_t Size;           // 4 bytes

These records are kept in a reserved area at the start of the card, the File Allocation Table (FAT). FAT16 and FAT32 are common on SD cards. ST provides the FatFs library to handle it.

Figure 2.1: The program only calls FatFs (f_open, f_read, f_write). FatFs calls a small SD-card library that turns block reads and writes into SD commands, which in turn calls the HAL SPI functions. Each layer hides the one below.

Worked example 2.1 block arithmetic

Using the record above: how many records fit in one block, how many blocks does a 1300-byte file occupy, and how many blocks does a 32 GB SDHC card have (taking 1 GB = 230 bytes)?

1
Record size \(4 + 12 + 4 + 4 = 24\) bytes; \(\lfloor 512/24 \rfloor = 21\) records per block.
2
\(\lceil 1300/512 \rceil = 3\) blocks (the last one partly empty).
3
\(32 \times 2^{30} / 512 = 67\,108\,864\) blocks.
21 records per block, 3 blocks for the file, 67 108 864 blocks on the card.
Worked example 2.2 time to write a block over SPI

How long does the data of one block take at a 16 MHz SPI clock?

1
\(512 \times 8 = 4096\) bits.
2
\(4096 / 16 \times 10^{6} = 256\) µs.
256 µs for the data alone, plus command and response bytes and the card's internal write time.

03Direct memory access

Reading Lecture 20

Microcontroller programs spend much of their time moving data: sampling an accelerometer or ADC into an array in SRAM, then writing that array to an SD card. The loop is always the same: read from address A0 (a peripheral register is just a memory address), write to address A1, maybe increment A0 or A1, repeat until the buffer is full.

The HAL call HAL_SPI_Transmit(&hspi1, buff, 512, 10) is such a loop: for each of 512 bytes it waits for TXE, then writes buffer[i] to DR. The source address increments; the destination (DR) stays fixed. It is a blocking function: it only returns once every byte has gone, and the waiting time is set by the data rate.

Key idea

A DMA controller is a unit in the MCU that moves data without the CPU. The CPU only sets the transfer up; during the transfer it is free for other work. Interrupts help a little (the CPU runs other code between bytes) but still need the CPU for every byte; DMA does not.

Watch out

Slide 4 says 100 kB at 11 520 B/s takes 8.9 s and at 1 000 000 B/s takes 102 ms. Both use 1 kB = 1024 bytes (102 400 bytes). With 100 000 bytes the UART time is 8.68 s.

How a DMA transfer works

The CPU programs the DMA controller through memory-mapped registers:

RegisterHolds
PARPeripheral address (for example a UART's DR)
M0ARMemory address (the buffer)
NDTRNumber of data items still to transfer
CRControl: DIR (00 peripheral-to-memory, 01 memory-to-peripheral, 10 memory-to-memory, where PAR is also a memory address), PSIZE and MSIZE (item size), PINC and MINC (increment the address after each item), circular mode, CHSEL, and EN to start

For each item the controller loads from the source and stores to the destination (direction from DIR), decrements NDTR, and optionally increments PAR and M0AR. The transfer stops when NDTR reaches zero, or in circular mode restarts with the original settings.

Figure 3.1: After setup (dashed) the CPU is free. Each DMA request from the peripheral (amber) moves one item between the peripheral's data register and the buffer in SRAM (teal). When NDTR reaches half or zero the stream interrupts the CPU.

DMA on the STM32F4

  • Two DMA controllers, each with 8 streams, so up to 8 active transfers per controller.
  • Each controller's memory and peripheral ports (AHB master ports) do one transfer at a time; an arbiter shares them between streams by priority.
  • DMA1 cannot do memory-to-memory: its peripheral port is not connected to flash or SRAM through the bus matrix. Use DMA2 for that.
  • Each stream has a FIFO; transfers can be bursts of 4, 8 or 16 beats, during which the AHB bus is locked.
  • Double-buffer mode: in circular mode the stream switches between M0AR and M1AR.

DMA requests. Without the CPU, what replaces "wait for TXE"? Each transfer is throttled by a DMA request from a peripheral event. The CHSEL bits in CR choose which request drives the stream; the reference manual has the mapping table. For example DMA1, stream 3, CHSEL = 0002 is SPI2 receive: one item moves when DMA is enabled in SPI2 and RXNE becomes 1. Timer events can also be requests. You could map a stream reading USART1's DR to the I2C1_TX request, but it would make no sense.

Memory-to-memory transfers need no request and run as fast as possible: a non-blocking memcpy.

DMA with HAL and double buffering

Rather than writing DMA registers, use the _DMA version of a peripheral's HAL function, after enabling DMA for that peripheral in the device configuration tool:

HAL_SPI_TransmitReceive_DMA(SPI_HandleTypeDef *hspi, uint8_t *pTxData, uint8_t *pRxData, uint16_t Size);
HAL_UART_Transmit_DMA(UART_HandleTypeDef *huart, uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size);
HAL_UART_Receive_DMA(UART_HandleTypeDef *huart, uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size);

The program learns about progress through interrupts: half-complete (NDTR at half its initial value) and complete (NDTR zero), plus error and FIFO interrupts. HAL turns these into callbacks you write, such as HAL_SPI_RxHalfCpltCallback and HAL_SPI_RxCpltCallback (and the Tx versions).

Double buffering uses a circular transfer over an array twice the block size (or the DMA's own double-buffer feature). While DMA fills one half, the CPU works on the other.

Figure 3.2: For input (slide scenario 1, averaging ADC samples): at half-complete the first half is full and safe to read while DMA writes the second. For output (scenario 2, a synthesiser feeding I2S): at half-complete the first half has been sent and the CPU refills it while DMA reads the second. Either way the CPU's work on one half must finish before DMA comes back round to it.

Worked example 3.1 deadline for double buffering

An ADC samples at 10 kHz into a circular DMA buffer of 2048 elements. How often do callbacks arrive, and how long does the CPU have to process each half?

1
Each half is 1024 samples: \(1024 / 10\,000 = 102.4\) ms to fill.
2
Callbacks alternate half-complete, complete, every 102.4 ms.
A callback every 102.4 ms; processing a half must take less than 102.4 ms or DMA overwrites data still in use.
Exam

Know the four registers (PAR, M0AR, NDTR, CR) and the DIR codes, what a DMA request is and why it is needed, the STM32F4 limits (2 controllers, 8 streams, DMA1 no memory-to-memory), and the double-buffering procedure with half and full callbacks.

04Practical 6: UART with HAL

Reading Practical 6 lecture

Practical 6 sends and receives UART data with the blocking HAL calls, looks at the TX pin on an oscilloscope, and then talks to a PC through the Discovery board's USB Virtual COM Port and the Termite terminal (Tera Term is used in Practical 9). The UART background is on the Week 7 page.

HAL_StatusTypeDef HAL_UART_Transmit(UART_HandleTypeDef *huart, const uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size, uint32_t Timeout);
HAL_StatusTypeDef HAL_UART_Receive (UART_HandleTypeDef *huart, uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size, uint32_t Timeout);

Size is in bytes and Timeout in milliseconds. Both block until done or timed out; the _IT and _DMA versions return at once.

05Practice problems

Problem 5.1 pin count

A master drives 5 SPI slaves with separate SS lines. How many pins does it need? How many with a 3-to-8 decoder for the selects?

1
\(3 + 5 = 8\) pins.
2
A 3-to-8 decoder needs 3 select inputs: \(3 + 3 = 6\) pins, for up to 8 slaves.
8 pins direct, 6 with the decoder (which also stops two slaves being selected at once).
Problem 5.2 daisy chain clocks

Four 8-bit LED drivers are daisy-chained. How many clocks load all four, and which byte ends up in the first driver?

1
The chain is \(4 \times 8 = 32\) bits long: 32 clocks.
2
The first byte sent travels furthest, to driver 4; the last byte sent stays in driver 1.
32 clocks; the fourth (last) byte sent is in driver 1.
Problem 5.3 blocking cost

A 168 MHz program sends 32 KiB (32 768 bytes) with blocking calls. How long does it block at 921 600 baud 8N1 UART, and at a 21 MHz SPI clock? How many CPU cycles are lost in the UART case?

1
UART: \(921\,600/10 = 92\,160\) B/s; \(32\,768/92\,160 = 0.356\) s.
2
SPI: \(32\,768 \times 8/21 \times 10^6 = 12.48\) ms.
3
Cycles: \(0.356 \times 168 \times 10^6 = 59.7\) million.
0.356 s (UART), 12.48 ms (SPI); about 59.7 million wasted cycles that DMA would give back.
Problem 5.4 setting up a DMA stream

Received SPI2 bytes must go into uint8_t rx[256]. Give DIR, PAR, M0AR, NDTR, PINC, MINC and the request, and the value of NDTR at the half-complete interrupt.

1
Peripheral to memory: DIR = 00. PAR = address of SPI2's DR; M0AR = address of rx.
2
NDTR = 256, byte sizes. PINC = 0 (DR is fixed), MINC = 1 (step through the array).
3
Request: SPI2 RX (DMA1 stream 3, CHSEL = 0002), with DMA enabled in SPI2.
DIR 00, PAR = &SPI2->DR, M0AR = rx, NDTR = 256, PINC 0, MINC 1; half-complete fires at NDTR = 128.
Problem 5.5 SPI exchange

In mode 0, the master's shift register holds 0x3C and the slave's 0xD2. What does each hold after 8 clocks, and what level is on MOSI and MISO for the first bit (MSB first)?

1
The registers swap: master 0xD2, slave 0x3C.
2
MSBs: 0x3C = 0011 1100 gives MOSI = 0; 0xD2 = 1101 0010 gives MISO = 1.
Master 0xD2, slave 0x3C; first bit MOSI 0, MISO 1.
