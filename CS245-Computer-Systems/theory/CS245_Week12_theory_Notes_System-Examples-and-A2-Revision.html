COMPUTER SYSTEMS 245 · LECTURE 28 · REVISION LECTURE A2

System examples and A2 revision

Two real microcontroller systems that combine everything in the course (a remotely controlled geyser and a satellite horizon/sun sensor), worked through number by number, followed by the A2 revision topics from the first half of the module: bit manipulation, pointers, the stack, interrupts and volatile, with a map of where the rest is covered.

01Example: smart geyser controller

Reading Lecture 28, slides 2 to 11

A normal geyser (hot water cylinder, HWC) switches its element on whenever the water falls below a set temperature. The smart controller adds remote control of the temperature and heating schedule, and an internet portal showing power and water use.

Figure 1.1: Analog inputs (teal) are the two MCP9700A temperature sensors and the current transformer through power conditioning; the flow meter feeds a timer input and the drip detector a GPIO. GPIO outputs (amber) drive the latching relay that switches the 220 V element and the H-bridge for the water valve. The 3G modem with its SIM and antenna is the only link to the outside world, over UART.

Software requirements (the "shall" list of Week 11):

  • Talk to the server only through the 3G modem, over UART. Every minute send water flow, temperatures and power.
  • The server replies with the set-point (0 °C when the schedule says not to heat, otherwise the user's value), and may send other commands: close the valve, start an over-the-air (OTA) update. The MCU must be able to reprogram itself in the field.
  • Control the temperature with hysteresis: element on if temperature < set-point − threshold, off if temperature > set-point + threshold.
  • Read temperatures with the ADC, flow with GPIO (a timer), element current with a current transformer and the ADC; detect drips (leaks or bursts) and close the valve.

The MCU is an Atmel ATxmega128A4U: 16-bit, 128 KB flash, 8 KB SRAM, 2 KB EEPROM, 32 MHz internal RC oscillator, 5 UARTs and 5 16-bit timers; developed in Atmel Studio and programmed with an Atmel-ICE.

Figure 1.2: Set-point 60 °C, threshold 3 °C, with illustrative heating (0.4 °C/min) and cooling (0.12 °C/min) rates. The element (ink trace at the bottom) turns on only below 57 °C and off only above 63 °C, so it switches a few times an hour instead of every time the temperature crosses 60 °C. That matters for a relay that wears out (Week 10).

Temperature, current and flow

Temperature. The MCP9700A gives \(V_{temperature} = T_c T + V_{0°C}\) with \(T_c = 0.01\) V/°C and \(V_{0°C} = 0.5\) V. The 12-bit ADC's reference is \(V_{ref} = V_{CC}/1.6\) with \(V_{CC} = 3.4\) V, so \(V_{ref} = 2.125\) V, and the ADC has a positive offset \(\Delta V \approx 0.05V_{ref}\):

Geyser temperature from the ADC\[ R = 4095\,\frac{V_{temperature} + \Delta V}{V_{ref}} \qquad T = \frac{V_{ref}}{4095\,T_c}\left(R - \frac{4095\,\Delta V}{V_{ref}}\right) - \frac{V_{0°C}}{T_c} \]
\(R\)
12-bit ADC reading.
\(V_{temperature}\)
Sensor output (V).
\(\Delta V\)
ADC positive offset, about \(0.05V_{ref}\) (V).
\(V_{ref}\)
ADC reference, \(V_{CC}/1.6 = 2.125\) V.
\(T\)
Water temperature (°C).
\(T_c\)
0.01 V/°C.
\(V_{0°C}\)
0.5 V.

Numerically \(V_{ref}/(4095 T_c) = 0.05189\) and \(4095\,\Delta V/V_{ref} = 0.05 \times 4095 = 204.75\) counts, giving the uncalibrated \(T = 0.05189(R - 204.75) - 50\). Calibration (Week 9) changed the offset to 185 counts: \(T = 0.05189(R - 185) - 50\).

Worked example 1.1 reading the geyser temperature

(a) The ADC reads 1500. What temperature do the two equations give? (b) What reading corresponds to 60 °C (uncalibrated model)?

1
(a) Uncalibrated: \(0.05189 \times (1500 - 204.75) - 50 = 17.21\) °C. Calibrated: \(0.05189 \times 1315 - 50 = 18.24\) °C.
2
(b) \(V_{temperature} = 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1\) V; \(R = 4095 \times (1.1 + 0.10625)/2.125 = 2324.5\), so 2324.
3
Check: \(0.05189 \times (2324 - 204.75) - 50 = 59.97\) °C.
17.21 °C uncalibrated, 18.24 °C calibrated; 60 °C reads as about 2324.

Current and energy. A current transformer (CT) around the element's supply gives a voltage proportional to the 50 Hz AC current. It is sampled every 2 ms (500 Hz), each sample converted with \(I = 0.50366R\) mA, and the RMS taken over 80 samples (160 ms, exactly 8 mains cycles):

RMS current and energy\[ I_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{80} I_i^2}{80}} \qquad E\,[\text{Wh}] \leftarrow E + \frac{T_{sample}\, V\, I_{RMS}}{3600} \]
\(I_{RMS}\)
RMS element current (A).
\(I_i\)
Calibrated current samples (A).
\(E\)
Running energy total (Wh), updated each interval.
\(T_{sample}\)
Time covered by one RMS value, here 80 × 2 ms = 0.16 s.
\(V\)
Mains voltage, 220 V (V).
3600
Seconds per hour, converting J to Wh.
Watch out

As printed, \(I = 0.50366R\) mA gives only 2.06 A at full scale (\(R = 4095\)), while a 3 kW element on 220 V draws 13.6 A. A one-sided reading also cannot represent the negative half of an AC current without an offset. Treat the constant as an example of a per-sample calibration, not a design value.

Flow. The Hall-effect flow meter outputs 380 pulses per litre, wired straight to a timer's trigger input so the timer counts pulses in hardware. Every 60 s: flow [l/min] = pulses in 60 s / 380.

Modem, relay, valve and OTA updates

  • Modem: UART at 9600 baud, 8N1, with a complicated AT-command interface: register on the network, activate the packet data protocol (PDP), connect to the server over TCP/IP, subscribe to a "mailbox" on the MQTT message bus.
  • Relay: a latching relay switches the element. A pulse sets or resets it and it keeps its state. Set and reset are driven by GPIOs through transistors, and an opto-isolator read on a GPIO confirms the actual state.
  • Valve: driven by an H-bridge from GPIO outputs: positive voltage opens, negative closes (Week 10).
Watch out

Slide 10 gives the relay as "12 V supply and 1.25 W to switch (2.5 A at 5 V for at least 10 ms)". 2.5 A at 5 V is 12.5 W, and 1.25 W at 12 V is 104 mA; the figures do not agree with each other, so one of them is a typo. Either way a transistor drives the coil, never the GPIO pin.

Bootloader and OTA update. To fix problems or add features after installation, the server sends "initiate OTA update". The application tells the modem to download the new binary, then resets. The MCU always starts in the bootloader, a separate program that checks the modem's memory for new firmware, writes it to a staging area in flash, then copies it over the application.

Figure 1.3: Application and staging area are each 61 440 bytes (60 KiB), so a complete new image fits before the old one is overwritten. The application info and bootloader areas are 8 KiB each, the bootloader sitting in the ATxmega's separate boot section above 128 KiB. Total 139 264 bytes.

Watch out

Slide 11 lists the application area as 0x00000 to 0x0DFFF, but its decimal end, 61 439, is 0x0EFFF, and the next area starts at 0x0F000. The hex end address 0x0DFFF is the typo.

02Example: horizon and sun sensor

Reading Lecture 28, slides 12 to 22

A satellite's control system needs its orientation, the attitude. It can be estimated by comparing a known or modelled direction (where the sun is now, or the direction to the centre of the Earth, the nadir vector) with a measurement of the same direction in the satellite's frame. Both can be measured with a CMOS camera and image processing: same electronics, different optics.

Horizon sensorSun sensor
OpticsRegular CCTV-type lensPin-hole lens with a neutral-density filter that blocks everything but the sun
ProcessingDetect edge points between space and the Earth, fit a circle; its centre gives the nadir vectorFind the cluster of pixels above a threshold; its centroid gives the sun vector

Requirements: talk to the rest of the satellite over a CAN bus (commands such as take photo and detect, telemetry requests, image downloads); configure the image sensor over I2C (exposure, gain); store images in external SRAM, with an FPGA clocking the image out of the sensor into SRAM and then letting the MCU read it; do the image processing; measure current. Lens distortion, worse at wide fields of view, is removed by calibration.

The MCU is an 8-bit C8051F050 (8051 core): 8-bit data bus, 16-bit address bus, 64 KB flash, 4.25 KB RAM, 5 timers, 12-bit ADC and built-in CAN. The image sensor is a 1288 × 1032 SXGA monochrome sensor with I2C address 10101012 (0x55) or 01100112 (0x33), selectable in case of a clash. A setting is written with an I2C write of address, register number and value; it is read with a write of address and register number followed by a read (Week 7).

Paged SRAM. Images are stored as 1024 × 1024 × 8 bits (brightness only): 1 MiB. A 16-bit address bus reaches only 0 to 65 535, so the SRAM is accessed in 32 KiB (32 768-byte) pages. Writing the page register (held in the FPGA) chooses which page appears at MCU addresses 0x8000 to 0xFFFF.

Figure 2.1: The 1 MiB image needs \(2^{20}/2^{15} = 32\) pages. To read a pixel, compute its linear address, write the page number to the page register, then read 0x8000 plus the offset within the page.

Paged address of a pixel\[ a = 1024\,r + c \qquad \text{page} = \left\lfloor \frac{a}{32\,768} \right\rfloor \qquad \text{address} = \text{0x8000} + (a \bmod 32\,768) \]
\(a\)
Linear byte address of the pixel in SRAM.
\(r, c\)
Pixel row and column (0 to 1023).
page
Value to write to the page register (0 to 31).
address
MCU address to read.
Worked example 2.1 reading one pixel

Which page and MCU address hold pixel row 500, column 300?

1
\(a = 1024 \times 500 + 300 = 512\,300\).
2
Page \(= \lfloor 512\,300/32\,768 \rfloor = 15\); offset \(= 512\,300 - 15 \times 32\,768 = 20\,780\) = 0x512C.
3
Address \(= \) 0x8000 + 0x512C = 0xD12C.
Write 15 to the page register, then read address 0xD12C. Each page holds 32 complete rows.

03A2 revision: bits, pointers and interrupts

Reading Revision lecture A2

About A2

A SUNLearn quiz, very like the practicals and A1: a 3-hour paper of 90 marks, 30 from the third quarter and 60 from the fourth. STM32CubeIDE is not needed; prescribed calculators (or the Windows calculator) are allowed. Final mark: FM = 10 % × SM + 40 % × A1 + 50 % × A2, where SM is the average of the practicals after dropping the lowest two (a missed practical counts as 0).

Inside the microcontroller are the processor core, memory (flash for the program, SRAM for data) and peripherals (timers, GPIO, serial ports, ADC), all reached through memory-mapped registers.

  • Mask: 1100 1010 AND 0110 0110 = 0100 0010.
  • OR: 0x12345678 | 0x0000FFFF = 0x1234FFFF. BIC: 0x12345678 BIC 0x0000FFFF = 0x12340000.
  • Set bit \(n\): a = b | (1 << n); with b = 1010 1010 and n = 6: 1110 1010.
  • Clear bit \(n\): a = b & ~(1 << n); with n = 5: 1000 1010.
  • Match a pattern: if ((input & 0b00001001) == 0b00000001) tests "bit 0 is 1 and bit 3 is 0" (pattern XXXX 0XX1).
Watch out

The slide shifts 1010 1010 right to 1101 0101: that is the arithmetic shift ASR, which copies the sign bit. In C, >> on an unsigned value shifts in zeros (giving 0101 0101); on a negative signed value the result is implementation-defined (ARM compilers use ASR). Use unsigned types such as uint32_t for registers.

Figure 3.1: Each pin has two MODER bits; pin 13 uses bits 27 and 26, and output mode is 01. GPIOD starts at 0x40020C00 and MODER is at offset 0. Clear the two bits with an AND mask, then set bit 26 with OR, leaving every other pin unchanged.

uint32_t *GPIOD_MODER = (uint32_t *) 0x40020C00;
*GPIOD_MODER = (*GPIOD_MODER & 0xF3FFFFFF) | 0x04000000;

In assembly the same is LDR (address), LDR (value), BIC #0x0C000000, ORR #0x04000000, STR.

Pointers, arrays and volatile

  • A pointer is a variable holding an address, with a type giving the size and kind of data there (int* my_ptr;). On the Cortex-M4 a pointer is always 32 bits whatever it points to.
  • & takes an address (my_ptr = &my_var;); * dereferences it (*my_ptr = *my_ptr + 1; reads, increments and writes back).
  • Uses: specific memory locations (peripheral registers), arrays, and passing by reference.
  • A constant address must be cast so the compiler knows the data size: *((uint32_t*)0x40020000), usually wrapped in a #define (no spaces needed, no semicolon at the end).
  • An array name acts as a pointer: scores[3] is *(scores + 3), where +3 means 3 elements, not 3 bytes (12 bytes for int). But an array name is not a variable: data++ is illegal, while dataPtr++ on a pointer is legal. int scores[4] reserves 16 bytes; int* ptr reserves only the pointer.
  • volatile tells the compiler the value can change at any time without nearby code changing it (an ISR, or hardware), so it must re-read it every time. volatile uint16_t x; and uint16_t volatile y; are the same; pointers to memory-mapped registers are declared volatile uint8_t *p_reg;.
Watch out

Revision slide 11's final example writes the mask as 0xffffff3 (seven hex digits), which is 0x0FFFFFF3 and would also clear the top four bits of MODER. The intended mask is 0xFFFFFFF3. Count the digits in every 32-bit mask.

Stack and exceptions

The stack pointer SP (R13) holds the address of the top of the stack. The stack starts at a high address and grows down: pushing two words decrements SP by 8 (in the slide's example from 0xBEFFFAE8 to 0xBEFFFAE0).

Interrupts raise four questions: which function to run and where it is (the vector table), how to keep the interrupted program's registers and flags intact (automatic stacking), which interrupt wins if two occur together (priorities), and how to protect critical code (masking). On the Cortex-M4 an exception returns when handler-mode code loads the PC with LDM, POP, LDR or BX, which acts like BX LR. On entry LR was loaded with an EXC_RETURN value (0xFFFFFFE1 to 0xFFFFFFFD) whose low 5 bits say which stack to return to and in which mode; LR does not hold the return address, which was saved on the stack.

Problem 4.2 RMS and energy

The 80 current samples follow a 50 Hz sine of peak 1000 mA. Find \(I_{RMS}\), the energy added per 160 ms at 220 V, and the energy per hour.

1
80 samples span exactly 8 cycles, so \(I_{RMS} = 1000/\sqrt{2} = 707.1\) mA.
2
\(\Delta E = 0.16 \times 220 \times 0.7071/3600 = 0.006914\) Wh.
3
Per hour: \(220 \times 0.7071 = 155.6\) Wh.
707.1 mA, 0.006914 Wh per update, 155.6 Wh per hour.
