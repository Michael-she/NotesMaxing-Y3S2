Noise: decibels, hearing, standards and control

Sound pressure and the decibel scale, how levels add, how the ear hears and why we weight, the South African occupational and environmental noise standards with their rating calculations, and how noise falls with distance and is controlled.

01What is sound?

Sound is any variation in air pressure that the human ear can detect: a disturbance propagated through an elastic medium, fluctuating about the ambient pressure. The pressure deviation is the sound pressure (Pa). Waves below the audible range are infrasonic, above it ultrasonic. Noise is sound that is unwanted or objectionable to a person.

Speed of sound and wavelength\[ c = 20.05\sqrt{T}\ \text{m/s}, \qquad c = \lambda f \]
\(c\)
Speed of sound; 343 m/s in air at 20 °C (about 1500 m/s in water and 6000 m/s in steel).
\(T\)
Absolute air temperature (K).
\(\lambda\)
Wavelength (m).
\(f\)
Frequency (Hz).

Figure 1.1: 10 Hz has a 34 m wavelength, 100 Hz 3.4 m, 1 kHz 343 mm and 10 kHz 34 mm. An obstacle must be larger than about one wavelength to disturb the sound, which is why bass is hard to block and treble easy.

A young, healthy ear hears from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and from the threshold of hearing (0 dB, 20 µPa) to the threshold of pain (about 130 dB, roughly 100 Pa).

02The decibel scale

The ear responds to pressures from 20 µPa to over 100 Pa, a ratio of more than a million. A logarithmic scale compresses this to 0 to 140 dB.

Sound pressure level\[ L_p = 10\log\left(\frac{p_{rms}}{p_{ref}}\right)^2 = 20\log\frac{p_{rms}}{p_{ref}}, \qquad p_{ref} = 20\ \mu\text{Pa} \]
\(L_p\)
Sound pressure level (dB).
\(p_{rms}\)
RMS sound pressure (Pa): square, average over the period, square root.
\(p_{ref}\)
Reference pressure, the threshold of hearing at 1 kHz.

Decibels describe energy: the squared RMS pressure relative to a reference. A tenfold increase in pressure adds 20 dB. Other quantities use their own references: acceleration \(10^{-6}\) m/s², velocity \(10^{-9}\) m/s, force \(10^{-6}\) N, power \(10^{-12}\) W, energy \(10^{-12}\) J.

ChangeHow it is perceived
1 dBBarely perceptible
3 dBNoticeable (double the energy)
6 dBClearly noticeable (double the pressure)
10 dBPerceived as a doubling of loudness

Typical levels: whisper 20 to 30 dB, normal speech about 60 dB, playground 80 dB, pneumatic drill about 110 dB, aircraft take-off nearby 130 to 150 dB. A pure tone can be heard even 10 dB below the ambient noise.

Adding and subtracting levels

Total level of several sources\[ L_{p,tot} = 10\log\left(10^{L_{p1}/10} + 10^{L_{p2}/10} + \cdots + 10^{L_{pn}/10}\right) \]
\(L_{pi}\)
Level of source \(i\) alone (dB).
Source level in the presence of background noise\[ L_S = 10\log\left(10^{L_{S+N}/10} - 10^{L_N/10}\right) \]
\(L_{S+N}\)
Measured with the source running (dB).
\(L_N\)
Background alone (dB).

So 60 dB + 60 dB = 63 dB, not 120 dB. The source must be at least 3 dB above the background to be measurable; between 3 and 10 dB a correction is needed; beyond 10 dB none. Background-sensitive measurements are often made at night.

Figure 2.1: Left: add the read value to the louder level (example: 55 and 51 dB differ by 4 dB, add 1.4 dB, total 56.4 dB). Right: subtract the read value from the total (example: 60 dB total, 53 dB background, difference 7 dB, subtract 1 dB, machine 59 dB).

Figure 2.2: Bars: the two levels and their total. Equal levels give +3 dB; a level 10 dB below the other adds only 0.4 dB.

Watch out

The perception slide labels 6 dB "actual doubling of sound level". Six decibels doubles the sound pressure; doubling the sound energy (two identical machines) adds 3 dB, and a perceived doubling of loudness needs about 10 dB.

03Hearing and weighting

Sound passes through the ear canal to the eardrum; the ossicles (hammer, anvil, stirrup) carry it to the fluid of the cochlea, where hair cells convert the motion to nerve impulses. Damage to the hair cells is cumulative and irreversible, which is why exposure is regulated. Noise-induced loss is usually greatest around 4 kHz, and hearing in the 2 to 10 kHz range declines with age.

The ear is most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz (the ear canal resonance) and much less sensitive at low frequency: a 20 Hz tone needs about 80 dB to be heard. Phon lines (equal loudness contours) are found by asking listeners to match tones to a 1 kHz reference; a sound on the 40 phon line is as loud as a 40 dB tone at 1 kHz.

Weighting scales

Weighting filters correct a measured spectrum for the ear's sensitivity. The A-scale follows the 40 phon line and is used for annoyance, hearing conservation and subjective loudness; units dB(A) or dBA. The C-scale follows the 60 phon line and is used for very loud noise or when low-frequency noise is present. Both are 0 dB at 1 kHz, the frequency used for calibration.

An octave-band measurement gives, from 31.5 Hz to 8 kHz: 80, 82, 85, 84, 83, 80, 75, 72, 70 dB. Find the A-weighted spectrum and the overall level in dB(A). (Notes problem 8, also Tutorial 5 problem 21.)

04SANS 10083: occupational noise

SANS 10083 deals with the measurement and assessment of occupational noise for hearing conservation.

Equivalent continuous A-weighted level\[ L_{Aeq,T} = 10\log\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T\frac{p_A^2(t)}{p_{ref}^2}dt\right] \]
\(L_{Aeq,T}\)
The steady level with the same energy as the fluctuating sound over \(T\) (dBA).
\(p_A(t)\)
A-weighted sound pressure (Pa).
Composite measurement\[ L_{Aeq,T} = 10\log\sum_if_i10^{L_{Aeq,T_i}/10} \]
\(f_i\)
Duration of interval \(i\) as a fraction of the total time.
\(L_{Aeq,T_i}\)
Level during interval \(i\) (dBA).
Rating level and 8-hour normalisation\[ L_{Req,T} = L_{Aeq,T} + C_i, \qquad L_{Req,8h} = L_{Req,T} + 10\log\frac{T}{8} \]
\(C_i\)
Impulse correction: 5 dB for regular impulsive sound (bouncing balls, church bells, tennis), 12 dB for highly impulsive sound (metal or pneumatic hammering, drop forging); specialist advice for high-energy impulses (blasting, gunfire).
\(T\)
Shift length (h).
Limits

\(L_{Req,8h} \geq 85\) dBA: the area is a noise zone and a hearing conservation programme is required. \(L_{Req,8h} \geq 130\) dBA: hearing protection is no longer sufficient and expert advice must be obtained.

Measurement: an integrating sound level meter of at least Type 2, calibrated; the microphone at the more exposed ear, 1.5 m above the floor for standing workers and 0.9 m for seated ones; at least three positions in larger areas; extraneous sound avoided, or the noise of interest at least 10 dB above it; intervals representative of the shift; for workers who move about, their personal dose.

A hearing conservation programme: assessment by a competent person, demarcated noise zones, information and training (refreshed at least every two years), certified hearing protection with instruction in its use, baseline audiometry (within 30 days of starting) and periodic audiometry, review at least every two years or when work methods or equipment change. Employees must use the measures provided and report defective protection.

Worked example 4.1 A ten-hour shift

A worker spends 2 h at 75 dBA, 4 h at 80 dBA, 2 h at 85 dBA and 2 h at 95 dBA, with no impulsive noise. Is the limit met? (Lecture slide example.)

1
\(L_{Aeq,10h} = 10\log\left[\frac{2}{10}10^{7.5} + \frac{4}{10}10^{8.0} + \frac{2}{10}10^{8.5} + \frac{2}{10}10^{9.5}\right] = 88.7\) dBA.
2
\(C_i = 0\), so \(L_{Req,10h} = 88.7\) dBA.
3
\(L_{Req,8h} = 88.7 + 10\log(10/8) = 89.7\) dBA.
89.7 dBA exceeds 85 dBA: noise zone, hearing conservation needed. The two hours at 95 dBA carry almost all the energy.
Worked example 4.2 Effect of shift length

From the SANS 10083 video: 2 h at 85, 2 h at 75, 1 h at 78 and 3 h at 92 dBA. Then the time at 78 dBA is increased to 2 h.

1
8-hour shift: \(L_{Req,8h} = 88.38\) dBA (the normalisation term is zero).
2
9-hour shift: \(L_{Req,9h} = 87.92\) dBA, lower because the quiet hour dilutes the average, but \(L_{Req,8h} = 87.92 + 10\log(9/8) = 88.43\) dBA.
Extra time at a moderate level still adds dose: the 8-hour level rises from 88.38 to 88.43 dBA.

05SANS 10103: environmental noise

SANS 10103 deals with the measurement and rating of environmental noise with respect to annoyance and speech communication.

Rating level for environmental noise\[ L_{Req,T} = L_{Aeq,T} + C_i + C_t + k_n \]
\(C_i\)
Impulse correction, 5 or 12 dB as before.
\(C_t\)
Tonal correction, 5 dB for an audible tone (whine, hum, whistle, music).
\(k_n\)
10 dB added to night-time measurements.
Day/night rating level\[ L_{R,dn} = 10\log\left[\frac{d}{24}10^{L_{Req,d}/10} + \frac{24 - d}{24}10^{L_{Req,n}/10}\right] \]
\(d\)
Daytime hours, 06:00 to 22:00 (16 h).
\(L_{Req,d}, L_{Req,n}\)
Day and night rating levels; the night level includes \(k_n\).

Figure 5.1: Notes problem 11: 70 dBA from 06:00 to 18:00, 65 dBA to 22:00 and 50 dBA overnight. Night hours (22:00 to 06:00) are penalised by 10 dB before averaging over 24 h.

Worked example 5.1 Day/night rating

Find \(L_{R,dn}\) for the day of Figure 5.1.

1
\(L_{R,dn} = 10\log\left[\frac{12}{24}10^{7.0} + \frac{4}{24}10^{6.5} + \frac{8}{24}10^{(50 + 10)/10}\right]\).
67.7 dBA (notes answer 67.7 dBA). For an urban district the guideline is 55 dBA, so this site is 13 dB over.

Measured: 60 dBA from 06:00 to 18:00, 45 dBA from 18:00 to 24:00, 40 dBA from 00:00 to 06:00.

Indoor values are 10 dB below outdoor: a building with open windows is assumed to give 10 dB of isolation. The standard also gives design and maximum indoor levels by occupancy (for example 40 dBA for a classroom), the distance at which speech is intelligible for a given rating level (at 45 dBA a normal voice carries about 7 m), telephone quality, and expected community response (sporadic complaints for 0 to 10 dB above the guideline, rising to vigorous action for large excesses). Its annexes cover the report, tonal character and low-frequency noise.

Low-frequency noise (roughly 8 to 100 Hz) comes from large diesel engines, ships, power plants and wind turbines; it is hard to muffle because the wavelengths are long, is felt as pressure or pulsation, and is more annoying than its A-weighted level suggests. A large difference between C- and A-weighted levels signals it. Psychoacoustics studies the character of a sound beyond its loudness; car makers tune engine and door sounds this way.

Western Cape noise control regulations

  • Ambient noise: the all-encompassing sound at a place and time, measured with an integrating impulse sound level meter for at least 10 minutes.
  • Residual noise: the same, excluding the noise alleged to be a nuisance.
  • Noise nuisance: any sound that impairs or may impair the convenience or peace of a reasonable person.
  • Disturbing noise (excluding the unamplified human voice): exceeds the rating level by 7 dBA; or exceeds the residual level where that is above the rating level; or exceeds the residual level by 3 dBA where that is below the rating level; or, for low-frequency noise, exceeds SANS 10103 Annex B.

06Prediction and control

A point source is small compared with the distance to the listener (a fan, a speaker): sound spreads spherically and drops 6 dB per doubling of distance. A line source is long and narrow (a road, a railway, a pipe): sound spreads cylindrically and drops 3 dB per doubling.

Point source near the ground\[ L_p = L_W - 20\log r - 8\ \text{dB} \]
\(L_p\)
Sound pressure level at distance \(r\) (dB).
\(L_W\)
Sound power level of the source (dB re 1 pW).
\(r\)
Distance (m).
Line source near the ground\[ L_p = L_W - 10\log r - 5\ \text{dB} \]
\(L_W\)
Sound power level per metre of source (dB re 1 pW/m).

Figure 6.1: Point source near the ground: \(L_p = L_W - 20\log r - 8\) (6 dB per doubling). Line source: \(L_p = L_W - 10\log r - 5\) with \(L_W\) per metre (3 dB per doubling). Ground and air absorption are ignored.

Watch out

The slides call \(L_W\) "the SPL at the source". It is the sound power level, \(10\log(W/10^{-12})\), a property of the source independent of the surroundings. The constants 8 and 5 dB assume a source on reflecting ground (hemispherical spreading); in a free field the point-source constant is 11 dB.

Noise control

When rating limits are exceeded, reduce noise at:

  • the source: change the operation or the machine, or enclose it acoustically;
  • the path: barriers, absorption, isolation (including vibration isolators so sound does not travel through the floor);
  • the receiver: ear defenders, soundproof booths, work rotation. Hearing protection is not a permanent solution.

Absorption: a surface treatment that dissipates sound energy instead of reflecting it (wool or foam against steel or hardwood). Isolation: a barrier between source and receiver; the ratio of transmitted to incident sound measures its effect. Products: rock wool (spun molten rock, non-combustible, resin bonded), glass wool (sheets and tiles, thermal and acoustic), dry-wall (gypsum board with a wool-filled cavity), and porous ceiling tiles for room absorption.
