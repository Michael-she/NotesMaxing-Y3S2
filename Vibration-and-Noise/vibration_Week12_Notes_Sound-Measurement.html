01Microphones and sound level meters

Week 11 defined the quantities; this week is about measuring them properly. Sound pressure is measured with a microphone, usually a condenser type, chosen for its flat frequency response, precision and stability.

A sound level meter (SLM) integrates the microphone, preamplifier, processing electronics and software into one instrument that gives accurate, repeatable sound levels.

Figure 2.1: The weak microphone signal is buffered by the preamplifier, optionally weighted (A, C, or unweighted Z/Lin) or split into octave bands, converted to an RMS level by a time-weighted detector, and displayed in decibels.

ClassUse
Type 0Laboratory reference, used to calibrate other instruments
Type 1Precision; required for measurements to most standards
Type 2General purpose and noise surveys; not for reportable measurements (but the minimum SANS 10083 accepts)

Microphone types

Every microphone disturbs the field it measures. A free-field microphone compensates for its own disturbance and is pointed at the source (outdoors, most free-field work; IEC meters). A pressure microphone reads the actual pressure at its diaphragm, disturbance included (couplers, walls). A random-incidence microphone responds evenly to sound from all directions (diffuse, reverberant rooms; ANSI meters); in a free field, aim it at 70° to 80° to the source. A free-field microphone in a diffuse field under-reads; a resonator cap can convert it.

02Meter settings and analysis

Time weighting

Most sounds fluctuate, so the RMS detector averages over a time constant. Two settings are standardised for steady and fluctuating sound, and one for impulses:

SettingTime constantUse
F (Fast)125 msFollows fluctuating levels that do not change too rapidly
S (Slow)1 sAverages out rapid fluctuations so an analogue display can be read
I (Impulse)35 msIsolated impulses and impact noise, weighted for human perception
PeaknoneThe maximum instantaneous pressure, independent of duration; a hold circuit stores peak or maximum RMS

Figure 3.1: Background 60 dB with a 90 dB burst starting at t = 0.5 s. Each curve is the exponentially time-weighted level \(10\log[\frac{1}{\tau}\int p^2(\xi)e^{-(t - \xi)/\tau}d\xi/p_{ref}^2]\) with \(\tau\) = 1 s (S), 125 ms (F) or 35 ms (I, rise only; the standard Impulse detector also holds the peak with a slow 1.5 s decay, not modelled). Short bursts are under-read by the slower detectors.

Watch out

The lecture 3 notes and 2025 slides give the Fast time constant as 125 µs and say Fast is for sound that does not change quickly while Slow is for sound that varies quickly. The time constant is 125 ms (Brüel & Kjær, IEC 61672). Fast tracks moderately fluctuating levels; Slow damps the reading so a rapidly fluctuating level can be read as an average. The slides' wording mixes up the purpose (Slow is used because Fast fluctuates too much to read).

Energy parameters

Hearing damage depends on level and duration, so the energy received matters. An integrating SLM computes \(L_{eq}\) (or \(L_{Aeq}\)) directly, even for randomly varying sound; for stepwise levels a meter and a stopwatch suffice (week 11). The sound exposure level SEL (\(L_{AE}\)) normalises a single event, such as a vehicle pass-by, to one second:

Sound exposure level\[ L_{AE} = 10\log\left[\frac{1}{t_0}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{p_A^2(t)}{p_{ref}^2}dt\right], \quad t_0 = 1\ \text{s} \;\Rightarrow\; L_{AE} = L_{Aeq,T} + 10\log\frac{T}{1\ \text{s}} \]
\(L_{AE}\)
A-weighted sound exposure level (dB).
\(T\)
Duration of the event \(t_2 - t_1\) (s).

Noise dose meters are worn by workers who move between environments; they read the percentage of the daily allowable dose. ISO 1999 uses an equal-energy rule (each +3 dB halves the allowed time); OSHA in the United States allows +5 dB per halving.

Frequency analysis

Narrow-band (FFT or PSD in dB) analysis identifies specific frequencies, such as a tone. When broad bands matter more, octave (1/1), third-octave (1/3) or finer bands give one number per band.

Octave band edges\[ f_l = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt2}, \quad f_u = \sqrt2f_0 \;(f_u = 2f_l); \qquad \text{third octave: } f_u = 2^{1/3}f_l \approx 1.26f_l \]
\(f_0\)
Band centre frequency (Hz): 31.5, 63, 125, 250, 500, 1000, ... for octaves.
\(f_l, f_u\)
Lower and upper band edges (Hz).

Figure 3.2: Each octave spans a factor of two (the 1 kHz octave runs from 707 to 1414 Hz); three third-octaves fit in one octave. On a log axis all bands of a type have equal width.

03Measuring in practice

Sound fields

An anechoic chamber absorbs all reflections (true free field); a reverberation chamber has hard, non-parallel surfaces that create a diffuse field in which the total sound power of a source can be measured. Real rooms are in between:

Figure 4.1: Near field (closer than the longest wavelength or twice the largest machine dimension, whichever is larger): the level varies erratically with position. Free field: the level falls 6 dB per doubling of distance; measure here. Reverberant field: reflections dominate and the level stops falling. Illustrative curve.

Where and how to measure

  • Away from reflecting surfaces (walls, floor, facades) and obstacles; usually 1.2 to 1.5 m above the ground and at least 1.2 m from walls, as the standards require.
  • Outdoors: downwind of the source, in dry conditions, with wind below 5 m/s, and always with a foam windscreen (it also protects against dust and rain).
  • Humidity up to 90% has negligible effect; avoid sudden temperature changes (condensation); isolate the meter from strong vibration.
  • Background noise: measure with and without the source; a difference under 3 dB is too small, 3 to 10 dB needs a correction, over 10 dB needs none (week 11 chart).
  • For a worker's exposure, measure where the person works, at the more exposed ear, regardless of near or far field.

Calibration and reporting

Instruments are calibrated annually by a SANAS-accredited laboratory using Type 0 equipment, and field-calibrated before and after every session with a handheld calibrator or pistonphone producing 94 dB (1 Pa) or 114 dB at 1 kHz. If recordings are made, record the calibration tone too.

A measurement report should contain: a sketch of the site with dimensions and microphone positions; the standard followed; instrument types and serial numbers; calibration method; weightings and detector settings; the character of the sound (continuous, impulsive, tonal); background level; environmental conditions; data on the source (type, load, speed); and the date.

Beyond single readings: a noise map joins points of equal level into contours to show where limits are exceeded and where hearing protection is mandatory; noise rating (NR) curves rate an octave spectrum by the highest curve it touches, weighting high frequencies more because they annoy more.

Watch out

The slides say instruments are calibrated by a "SANS accredited laboratory". SANS is the national standards series; laboratories are accredited by SANAS (the South African National Accreditation System), as the lecture notes say.

04Tutorial 5

Tutorial 5 covers weeks 11 and 12. Answers were computed for these notes (no solutions were supplied).

Figure 5.1: Grey bars: the measured levels read from the tutorial figure. Teal: after adding the A-weighting corrections. The low bands drop sharply, leaving the 500 Hz to 2 kHz bands dominant; overall 84.9 dB(A).

Problem 1. Speed of sound in dry air at 20 °C?

\(c = 20.05\sqrt{293.15} = 343\) m/s.

Problem 2. Sensors for an acoustic earthquake warning system: air, water or ground?

In the ground. The disturbance starts as a wave in the rock; sound travels several times faster in solids (thousands of m/s against 343 m/s in air) with less loss, so ground sensors give the earliest and strongest signal, and the airborne path would only pick up what is re-radiated.

Problem 3. Wavelength of 300 Hz in air at 20 °C?

\(\lambda = 343/300 = 1.14\) m.

Problem 4. Threshold of pain for a sound pressure of 100 Pa, in dB?

\(20\log(100/2\times10^{-5}) = 134\) dB (the booklets quote "about 130 dB").

Problem 5. Add 75, 70 and 90 dB.

90.2 dB: the two quieter sources add only 0.2 dB.

Problem 6. Low-frequency noise has its energy mainly below ___ Hz.

100 Hz (the Environmental Noise booklet: significant energy from about 8 to 100 Hz; below 20 Hz it is infrasound).

Problem 7. A train emits 90 dB/m; 88 dB is measured at distance \(r\). Find \(r\).

Line source: \(88 = 90 - 10\log r - 5\), so \(\log r = -0.3\) and \(r = 0.50\) m. The number is implausibly small for a train, a sign the data are idealised; without the 5 dB ground constant the answer would be 1.6 m.

Problem 8. A tonal (whistling) sound measures 60 dB(A). Equivalent rating?

Add \(C_t = 5\) dB: 65 dB(A).

Problem 9. Scope of SANS 10103 and SANS 10083?

SANS 10083: measurement and assessment of occupational noise for hearing conservation. SANS 10103: measurement and rating of environmental noise with respect to annoyance and speech communication.

Problem 10. Define the equivalent continuous day/night rating level.

The A-weighted equivalent rating level over 24 h, combining the day (06:00 to 22:00) and night rating levels in proportion to their hours, with 10 dB added to night levels: \(L_{R,dn} = 10\log[\frac{d}{24}10^{L_{Req,d}/10} + \frac{24 - d}{24}10^{L_{Req,n}/10}]\).

Problem 11. Night adjustment \(k_n\): ___ dB between ___ and ___.

10 dB, between 22:00 and 06:00.

Problem 12. Everyday speech at 1 m: \(L_{Aeq}\) of about ___ dB(A).

About 60 dB(A) (normal conversation, lecture 1).

Problem 13. Find \(L_{R,dn}\): 70 dB from 06:00 to 17:00, 65 dB from 17:00 to 20:00, 50 dB from 20:00 to 06:00.

Split at 22:00: day 11 h at 70, 3 h at 65, 2 h at 50; night 8 h at 50 + 10. \(L_{R,dn} = 10\log[\frac{11}{24}10^{7} + \frac{3}{24}10^{6.5} + \frac{2}{24}10^{5} + \frac{8}{24}10^{6}] = 67.3\) dB(A), matching the SANS 10103 video (67.25).

Problem 14. Exposure is normalised to a ___ hour day and the limit is ___ dB(A).

8 hours and 85 dB(A). (The tutorial names SANS 10084; the occupational standard taught in this course is SANS 10083.)

Problem 15. Which worker does not meet the hearing conservation limit? A: 3 h at 85, 5 h at 75, 2 h at 90, 2 h at 60 dB(A). B: 3 h at 82, 1 h at 85, 3 h at 65, 2 h at 60 dB(A).

A: 12 h shift, \(L_{Aeq,12h} = 84.1\), \(L_{Req,8h} = 84.1 + 10\log(12/8) = 85.9\) dB(A): exceeds 85. B: 9 h shift, \(L_{Aeq,9h} = 79.5\), \(L_{Req,8h} = 80.0\) dB(A): within the limit. Worker A (SA Agulhas II) is in a noise zone.

Problem 16. What does a hearing conservation programme entail?

Assessment by a competent person; demarcating and signposting noise zones; engineering and administrative noise reduction; certified hearing protection with training in its use and limitations; information and refresher training at least every two years; baseline audiometry within 30 days of starting and periodic audiometry; record keeping; review at least every two years or when work changes. Employees must use the measures and report defective protection.

Problem 17. What if \(L_{Req,8h} > 130\) dB(A)?

Hearing protection alone is not sufficient: obtain expert advice and reduce the noise at the source or path, or remove people from the exposure.

Problem 18. Regular versus highly impulsive sound, and \(C_i\)?

Highly impulsive: metal or wood hammering, drop forging, pneumatic hammering, \(C_i = 12\) dB. Regular impulsive: impulsive sound that is neither highly impulsive nor high-energy (bouncing balls, church bells, tennis), \(C_i = 5\) dB. High-energy impulses (blasting, gunfire) need specialist assessment.

Problem 19. When is the C-weighting preferred?

For very loud noise and when significant low-frequency content is present; a large C minus A difference flags low-frequency noise.

Problem 20. A machine has sound power level 112 dB(A); receiver 2 m away. Level there, and distance to fall below 85 dB(A)?

Point source near the ground: \(L_p = 112 - 20\log 2 - 8 = 98.0\) dB(A). Below 85 dB(A) for \(r > 10^{(112 - 8 - 85)/20} = 8.9\) m. (If 112 dB(A) were instead the level measured at 2 m, the 6 dB per doubling rule gives \(r = 2\times10^{27/20} = 45\) m; the Afrikaans and English wordings differ on this.)
