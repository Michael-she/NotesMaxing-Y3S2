Harmonic excitation of single-degree-of-freedom systems

The response of undamped and viscously damped systems to a harmonic force: the forced amplitude, beats, resonance, transient and steady-state response, and the amplitude and phase curves against frequency ratio.

01Harmonic excitation of undamped systems

A harmonic excitation is a force at a single frequency, \(F(t) = F_0\cos\omega t\). It is the model for a constant-speed pump on elastic mounts, an unbalanced rotor, or a crusher on a compliant foundation. The aim is the motion of the system when it starts from \(x_0\), \(v_0\) and this force acts.

Undamped forced equation of motion\[ m\ddot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \ddot x + \omega_n^2x = f_0\cos\omega t, \qquad f_0 = \frac{F_0}{m} \]
\(F_0\)
Force amplitude (N).
\(\omega\)
Driving (excitation) frequency (rad/s).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency \(\sqrt{k/m}\) (rad/s).
\(f_0\)
Mass-normalised force amplitude (N/kg = m/s²).

The solution is the homogeneous solution (free vibration, at \(\omega_n\)) plus a particular solution (forced response). Guess that the forced response has the form of the force, \(x_p = X\cos\omega t\). Substituting gives \((-\omega^2 + \omega_n^2)X\cos\omega t = f_0\cos\omega t\), so by undetermined coefficients:

Forced amplitude (undamped)\[ X = \frac{f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2} \]
\(X\)
Amplitude of the forced response (m); negative means opposite in phase to the force.
\(f_0\)
Mass-normalised force (m/s²).
\(\omega_n, \omega\)
Natural and driving frequencies (rad/s).

Adding \(A_1\sin\omega_n t + A_2\cos\omega_n t\) and applying \(x(0) = x_0\), \(\dot x(0) = v_0\):

Total undamped forced response\[ x(t) = \frac{v_0}{\omega_n}\sin\omega_n t + \left(x_0 - \frac{f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2}\right)\cos\omega_n t + \frac{f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2}\cos\omega t \]
\(x_0, v_0\)
Initial displacement (m) and velocity (m/s).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency: frequency of the free part (rad/s).
\(\omega\)
Driving frequency: frequency of the forced part (rad/s).
Key idea

The undamped response contains two frequencies: the free vibration at \(\omega_n\) and the forced vibration at \(\omega\). Without damping the free part never dies out. Below resonance (\(\omega \lt \omega_n\)) \(X \gt 0\) and the forced motion is in phase with the force; above resonance \(X \lt 0\): it is 180° out of phase.

Figure 1.1: Here \(f_0 = 0.1\) N/kg, \(x_0 = 10\) mm, \(v_0 = 10\) mm/s. The forced amplitude is \(X = 0.1/(1 - 4) = -3.33\) cm (out of phase), and the free part has amplitude \(\sqrt{0.01^2 + (0.01 + 0.0333)^2} = 4.45\) cm. The sum of the two never settles.

Worked example 1.1 Forced response from rest

A 10 kg machine on mounts of total stiffness 4000 N/m is driven by \(F = 50\cos 15t\) N, starting at rest. Find \(X\) and \(x(t)\). Repeat for \(\omega = 25\) rad/s.

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{4000/10} = 20\) rad/s; \(f_0 = 50/10 = 5\) N/kg.
2
\(\omega = 15\): \(X = 5/(400 - 225) = 0.02857\) m. With \(x_0 = v_0 = 0\): \(x(t) = 0.02857(\cos 15t - \cos 20t)\) m.
3
\(\omega = 25\): \(X = 5/(400 - 625) = -0.02222\) m: an amplitude of 22.2 mm, 180° out of phase with the force.
Below resonance the forced part is 28.6 mm in phase; above resonance it is 22.2 mm in antiphase. In both cases the free part at 20 rad/s has the same size and never decays.

Beats

Start from rest (\(x_0 = v_0 = 0\)) with \(\omega\) close to \(\omega_n\). The response is \(x = \frac{f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2}(\cos\omega t - \cos\omega_n t)\), and the identity \(\cos u - \cos v = 2\sin\frac{v-u}{2}\sin\frac{v+u}{2}\) turns it into a product:

Beating\[ x(t) = \frac{2f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2}\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega_n - \omega}{2}t\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega_n + \omega}{2}t\right), \qquad \omega_{beat} = |\omega_n - \omega| \]
\(\omega_{beat}\)
Beat frequency (rad/s): the rate at which the loud-quiet pattern repeats.
\((\omega_n + \omega)/2\)
Fast carrier frequency, close to both (rad/s).
\((\omega_n - \omega)/2\)
Slow envelope frequency (rad/s).

The fast sine at the average frequency is modulated by a slow sine envelope. The amplitude swells and fades once every \(2\pi/|\omega_n - \omega|\) seconds: a beat.

Figure 1.2: Lecture example (worked example 1.3 below): \(\theta = 0.182(\cos 8t - \cos 8.66t)\) rad. The amber dashed envelope is \(\pm 2X\sin\big((\omega_n - \omega)t/2\big)\). The angle reaches \(2X = 0.364\) rad at the middle of each beat and returns to zero every 9.52 s.

Watch out

The lecture notes print the beat amplitude as \(2f_0/(\omega_n^2 - \omega_n^2)\); the denominator should be \(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2\), as in the video and the line above it.

Resonance

When \(\omega = \omega_n\) exactly, \(X = f_0/(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)\) divides by zero and \(X\cos\omega t\) is no longer a valid particular solution (it solves the homogeneous equation). The particular solution takes the form \(x_p = \frac{f_0}{2\omega}t\sin\omega t\):

Resonance (undamped, ω = ωₙ)\[ x(t) = \frac{v_0}{\omega}\sin\omega t + x_0\cos\omega t + \frac{f_0}{2\omega}t\sin\omega t \]
\(f_0/(2\omega)\)
Growth rate of the amplitude (m/s).
\(t\)
Time (s): the amplitude grows linearly with it.

The amplitude grows without bound until something yields or breaks. This is resonance, the reason a driving frequency near \(\omega_n\) is the danger zone (the lecturer refers to the Chinook helicopter video as an example).

Worked example 1.2 Growth at resonance

The machine of example 1.1 is driven at exactly 20 rad/s from rest. How long until the amplitude reaches 50 mm?

1
\(x = \frac{5}{2 \times 20}t\sin 20t = 0.125t\sin 20t\) m.
2
Envelope \(0.125t = 0.05\) gives \(t = 0.4\) s, about 1.3 cycles.
Only 0.4 s: an undamped structure at resonance reaches large amplitudes very quickly.

Figure 1.3: Worked example 1.2: the peaks lie on the straight lines \(\pm f_0t/(2\omega)\).

Lecture example: two rods on springs

Figure 1.4: The horizontal rod (length 4L) carries the vertical spring \(k_1\) at its right end and the force at its left end, both 2L from O. The vertical rod (length 2L) carries the horizontal spring \(k_2\) at its lower end, L from O. Both rods turn together through \(\theta\), anticlockwise positive.

Worked example 1.3 Rods on springs (lecture example)

Each rod has mass per length \(\rho\). Derive the equation of motion for small \(\theta\) and find \(\theta(t)\) from rest for \(\rho = 1\) kg/m, \(L = 1\) m, \(k_1 = 100\) N/m, \(k_2 = 50\) N/m, \(F_0 = 6\) N, \(\omega = 8\) rad/s.

1
Moments about O (anticlockwise positive): \(-k_1(2L)^2\cos\theta\sin\theta - k_2L^2\cos\theta\sin\theta + 2FL\cos\theta = I_O\ddot\theta\).
2
\(I_O = \tfrac{1}{12}(4\rho L)(4L)^2 + \tfrac{1}{12}(2\rho L)(2L)^2 = 6\rho L^3\).
3
Small angles, divide by \(L\): \(6\rho L^2\ddot\theta + (4k_1 + k_2)L\theta = 2F_0\cos\omega t\).
4
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{(4k_1 + k_2)/(6\rho L)} = \sqrt{450/6} = 8.660\) rad/s; \(f_0 = 2F_0/(6\rho L^2) = 2\) rad/s².
5
\(X = 2/(75 - 64) = 0.182\) rad.
\(\theta(t) = 0.182(\cos 8t - \cos 8.66t)\) rad. Because 8 rad/s is close to 8.66 rad/s, the rods beat with period \(2\pi/0.66 = 9.52\) s (Figure 1.2).

02Harmonic excitation of damped systems

Add viscous damping:

Damped forced equation of motion\[ m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot x + \omega_n^2x = f_0\cos\omega t \]
\(c\)
Viscous damping coefficient (N s/m).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio \(c/(2m\omega_n)\).
\(f_0\)
Mass-normalised force \(F_0/m\) (m/s²).

Damping makes the forced response lag the force, so the particular solution needs a phase: \(x_p = X\cos(\omega t - \theta)\), or equivalently \(A_s\cos\omega t + B_s\sin\omega t\) with \(X = \sqrt{A_s^2 + B_s^2}\) and \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(B_s/A_s)\). Substituting and matching the cosine and sine terms (or setting \(t = 0\) and \(t = \pi/2\omega\)) gives two equations for \(A_s\) and \(B_s\):

Steady-state amplitude and phase\[ X = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}}, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{2\zeta\omega_n\omega}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2} \]
\(X\)
Steady-state amplitude (m).
\(\theta\)
Phase lag of the response behind the force (rad), between 0 and \(\pi\).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n, \omega\)
Natural and driving frequencies (rad/s).
Watch out

The phase lag runs from 0 to \(\pi\). Above resonance \(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2 \lt 0\), and a calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) returns a negative angle: add \(\pi\), or use atan2(2*z*wn*w, wn^2 - w^2). The Lecture 6 MATLAB code for the forced response uses atan with \(\omega = 10\) rad/s \(\gt \omega_n = 4.47\) rad/s, so its steady-state part has the wrong sign: checked against a numerical solution of the equation of motion, it is off by up to 0.31 m. With atan2 it agrees to \(10^{-11}\) m. The steady-state plotting code correctly uses atan2.

The total response is the decaying free vibration plus the steady state:

Total damped forced response\[ x(t) = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi) + X\cos(\omega t - \theta) \]
\(A, \phi\)
Constants of the free part, set by the initial conditions.
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency \(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) (rad/s).
\(X, \theta\)
Steady-state amplitude and phase, set by the force only.
Constants from the initial conditions\[ \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega_d(x_0 - X\cos\theta)}{v_0 + (x_0 - X\cos\theta)\zeta\omega_n - \omega X\sin\theta}, \qquad A = \frac{x_0 - X\cos\theta}{\sin\phi} \]
\(x_0, v_0\)
Initial displacement (m) and velocity (m/s).
\(X, \theta\)
Steady-state amplitude (m) and phase (rad).
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency (rad/s).
Definition

The free part decays and is called the transient response. What remains, \(X\cos(\omega t - \theta)\), is the steady-state response. With enough damping the transient dies out quickly and only the steady state matters.

Worked example 2.1 Steady-state amplitude

The machine of example 1.1 (\(m = 10\) kg, \(k = 4000\) N/m, \(F_0 = 50\) N, \(\omega = 15\) rad/s) now has \(c = 40\) N s/m. Find \(\zeta\), \(X\) and \(\theta\).

1
\(\zeta = 40/(2\sqrt{4000 \times 10}) = 0.1\); \(\omega_n = 20\) rad/s; \(r = 0.75\).
2
\(X = 5/\sqrt{(400 - 225)^2 + (2 \times 0.1 \times 20 \times 15)^2} = 5/\sqrt{30625 + 3600} = 0.02703\) m.
3
\(\theta = \tan^{-1}(60/175) = 0.3303\) rad = 18.9° (positive denominator: no correction).
\(X = 27.0\) mm lagging the force by 18.9°. The static deflection is \(F_0/k = 12.5\) mm, so the dynamic amplification is 2.16.
Worked example 2.2 Total response from rest

For example 2.1 starting from rest, find \(A\) and \(\phi\).

1
\(\omega_d = 20\sqrt{0.99} = 19.90\) rad/s; \(x_0 - X\cos\theta = -0.02703\cos 0.3303 = -0.02557\) m.
2
Denominator: \(0 + (-0.02557)(0.1)(20) - 15(0.02703)\sin 0.3303 = -0.05114 - 0.13137 = -0.1825\) m/s.
3
\(\phi = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{19.90(-0.02557)}{-0.1825}\): numerator and denominator are both negative, so \(\phi\) is in the third quadrant: \(\phi = -1.915\) rad.
4
\(A = -0.02557/\sin(-1.915) = 0.02716\) m.
\(x(t) = 27.16e^{-2t}\sin(19.90t -1.915) + 27.03\cos(15t - 0.3303)\) mm. Checked against a numerical solution of the equation of motion: they agree to \(10^{-11}\) m.

Figure 2.1: Early on the transient and steady state add and subtract (a damped beat between 15 and 19.9 rad/s). By about 2 s the transient, decaying as \(e^{-2t}\), has almost gone, and the response follows the dashed steady state.

Figure 2.2: It starts at worked example 2.2. Set ζ = 0 and slide ω close to 20 rad/s (lengthen the window) to see beats; at exactly 20 rad/s the undamped response grows without bound. With damping, the transient (difference between the blue curve and the dashed steady state) dies out at the rate ζωₙ, whatever the driving frequency.

The steady state against frequency

Divide by the static deflection \(f_0/\omega_n^2 = F_0/k\) and write \(r = \omega/\omega_n\), the frequency ratio:

Normalised steady-state response\[ \frac{X\omega_n^2}{f_0} = \frac{Xk}{F_0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2} \]
\(r\)
Frequency ratio \(\omega/\omega_n\).
\(Xk/F_0\)
Dynamic amplification: amplitude divided by the static deflection.
\(\theta\)
Phase lag (rad), 0 to \(\pi\).

Figure 2.3: Top: below \(r \approx 0.5\) the response is close to the static deflection; around \(r = 1\) it peaks, the more sharply the less damping; above \(r = \sqrt2\) it is smaller than static and falls towards zero. Bottom: the phase lag is near 0° at low frequency, exactly 90° at \(r = 1\) for every \(\zeta\), and tends to 180° at high frequency.

RegionAmplitudePhaseControlled by
\(r \ll 1\)\(X \approx F_0/k\)\(\theta \approx 0\)Stiffness
\(r = 1\)\(X = F_0/(2\zeta k)\)\(\theta = 90°\)Damping
\(r \gg 1\)\(X \approx F_0/(m\omega^2)\)\(\theta \approx 180°\)Mass
Key idea

Damping matters most near resonance. At \(r = 1\) the amplitude is \(1/(2\zeta)\) times the static deflection: 5 for \(\zeta = 0.1\), 50 for \(\zeta = 0.01\). Far from resonance, damping hardly changes the amplitude. The true peak is slightly below \(r = 1\), at \(r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\) (for \(\zeta \lt 0.707\)), with height \(1/(2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2})\).

Complex exponential method

Since \(Ae^{j\omega t} = A\cos\omega t + jA\sin\omega t\), the force \(F_0\cos\omega t\) is the real part of \(F_0e^{j\omega t}\). Solve \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0e^{j\omega t}\) with \(x_p = Xe^{j\omega t}\), and take the real part at the end:

\[ (-\omega^2m + jc\omega + k)Xe^{j\omega t} = F_0e^{j\omega t} \quad\Rightarrow\quad X = \frac{f_0}{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2) + j\,2\zeta\omega_n\omega}. \]
Steady state from the complex form\[ x_p(t) = \mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}}\,e^{j(\omega t - \theta)}\right\} \]
\(\mathrm{Re}\)
Real part.
\(X\)
Complex amplitude: its magnitude is the steady-state amplitude and its angle is \(-\theta\).

The magnitude of the complex \(X\) is the amplitude and its angle is the phase lag, which is why the complex form is a quick route to both.

Watch out

Equation 15 in the Lecture 6 notes omits the \(j\) on the damping term in the denominator. With it, the magnitude and angle of \(X\) give equations 16 and 17.

03Practice problems

Problem 3.1. A 20 kg mass on a 5 kN/m spring (undamped) is driven by \(100\cos 10t\) N from rest. Find the forced amplitude, the full \(x(t)\), and the beat period.

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{250} = 15.81\) rad/s, \(f_0 = 5\) N/kg, \(X = 5/(250 - 100) = 33.33\) mm. \(x(t) = 33.33(\cos 10t - \cos 15.81t)\) mm. The frequencies are not very close, but the envelope still repeats every \(2\pi/5.81 = 1.081\) s.

Problem 3.2. For the system of 3.1 with \(\zeta = 0.05\), what is the steady-state amplitude at resonance, and how does it compare with the static deflection?

Static deflection \(F_0/k = 20\) mm. At \(r = 1\), \(X = F_0/(2\zeta k) = 20/0.1 = 200\) mm: ten times larger.

Problem 3.3. At what frequency ratio is the steady-state amplitude equal to the static deflection, for any damping ratio?

Set \((1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2 = 1\): \(r^2(r^2 - 2 + 4\zeta^2) = 0\), so \(r = \sqrt{2 - 4\zeta^2}\), which is \(\sqrt2\) for light damping. Above this ratio the forced amplitude is smaller than static.

Problem 3.4. A system with \(\zeta = 0.2\) is driven at \(r = 2\). Find \(Xk/F_0\) and the phase lag in degrees.

\(Xk/F_0 = 1/\sqrt{(1-4)^2 + 0.8^2} = 1/\sqrt{9.64} = 0.3221\). \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.8/(-3))\): the calculator gives −14.9°, so add 180°: \(\theta = 165.1\)°.

Problem 3.5. Measured steady-state amplitudes at \(r = 1\) are 8 mm, and the static deflection under the same force amplitude is 0.5 mm. Estimate \(\zeta\).

\(X/X_{st} = 1/(2\zeta) = 16\), so \(\zeta = 0.031\).

Problem 3.6. Why does the undamped forced response not reach a steady state, even far from resonance?

With \(c = 0\) the free vibration at \(\omega_n\) excited by the starting transient never decays, so the response is always the sum of two harmonics. Any real damping, however small, eventually removes the \(\omega_n\) part.

04Formula sheet

Undamped forcing

\(\ddot x + \omega_n^2x = f_0\cos\omega t\), \(f_0 = F_0/m\)

\(X = f_0/(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)\)

\(x = \frac{v_0}{\omega_n}\sin\omega_nt + (x_0 - X)\cos\omega_nt + X\cos\omega t\)

Beats and resonance

From rest: \(x = \frac{2f_0}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2}\sin\frac{\omega_n - \omega}{2}t\,\sin\frac{\omega_n + \omega}{2}t\)

\(\omega_{beat} = |\omega_n - \omega|\)

\(\omega = \omega_n\): \(x_p = \frac{f_0}{2\omega}t\sin\omega t\)

Damped forcing

\(X = f_0/\sqrt{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}\)

\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\big(2\zeta\omega_n\omega/(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)\big)\), 0 to \(\pi\)

\(x = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\sin(\omega_dt + \phi) + X\cos(\omega t - \theta)\)

\(\phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega_d(x_0 - X\cos\theta)}{v_0 + (x_0 - X\cos\theta)\zeta\omega_n - \omega X\sin\theta}\), \(A = \frac{x_0 - X\cos\theta}{\sin\phi}\)

Normalised

\(r = \omega/\omega_n\), \(Xk/F_0 = 1/\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}\)

\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\big(2\zeta r/(1 - r^2)\big)\)

At \(r = 1\): \(Xk/F_0 = 1/2\zeta\), \(\theta = 90°\)

Peak at \(r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\), height \(1/(2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2})\)
