Coulomb damping, energy methods and modelling

Free vibration with dry friction, deriving equations of motion from energy, equivalent stiffness and inertia, and the worked additional problems of Tutorial 1.

01Coulomb (dry friction) damping

Viscous damping (week 1) is a convenient model, but many real systems lose energy through dry sliding friction instead. This is Coulomb damping. Its friction force has a constant size \(\mu N\) and always opposes the velocity, which makes the equation of motion nonlinear and piecewise.

Figure 1.1: The mass has been pulled to the right and released, so it moves left (\(\dot x \lt 0\)). The spring force \(kx\) acts to the left and the friction force \(\mu N\) opposes the motion, so it acts to the right. The normal force \(N\) balances the weight, \(N = mg\).

With \(\mu\) the kinetic (dynamic) coefficient of friction and \(\mu_s\) the static coefficient, the friction force is

Coulomb friction force\[ f_f = \begin{cases} -\mu N & \dot x \gt 0 \\ 0 \ldots \mu_s N & \dot x = 0 \\ \mu N & \dot x \lt 0 \end{cases} \]
\(f_f\)
Friction force on the mass, positive to the right (N).
\(\mu\)
Kinetic coefficient of friction.
\(N\)
Normal force (N); \(N = mg\) on a horizontal surface.
\(\mu_s\)
Static coefficient of friction: while the mass is at rest, friction can supply anything up to \(\mu_sN\).
\(\dot x\)
Velocity of the mass (m/s).

Newton's second law, \(m\ddot x = -kx + f_f\), gives the equation of motion. Written with the signum function it is one line:

Equation of motion with Coulomb damping\[ m\ddot x + \mu N\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot x) + kx = 0, \qquad \mathrm{sgn}(\dot x) = \begin{cases} -1 & \dot x \lt 0 \\ 0 & \dot x = 0 \\ 1 & \dot x \gt 0 \end{cases} \]
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(\ddot x\)
Acceleration (m/s²).
\(\mu N\)
Size of the kinetic friction force (N).
\(\mathrm{sgn}(\dot x)\)
Sign of the velocity: switches the friction direction.
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(x\)
Displacement from the unstretched spring position (m).

Solving it one half cycle at a time

Within one stroke the velocity does not change sign, so the friction force is constant and the equation is linear: an undamped oscillator with a constant force. Its solution is the free solution (homogeneous part) plus a constant (particular part) \(\pm\mu N/k\), the static deflection the friction force alone would cause.

Release from rest at \(x_0 \gt 0\): the mass moves left, \(\dot x \lt 0\), so \(m\ddot x + kx = \mu N\). With \(x(0) = x_0\) and \(\dot x(0) = 0\):

First half cycle\[ x(t) = \left(x_0 - \frac{\mu N}{k}\right)\cos\omega_n t + \frac{\mu N}{k}, \qquad 0 \le t \le \frac{\pi}{\omega_n} \]
\(x_0\)
Initial displacement, released from rest (m).
\(\mu N/k\)
Offset caused by friction (m): the centre of this half cycle.
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency \(\sqrt{k/m}\) (rad/s).
\(t\)
Time (s).

The velocity \(\dot x = -\omega_n(x_0 - \mu N/k)\sin\omega_n t\) is next zero at \(t = \pi/\omega_n\), where \(x = -(x_0 - 2\mu N/k)\). The friction then flips, \(m\ddot x + kx = -\mu N\), and the second stroke starts from this new rest point:

\[ x(t) = \left(x_0 - \frac{3\mu N}{k}\right)\cos\omega_n t - \frac{\mu N}{k}, \qquad \frac{\pi}{\omega_n} \le t \le \frac{2\pi}{\omega_n}, \]

which ends at \(x = x_0 - 4\mu N/k\). The pattern repeats: each stroke is half a cosine about \(\pm\mu N/k\), and every turning point is \(2\mu N/k\) closer to zero than the last.

Coulomb decay\[ \Delta x_{\text{half cycle}} = \frac{2\mu N}{k}, \qquad \Delta x_{\text{cycle}} = \frac{4\mu N}{k}, \qquad \text{envelope slope} = -\frac{2\mu N\omega_n}{\pi k} \]
\(\Delta x\)
Loss of amplitude (m) per half cycle or per full cycle.
\(\mu N\)
Kinetic friction force (N).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).
Key idea

Coulomb damping: the oscillation frequency stays exactly \(\omega_n\) (viscous damping lowers it to \(\omega_d\)); the peaks fall on straight lines, not an exponential; and the motion stops after a finite time, usually away from equilibrium.

When does it stop?

At each turning point the mass is momentarily at rest and only the spring pulls on it. It moves off again only if the spring force beats static friction, \(k|x| \gt \mu_sN\). So the motion stops at the first turning point inside the band

Stick condition\[ |x| \le \frac{\mu_s N}{k} \]
\(x\)
Displacement at a turning point (m).
\(\mu_s N\)
Largest static friction force (N).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
Watch out

The mass does not come to rest at the spring's unstretched position. It sticks wherever the last turning point falls inside the band. If only one friction coefficient is given, use \(\mu_s = \mu\), as the lecture example does.

Worked example 1.1 Coulomb decay

A 2 kg mass slides on a surface with \(\mu = 0.1\) and is held by a spring of 800 N/m. It is released from rest at 30 mm. Take \(\mu_s = \mu\). Find the loss per cycle, the turning points, where it stops and when.

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{800/2} = 20\) rad/s; \(\mu N/k = 0.1(2)(9.81)/800 = 2.453\) mm.
2
Each half cycle loses \(2\mu N/k = 4.91\) mm, each cycle \(9.81\) mm.
3
Turning points: 30.00, -25.09, 20.19, -15.29, 10.38, -5.47, 0.57 mm.
4
The last value, 0.57 mm, is inside the stick band ±2.45 mm, so the mass stops there; the one before it (-5.47 mm) was outside the band.
5
That is 6 half cycles: \(t = 6\pi/\omega_n = 0.942\) s.
The mass loses 9.81 mm per cycle, makes 6 strokes and stops at 0.57 mm after 0.942 s.

Figure 1.2: The peaks (amber) lie on the two dashed straight lines of slope \(\mp 2\mu N\omega_n/(\pi k)\). Each stroke is half a cosine centred at \(+\mu N/k\) or \(-\mu N/k\). The mass stops at its sixth turning point, inside the shaded stick band, and stays there.

Figure 1.3: It starts at worked example 1.1 (m = 2 kg, ωₙ = 20 rad/s, μ = μₛ = 0.1, x₀ = 30 mm, so μN/k = 2.45 mm). Raise μₛ above μ to widen the stick band: the mass then stops earlier and further from equilibrium. The faint curve is a viscously damped response with the same loss in its first half cycle, for comparison: its peaks decay exponentially rather than along a straight line.

Because the decay is linear and its slope is \(2\mu N\omega_n/(\pi k)\), a measured decay gives the friction: read the slope, and if \(N\), \(k\) and \(\omega_n\) are known, solve for \(\mu\).

Intuition

The lecture ends with two questions. Is Coulomb friction a convenient model? It is simple to state but nonlinear: you cannot superpose solutions, and every stroke must be solved separately. Is the motion smooth? The displacement and velocity are continuous (the velocity is zero at every switch), but the acceleration jumps by \(2\mu N/m\) at each turning point, where the friction force reverses.

02Modelling and energy methods

So far equations of motion came from Newton's second law, \(\sum F = m\ddot x\), or for rotation about a fixed axis \(\sum M = I\ddot\theta\). For conservative systems there is an alternative that avoids free-body diagrams and internal forces.

Definition

A conservative system neither gains nor loses energy through external forces: no damping, no friction, no applied force. Its total energy is constant.

Energy method\[ T + U = \text{constant} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}(T + U) = 0 \]
\(T\)
Total kinetic energy (J).
\(U\)
Total potential energy (J).
Exam

The lecturer notes that writing the principle \(\frac{{d}}{{dt}}(T + U) = 0\) in a test earns a mark. State it before you start differentiating.

Watch out

Damping and friction remove energy, so the energy method as used here applies only to conservative, single-degree-of-freedom systems. Leave the damper out, find the undamped equation of motion, then add damping by Newton if needed.

EnergyExpressionNotes
Spring\(U = \tfrac12 k(\delta_{st} + x)^2\)Total deflection from the free length
Gravity\(U = mgh\)\(h\) is the height above a chosen reference
Translation\(T = \tfrac12 mv^2\)\(v\) is the speed of the centre of mass
Rotation\(T = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\)About the centre of mass (plus translation), or about a fixed pivot alone

Recipe

  1. Choose one coordinate (\(x\) or \(\theta\)) and write every other motion in terms of it (no-slip, rope and lever relations).
  2. Write \(T\) for every moving mass: translation and rotation.
  3. Write \(U\) for every spring and for gravity, from a stated reference.
  4. Differentiate \(T + U\) with respect to time, divide by \(\dot x\) (or \(\dot\theta\)), and use static equilibrium to cancel the constant terms.
  5. Linearise for small angles: \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\), \(\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2\) inside energies.
Worked example 2.1 The spring-mass system by energy

Derive the equation of motion of the hanging spring-mass system of week 1 using energy, with the unstretched spring position as reference.

1
\(T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2\); spring \(U = \tfrac12 k(\delta_{st} + x)^2\); gravity \(U = -mg(\delta_{st} + x)\) (the mass is below the reference).
2
\(\frac{d}{dt}(T + U) = m\dot x\ddot x + k(\delta_{st} + x)\dot x - mg\dot x = 0\).
3
Divide by \(\dot x\): \(m\ddot x + kx + (k\delta_{st} - mg) = 0\), and \(k\delta_{st} = mg\).
\(m\ddot x + kx = 0\): the same result as Newton's law.
Shortcut

If the energies reduce to \(T = \tfrac12 m_{eq}\dot x^2\) and \(U = \tfrac12 k_{eq}x^2\) (about equilibrium), the method always gives \(m_{eq}\ddot x + k_{eq}x = 0\), so \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_{eq}/m_{eq}}\). The tutorial solutions write answers in this \(m_{eq}\), \(k_{eq}\) form.

Example: a disc rolling in a half-pipe

A uniform disc of mass \(m\) and radius \(r\) rolls without slipping inside a circular track of radius \(R\). Find its natural frequency for small swings.

Figure 2.1: The angle \(\theta\) locates the disc centre on its circular path of radius \(R - r\). The angle \(\varphi\) is the rotation of the disc itself. Rolling without slip links them: \(r\dot\varphi = (R - r)\dot\theta\).

Energies of the rolling disc\[ U = mg(R-r)(1-\cos\theta), \quad T_{\text{roll}} = \tfrac12 I_C\dot\varphi^2 = \tfrac14 mr^2\dot\varphi^2, \quad T_{\text{trans}} = \tfrac12 m(R-r)^2\dot\theta^2 \]
\(U\)
Gravitational potential energy, zero at the bottom (J).
\(I_C\)
Moment of inertia of the disc about its centre, \(\tfrac12 mr^2\) (kg m²).
\(\dot\varphi\)
Spin rate of the disc (rad/s).
\(\dot\theta\)
Rate of swing of the centre about the track centre (rad/s).
\(R, r\)
Track and disc radii (m).

Differentiate, use \(r\dot\varphi = (R-r)\dot\theta\) so that \(\tfrac12 mr^2\dot\varphi\ddot\varphi = \tfrac12 m(R-r)^2\dot\theta\ddot\theta\), and divide by \(\dot\theta\):

\[ \tfrac32 m(R-r)^2\ddot\theta + mg(R-r)\sin\theta = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tfrac32(R-r)\ddot\theta + g\theta = 0. \]
Disc in a circular track\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2g}{3(R-r)}} \]
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency for small swings (rad/s).
\(g\)
Gravitational acceleration (m/s²).
\(R - r\)
Radius of the path of the disc centre (m).
Worked example 2.2 Numbers for the half-pipe

A disc of radius 0.1 m rolls in a track of radius 1.0 m. Find \(\omega_n\) and the period.

1
\(R - r = 0.9\) m.
2
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{2(9.81)/(3 \times 0.9)} = 2.696\) rad/s.
3
\(T = 2\pi/\omega_n = 2.331\) s.
\(\omega_n = 2.70\) rad/s, period 2.33 s. A point mass sliding without friction would have \(\sqrt{g/(R-r)} = 3.30\) rad/s: the spin energy makes the rolling disc slower.

Typical test question: disc, pulley and hanging mass

A thin disc of mass \(m\) and radius \(3r\) rolls without slipping and is attached to a spring \(k\). It is connected to a pulley (inner radius \(r\), outer radius \(2r\), inertia \(I_p\)). A box of mass \(2m\) hangs from the outer radius and rests on a spring \(2k\). Use the pulley angle \(\theta\) as the coordinate.

  • Kinematics. The rope on the inner radius moves \(r\theta\): the disc centre and its spring move \(r\theta\), so the disc turns \(r\theta/3r = \theta/3\). The box moves \(2r\theta\).
  • Static equilibrium. With \(T_1 = kr\theta_0\) and \(T_2 = 2mg - 4kr\theta_0\), moments on the pulley give \(rT_1 - 2rT_2 = 0\), so \(\theta_0 = 4mg/(9kr)\).
  • Potential energy. \(U = \tfrac12 k(r\theta + r\theta_0)^2 + \tfrac12(2k)(2r\theta + 2r\theta_0)^2 - 2mg(2r\theta + 2r\theta_0)\).
  • Kinetic energy. Disc rotation \(\tfrac12(\tfrac12 m(3r)^2)(\dot\theta/3)^2 = \tfrac14 mr^2\dot\theta^2\); disc translation \(\tfrac12 m(r\dot\theta)^2\); pulley \(\tfrac12 I_p\dot\theta^2\); box \(\tfrac12(2m)(2r\dot\theta)^2 = 4mr^2\dot\theta^2\). Total \(T = \tfrac{19}{4}mr^2\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12 I_p\dot\theta^2\).
  • Differentiate. \(9kr^2\theta\dot\theta + (9kr^2\theta_0 - 4mgr)\dot\theta + (\tfrac{19}{2}mr^2 + I_p)\dot\theta\ddot\theta = 0\). The bracket is zero by static equilibrium.
Equation of motion of the test question\[ \left(\tfrac{19}{2}mr^2 + I_p\right)\ddot\theta + 9kr^2\theta = 0, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{9kr^2}{\tfrac{19}{2}mr^2 + I_p}} \]
\(m\)
Disc mass (kg); the box is \(2m\).
\(r\)
Inner pulley radius (m).
\(I_p\)
Pulley moment of inertia (kg m²).
\(k\)
Stiffness of the disc spring (N/m); the box spring is \(2k\).
\(\theta\)
Pulley rotation from static equilibrium (rad).
Watch out

The lecture notes say "substituting equation 34 into equation 37"; they mean equation 21 (\(\theta_0\)) into equation 24. Gravity always drops out once you measure from static equilibrium: the terms that do not multiply \(\theta\) cancel.

Exam

This disc-pulley-mass layout is the lecture's "typical test question". Practise writing each kinetic energy term with its kinematic link stated; most marks are lost on \(\dot\theta/3\) and \(2r\dot\theta\) type relations.

Figure 2.3: Parallel springs share the same deflection and their forces add. Series springs carry the same force and their deflections add.

\(k_1, k_2\)
Individual stiffnesses (N/m).

Worked example 2.3 Combining stiffnesses

A steel helical spring (\(G = 80\) GPa, \(d = 5\) mm, coil radius \(R = 25\) mm, 10 coils) is attached to the tip of a steel cantilever (\(E = 200\) GPa, 300 mm long, 20 mm wide, 5 mm thick, bending about the thin direction). Find each stiffness and the combination in series and in parallel.

1
Spring: \(k_s = \dfrac{80\times10^9(0.005)^4}{64(10)(0.025)^3} = 5000\) N/m.
2
Beam: \(I = bh^3/12 = 0.02(0.005)^3/12 = 2.083\times10^{-10}\) m⁴; \(k_b = 3(200\times10^9)(2.083\times10^{-10})/0.3^3 = 4630\) N/m.
3
Series (mass on the spring, spring on the beam tip): \(k = 5000(4630)/(5000 + 4630) = 2404\) N/m.
4
Parallel (both acting on the mass): \(k = 5000 + 4630 = 9630\) N/m.
Series 2404 N/m, parallel 9630 N/m. In series the softer element dominates; in parallel the stiffer one does.

03Tutorial 1

Tutorial 1 covers free vibration of SDOF systems. The partial solutions list what the tutorial is meant to teach:

  • Start every problem with a free-body diagram showing all forces and distances.
  • Choose coordinates from static equilibrium so gravity drops out, giving a homogeneous equation.
  • Decide whether a sum of forces or a sum of moments is more convenient; for moments, know the inertias of point masses, rods and discs.
  • Linearise with \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\), \(\cos\theta \approx 1\).
  • Read \(\omega_n\) (or \(\omega_d\)), \(A\) and \(\phi\) from the solution and connect them to a sketch.
  • Know how viscous and Coulomb decays differ, and use the decay to find damping or friction. For Coulomb damping, know where the mass finally rests.
  • The energy method applies only to conservative SDOF systems.

Mass \(m = 4\) kg with \(k_1 = k_2 = 26\) N/m in parallel on one side and \(k_3 = k_4 = 96\) N/m in series on the other. \(x_0 = 0.1\) m, \(v_0 = -0.1\) m/s. Find the equation of motion, \(f_n\) and \(x(t)\).

\(A = \sqrt{5^2(0.1)^2 + 0.1^2}/5 = 0.1020\) m. \(v_0 \lt 0\), so \(\phi = \pi - \tan^{-1}(0.5/0.1) = 1.768\) rad.

Rod (\(\rho = 2\) kg/m, \(l = 2\) m) hinged at one end, spring \(k_1 = 1000\) N/m at the tip, mass \(m = 2\) kg at the middle with spring \(k_2 = 200\) N/m and damper \(c = 50\) N s/m. \(\theta(0) = \pi/12\), \(\dot\theta(0) = 0\).

4
\(\theta(t) = 0.2645\,e^{-3.409t}\sin(23.69t + 1.428)\) rad.

Watch out

In AP3 the springs are unstretched with the rod horizontal, so gravity on the rod and mass is not balanced there. Measured from horizontal, the equation carries a constant term \((\rho l\cdot l/2 + ml/2)g = 58.9\) N m, which shifts equilibrium by \(58.9/4200 = 0.014\) rad (0.8°). The partial solution leaves it out; the frequency and damping ratio are unaffected.

Worked example AP4 Pulley with Coulomb friction

Mass \(m_1 = 1\) kg on a rough surface (\(\mu = 0.4\)) is tied to a wall by \(k_1 = 200\) N/m and by a rope to radius \(r/2\) of a pulley (\(M = 0.5\) kg solid disc, \(r = 0.25\) m). Mass \(m_2 = 2\) kg hangs from radius \(r\) on springs \(k_2 = k_3 = 30\) N/m. \(\theta(0) = \pi/10\), \(\dot\theta(0) = 0\).

1
(a) One degree of freedom: with inextensible ropes, the pulley angle \(\theta\) fixes both masses (\(x_1 = r\theta/2\), \(x_2 = r\theta\)).
2
(b) Moments about the pulley axis, from static equilibrium: \(J\ddot\theta + \mu m_1g\tfrac{r}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot\theta) + K\theta = 0\), with \(J = \tfrac12 Mr^2 + \tfrac14 m_1r^2 + m_2r^2\) and \(K = \tfrac14 k_1r^2 + (k_2 + k_3)r^2\).
3
\(J = 0.15625\) kg m², \(K = 6.875\) N m/rad, friction torque \(0.4905\) N m. \(\omega_n = 6.633\) rad/s; friction offset \(0.0713\) rad.
4
(c, d) Turning points: 0.3142, -0.1715, 0.0288 rad. The last is inside the band, so the pulley sticks after 2 strokes, at \(t = 2\pi/\omega_n = 0.947\) s.
5
(e) Frictionless, by energy: \(T = \tfrac12 J\dot\theta^2\), \(U = \tfrac12 K\theta^2\), so \(J\ddot\theta + K\theta = 0\) and \(\omega_n = 6.633\) rad/s (1.056 Hz).
The pulley stops at \(\theta = 0.0288\) rad after 0.95 s.

04Practice problems

Problem 4.1. A 5 kg block on a 2 kN/m spring slides with \(\mu = \mu_s = 0.15\). Released from rest at 20 mm, how many strokes does it make, where does it stop, and when?

\(\omega_n = 20\) rad/s; \(\mu N/k = 0.15(5)(9.81)/2000 = 3.679\) mm, so each stroke loses 7.357 mm. Turning points: 20.000, -12.643, 5.285, 2.072 mm. The last stroke does not even cross zero: it swings about +μN/k = 3.679 mm from 5.285 mm to 2.072 mm. That point is inside ±3.679 mm, so it stops there after 3 strokes, at \(t = 3\pi/20 = 0.471\) s.

Problem 4.2. A measured free decay drops from 40 mm to 16 mm in 3 full cycles, losing the same amount each cycle. The system has \(k = 5\) kN/m, \(m = 10\) kg. Is it viscous or Coulomb damped, and what is \(\mu\)?

A constant loss per cycle means Coulomb damping. \(4\mu N/k = (40 - 16)/3 = 8\) mm per cycle, so \(\mu = 0.008(5000)/(4 \times 10 \times 9.81) = 0.102\).

Problem 4.3. A solid cylinder (mass \(m\), radius \(r\)) rolls without slipping on a flat floor, with a spring \(k\) attached at its axle. Find \(\omega_n\) by the energy method, and evaluate it for \(m = 3\) kg, \(k = 900\) N/m.

\(T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12(\tfrac12 mr^2)(\dot x/r)^2 = \tfrac34 m\dot x^2\); \(U = \tfrac12 kx^2\). So \(\tfrac32 m\ddot x + kx = 0\) and \(\omega_n = \sqrt{2k/3m} = \sqrt{1800/9} = 14.14\) rad/s. The radius does not appear.

Problem 4.4. A uniform rod of length 0.6 m swings about one end. Find its period for small swings.

\(\tfrac13 ml^2\ddot\theta + mg\tfrac{l}{2}\theta = 0\), so \(\omega_n = \sqrt{3g/2l} = \sqrt{3(9.81)/1.2} = 4.952\) rad/s and \(T = 1.269\) s.

Problem 4.5. Two springs of 300 N/m and 600 N/m support a 2 kg mass, first in series, then in parallel. Find both natural frequencies.

Series: \(k = 300(600)/900 = 200\) N/m, \(\omega_n = 10\) rad/s. Parallel: \(k = 900\) N/m, \(\omega_n = \sqrt{450} = 21.21\) rad/s.

Problem 4.6. A 3 kg block on a 1200 N/m spring has \(\mu = 0.2\). It is at rest at \(x = 4\) mm from the unstretched position. Will it move if \(\mu_s = 0.25\)?

Spring force \(1200(0.004) = 4.8\) N. Static friction can supply up to \(0.25(3)(9.81) = 7.36\) N. Since 4.8 N < 7.36 N, the block stays put. (Equivalently, 4 mm is inside \(\mu_sN/k = 6.13\) mm.)

Problem 4.7. Explain why the half-pipe disc oscillates more slowly than a bead sliding without friction on the same circle.

Both have the same restoring potential \(mg(R-r)(1-\cos\theta)\), but the rolling disc stores extra kinetic energy in spin, \(\tfrac14 m(R-r)^2\dot\theta^2\). Its effective inertia is 1.5 times larger, so \(\omega_n\) is \(1/\sqrt{1.5} = 0.816\) times the bead's.

05Formula sheet

Coulomb damping

\(m\ddot x + \mu N\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot x) + kx = 0\)

Stroke: \(x = (x_i \mp \mu N/k)\cos\omega_n(t - t_i) \pm \mu N/k\)

Loss \(2\mu N/k\) per half cycle, \(4\mu N/k\) per cycle

Envelope slope \(-2\mu N\omega_n/(\pi k)\); stops when \(|x| \le \mu_sN/k\)

Frequency stays \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}\)

Energy method

\(T + U = \text{const}\), \(\frac{d}{dt}(T + U) = 0\)

\(U = \tfrac12 k(\delta_{st} + x)^2\), \(U = mgh\)

\(T = \tfrac12 mv^2\), \(T = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\)

\(T = \tfrac12 m_{eq}\dot x^2\), \(U = \tfrac12 k_{eq}x^2 \Rightarrow \omega_n = \sqrt{k_{eq}/m_{eq}}\)

Disc in a track: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{2g/3(R-r)}\)

Stiffness

Parallel \(k_1 + k_2\); series \(k_1k_2/(k_1 + k_2)\)

Bar \(EA/l\); torsion \(GJ_p/l\)

Helical \(Gd^4/(64nR^3)\); cantilever \(3EI/l^3\)

Inertia

Point \(ml^2\); rod end \(\tfrac13 ml^2\); rod centre \(\tfrac1{12}ml^2\)

Disc \(\tfrac12 mr^2\); \(I_O = I_C + md^2\)

Rolling: \(v = r\omega\)
