Distributed parameter systems: strings, rods and shafts

The wave equation for a taut string, its mode shapes and natural frequencies by separation of variables, the response to initial conditions through orthogonality, and the same equation for rods and shafts.

01From lumped to distributed systems

Until now systems were lumped: mass concentrated at discrete points, joined by massless springs, with a finite number of degrees of freedom (SDOF or MDOF). Real structures are continuous: mass and stiffness are spread over the body. In a distributed parameter system the motion is a continuous function of position, \(w(x, t)\), so there are infinitely many degrees of freedom and infinitely many natural frequencies.

Key idea

Lumped models are simplifications of continuous systems. The continuous models of this week (strings, rods, shafts, and beams next week) show where lumped models come from and when they stop being accurate.

02The wave equation for a string

The simplest continuous system is a string or cable stretched with tension \(\tau\) between two supports, carrying a distributed transverse load \(f(x, t)\) (force per unit length). Its transverse displacement is \(w(x, t)\).

Figure 2.1: The element between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) has mass \(\rho\Delta x\). The tension acts along the string at each end, at angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) to the horizontal; the load \(f\Delta x\) acts transversely.

The derivation follows four steps: free-body diagram, sum of forces, limit, rearrange. Summing transverse forces on the element:

Transverse force balance\[ -\tau_1\sin\theta_1 + \tau_2\sin\theta_2 + f(x, t)\Delta x = \rho\Delta x\frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial t^2} \]
\(\tau_1, \tau_2\)
Tension at the two ends of the element (N).
\(\theta_1, \theta_2\)
Slope angles of the string at the two ends (rad).
\(f(x, t)\)
Distributed transverse force per unit length (N/m).
\(\rho\)
Linear density, mass per unit length (kg/m).
\(w(x, t)\)
Transverse displacement (m).

For small angles \(\cos\theta \approx 1\), so the horizontal balance gives \(\tau_1 = \tau_2 = \tau\), and \(\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = \partial w/\partial x\). Dividing by \(\Delta x\):

\[ \frac{\tau}{\Delta x}\left[\frac{\partial w(x + \Delta x, t)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial w(x, t)}{\partial x}\right] + f(x, t) = \rho\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial t^2}. \]

As \(\Delta x \to 0\) the bracket divided by \(\Delta x\) becomes \(\partial^2w/\partial x^2\) (definition of the derivative). With no external load:

One-dimensional wave equation\[ \frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial t^2}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\rho}} \]
\(c\)
Wave speed along the string (m/s).
\(\tau\)
Tension (N).
\(\rho\)
Mass per unit length (kg/m).

It is a partial differential equation, second order in space and in time. Its solution needs two boundary conditions (one at each end) and two initial conditions (initial displacement and velocity everywhere).

Watch out

The notes say Inman's equation 6.1 "uses a volume density with units [kg/m]". A volume density has units kg/m³; Inman multiplies it by the cross-sectional area \(A\) to get the linear density in kg/m. On this page \(\rho\) is the linear density for strings, and the volume density for rods and shafts (section 5).

03Modes and natural frequencies

Solve by separation of variables: assume the shape and the time behaviour factor, \(w(x, t) = X(x)T(t)\). Substituting and dividing by \(XT\):

\[ \frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\ddot T(t)}{T(t)} = -\sigma^2. \]

The left side depends only on \(x\) and the right only on \(t\), so both equal the same constant, written \(-\sigma^2\). A positive or zero constant is also possible in principle, but with fixed ends it only gives the trivial solution \(w = 0\) (Inman example 6.2.1), so the negative choice, which gives oscillation, is the useful one.

Spatial solution: mode shapes

\(X'' + \sigma^2X = 0\) is the SDOF equation again, in \(x\): \(X = a_1\sin\sigma x + a_2\cos\sigma x\). A string fixed at both ends has \(X(0) = 0\) and \(X(l) = 0\). The first gives \(a_2 = 0\); the second gives the characteristic equation \(a_1\sin\sigma l = 0\), with infinitely many roots:

Mode shapes of a fixed-fixed string\[ \sigma_n = \frac{n\pi}{l}, \qquad X_n(x) = a_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]
\(\sigma_n\)
Spatial frequency (wavenumber) of mode \(n\) (rad/m).
\(X_n\)
Mode shape \(n\): \(n\) half-sine waves along the string.
\(l\)
Length between supports (m).

Temporal solution: natural frequencies

For each \(\sigma_n\), the time equation is \(\ddot T_n + \sigma_n^2c^2T_n = 0\), an undamped oscillator with \(T_n = A_n\sin\omega_nt + B_n\cos\omega_nt\) and

Natural frequencies of a fixed-fixed string\[ \omega_n = c\sigma_n = \frac{n\pi}{l}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\rho}}, \qquad f_n = \frac{n}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\rho}} \]
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency of mode \(n\) (rad/s).
\(f_n\)
In hertz: every frequency is a whole multiple of the fundamental \(f_1\).
General free response\[ w(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[c_n\sin\sigma_nx\,\sin\sigma_nct + d_n\sin\sigma_nx\,\cos\sigma_nct\right] \]
\(c_n, d_n\)
Constants (m), combining \(a_n\), \(A_n\), \(B_n\); set by the initial velocity and displacement.

Figure 3.1: Mode \(n\) has \(n\) half-waves and \(n - 1\) nodes (amber): interior points that do not move in that mode.

Worked example 3.1 Tuning a guitar string

A guitar string of vibrating length 0.65 m and linear density 5.5 g/m must sound at 110 Hz (the A string). Find the wave speed, the tension, and the first four natural frequencies.

1
\(f_1 = c/(2l)\), so \(c = 2lf_1 = 2(0.65)(110) = 143\) m/s.
2
\(\tau = \rho c^2 = 0.0055(143)^2 = 112.5\) N.
3
\(f_n = nf_1\): 110, 220, 330 and 440 Hz.
Tension about 112 N. Doubling the tension raises the pitch by \(\sqrt2\) (about six semitones); halving the length doubles it.
Worked example 3.2 Power line

A 300 m span of power line weighs 1.6 kg/m and is under 20 kN tension. Find its first three natural frequencies.

1
\(c = \sqrt{20000/1.6} = 111.8\) m/s.
2
\(f_n = nc/(2l) = n(111.8)/600\): 0.186, 0.373, 0.559 Hz.
Well below 1 Hz: long cables are easily excited by wind, which is why Stockbridge dampers (week 8) are hung on them.

04Response from initial conditions

The mode shapes are orthogonal over the length:

Orthogonality of the string modes\[ \int_0^l\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{l}\,dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n \neq m \\ l/2, & n = m \end{cases} \]
\(n, m\)
Mode numbers.

At \(t = 0\), \(w(x, 0) = \sum d_n\sin\sigma_nx\). Multiply both sides by \(\sin(m\pi x/l)\) and integrate: every term but \(n = m\) vanishes. The same works for the initial velocity \(\dot w(x, 0) = \sum c_n\sigma_nc\sin\sigma_nx\):

Coefficients from the initial conditions\[ d_n = \frac{2}{l}\int_0^lw(x, 0)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}\,dx, \qquad c_n = \frac{2}{n\pi c}\int_0^l\dot w(x, 0)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}\,dx \]
\(w(x, 0)\)
Initial displacement shape (m).
\(\dot w(x, 0)\)
Initial velocity distribution (m/s).
\(d_n, c_n\)
Amount of mode \(n\) in the displacement and velocity starts (m).
Worked example 4.1 String plucked at the centre

A string of length \(l\) is pulled up by \(h = 5\) mm at its midpoint and released from rest. Find the modal coefficients.

1
Released from rest: \(\dot w(x, 0) = 0\), so all \(c_n = 0\).
2
The triangle \(w(x, 0) = 2hx/l\) for \(x \le l/2\) (mirror image beyond) gives, after integrating by parts, \(d_n = \dfrac{8h}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\dfrac{n\pi}{2}\).
3
\(d_1 = 4.053\) mm, \(d_2 = 0\), \(d_3 = -0.450\) mm, \(d_4 = 0\), \(d_5 = 0.162\) mm, \(d_7 = -0.0827\) mm (checked by numerical integration).
\(w(x, t) = \sum d_n\sin(n\pi x/l)\cos(n\pi ct/l)\) with only odd modes: the even modes have a node at the centre, where the string was plucked, so they are not excited. The amplitudes fall as \(1/n^2\), so a few modes give a good approximation.

Figure 4.1: The string is pulled up 5 mm at x = a and released from rest. Teal: the sum of the first N modes; grey: the same with 400 modes, effectively exact. At t = 0 the triangle needs many modes for its sharp corner, but three already give the overall shape. Slide t to watch the corner split into two that travel and reflect. Pluck at a node of a mode (for example a/l = 0.5 for the even modes) and that mode is absent.

Important points

The free response of a string (or any structure) is a weighted sum, a superposition, of its mode shapes, with the weights set by the initial conditions. A finite number of modes approximates the response. A point that does not move in a mode is a node; a structure can be driven at a frequency where a chosen point does not move, either because it is a node of the mode at that frequency or because the frequency is an anti-resonance for that point.

05Rods and shafts

Longitudinal vibration of a rod (bar) and torsional vibration of a shaft obey the same wave equation; only the wave speed changes:

Longitudinal vibration of a rod\[ \frac{E}{\rho}\frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial t^2}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}} \]
\(w(x, t)\)
Axial displacement of the cross-section at \(x\) (m).
\(E\)
Young's modulus (Pa).
\(\rho\)
Volume density (kg/m³).
Torsional vibration of a shaft\[ \frac{G\gamma}{\rho J}\frac{\partial^2\theta(x, t)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2\theta(x, t)}{\partial t^2}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{G\gamma}{\rho J}} \]
\(\theta(x, t)\)
Angle of twist of the cross-section at \(x\) (rad).
\(G\)
Shear modulus (Pa).
\(\gamma\)
Torsional constant of the cross-section (m⁴); \(\gamma = J\) for a circular section, so \(c = \sqrt{G/\rho}\).
\(J\)
Polar second moment of area (m⁴).

The mode shapes and frequencies come from the boundary conditions, exactly as for the string. A fixed end has zero displacement, \(X = 0\). A free end carries no force, so the strain is zero: \(X' = 0\).

EndsCharacteristic equationMode shapes\(\omega_n\)
Fixed-fixed\(\sin\sigma l = 0\)\(\sin(n\pi x/l)\)\(n\pi c/l\)
Fixed-free\(\cos\sigma l = 0\)\(\sin((2n - 1)\pi x/2l)\)\((2n - 1)\pi c/(2l)\)
Free-free\(\sin\sigma l = 0\)\(\cos(n\pi x/l)\), plus a rigid body mode\(n\pi c/l\), and \(\omega_0 = 0\)

The notes give only the two wave equations; the boundary conditions and the table follow Inman 6.3 and are included so that the equations can be used.

Worked example 5.1 Steel rod and shaft

A 1 m steel rod (\(E = 200\) GPa, \(\rho = 7850\) kg/m³) is clamped at one end and free at the other. Find its first two longitudinal natural frequencies. Repeat for torsion of a 0.5 m circular steel shaft (\(G = 80\) GPa), fixed-free.

1
Rod: \(c = \sqrt{200\times10^9/7850} = 5048\) m/s.
2
Fixed-free: \(f_n = (2n - 1)c/(4l)\): \(f_1 = 1262\) Hz, \(f_2 = 3786\) Hz.
3
Shaft: \(c = \sqrt{G/\rho} = 3192\) m/s; \(f_1 = c/(4 \times 0.5) = 1596\) Hz.
About 1262 Hz and 3786 Hz axially, 1596 Hz in torsion. Waves in solid steel travel some 35 times faster than on the guitar string of example 3.1, so the frequencies of short metal parts are high.

06Practice problems

Problem 6.1. A 1.2 m cable with \(\rho = 0.02\) kg/m must have its fundamental at 50 Hz. What tension is needed?

\(c = 2lf_1 = 120\) m/s; \(\tau = \rho c^2 = 0.02(120)^2 = 288\) N.

Problem 6.2. Where are the nodes of mode 4 of a fixed-fixed string?

At \(x = l/4\), \(l/2\) and \(3l/4\) (and the fixed ends).

Problem 6.3. A string is plucked at \(x = l/4\). Which modes are missing from its response?

Those with a node at \(l/4\): \(\sin(n\pi/4) = 0\) for \(n = 4, 8, 12, \ldots\). (Try it in Figure 4.1.)

Problem 6.4. A string at rest is struck so that \(\dot w(x, 0) = v_0\sin(\pi x/l)\). Find \(w(x, t)\).

Only \(c_1\) is non-zero: \(c_1 = \frac{2}{\pi c}\int_0^lv_0\sin^2(\pi x/l)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi c}\cdot\frac{v_0l}{2} = \frac{v_0l}{\pi c}\). So \(w = \frac{v_0l}{\pi c}\sin\frac{\pi x}{l}\sin\frac{\pi ct}{l}\). For the guitar string with \(v_0 = 0.5\) m/s: amplitude 0.723 mm.

Problem 6.5. How do the natural frequencies of a fixed-free rod compare with those of the same rod fixed at both ends?

Fixed-free: \(f_n = (2n - 1)c/(4l)\), that is \(c/4l\), \(3c/4l\), \(5c/4l\). Fixed-fixed: \(c/2l\), \(c/l\), \(3c/2l\). The fundamental of the fixed-free rod is half that of the fixed-fixed rod, and only odd multiples of it appear.

Problem 6.6. Why does a continuous system have infinitely many natural frequencies, while a lumped model of it has only as many as its degrees of freedom?

Its displacement is a function \(w(x, t)\): infinitely many independent coordinates. A lumped model with \(N\) masses captures only the \(N\) lowest modes approximately, which is often enough because higher modes are harder to excite and decay faster.
