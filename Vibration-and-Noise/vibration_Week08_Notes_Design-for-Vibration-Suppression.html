Design for vibration suppression: isolation and absorbers

Acceptable vibration levels, isolating a device from a moving base or the floor from a machine, designing isolators by static deflection, and tuned vibration absorbers, with Tutorial 3 worked and checked.

01Why and how much to suppress

Weeks 1 to 7 predicted vibration. This week is about reducing it. Vibration may be undesirable because of:

  • its effect on people (health, safety and comfort);
  • its effect on machines (wear, malfunction, manufacturer's vibration limits);
  • its effect on surrounding structures (fatigue, disturbing neighbours).

Acceptable levels come from ISO standards, military specifications, or limits set by the designer or supplier, and are often plotted on a nomograph: a log-log chart of frequency against displacement, velocity and acceleration amplitude (week 1, Figure 7.2), with the acceptable region marked. Levels are usually expressed as the amplitude or rms of acceleration (human response and structures), velocity (machines and structures) or displacement. For harmonic motion, rms \(= A/\sqrt2\).

Range (Inman Table 5.1)Frequency (Hz)Displacement amplitude (mm)
Atomic vibration\(10^{12}\)\(10^{-7}\)
Threshold of human perception1 to 8\(10^{-2}\)
Machinery and building vibration10 to 100\(10^{-2}\) to 1
Swaying of tall buildings1 to 510 to 1000

There are two design routes. Isolation changes the dynamic characteristics (\(k\), \(m\), \(c\)) of the system, usually by adding suspension elements (isolators). Absorption adds a second mass-spring system tuned to take up the vibration.

Design is a compromise

"Design is a difficult subject that does not always lend itself to simple formulations. Design problems typically do not have a unique solution. Many different designs may all give acceptable answers." (Inman.) Cost, geometry, maximum force and static deflection all compete, and the tutorial solutions stress that there is no single correct answer.

02Vibration isolation

There are two isolation problems, and they use the same mathematics:

Figure 2.1: Problem 1 protects a system from a moving support (vehicle suspension, an instrument on a vibrating panel): the measure is the displacement transmissibility \(X/Y\) of week 4. Problem 2 protects the surroundings from a vibrating source (an engine, pump or screen on a plant floor): the measure is the force passed to the floor.

For problem 2 the mount is a spring and damper in parallel, and the force transmitted to the floor is \(F_T(t) = kx + c\dot x\). Dividing its amplitude by the force amplitude gives the force transmissibility ratio:

Transmissibility ratio\[ \text{T.R.} = \frac{F_T}{F_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} \]
\(F_T\)
Amplitude of the force transmitted to the floor (N).
\(F_0\)
Amplitude of the force generated by the machine (N).
\(r\)
Frequency ratio \(\omega/\omega_n\).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio of the mount.

It is identical to the displacement transmissibility \(X/Y\) of the base excitation problem. For problem 1 the force transmitted to the device is \(F_T = kYr^2\,\text{T.R.}\).

Figure 2.2: All curves pass through T.R. = 1 at \(r = \sqrt2\). Isolation (shaded) needs \(r \gt \sqrt2\), and there the smaller \(\zeta\) is, the smaller the transmissibility.

Key idea

Isolate by making the mount soft enough that the operating frequency is well above \(\sqrt2\,\omega_n\). Damping is unhelpful at the operating point but limits the resonance the machine passes through when it starts and stops.

Designing with static deflection

Isolators are catalogued by stiffness or by static deflection \(\Delta = mg/k\), and machines by their speed \(n\) in rpm. Since \(k\Delta = mg\), the natural frequency follows from the deflection alone: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/\Delta}\). Damping is often neglected at the design stage, which simplifies the transmissibility:

Undamped isolator design\[ \text{T.R.} = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1}\;(r \gt \sqrt2), \qquad R = 1 - \text{T.R.}, \qquad r = \sqrt{\frac{2 - R}{1 - R}}, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\Delta}} \]
\(R\)
Reduction in transmitted force, as a fraction (0.9 = 90 % isolation).
\(\Delta\)
Static deflection of the isolator under the machine weight (m).
\(g\)
9.81 m/s².

Other design considerations

  • Maximum allowable force: \(F_T = kYr^2\,\text{T.R.}\) (base problem) or \(F_0\,\text{T.R.}\) (source problem).
  • Static deflection: \(\delta = mg/k\). Soft mounts isolate well but sag.
  • Rattle space: the clearance needed around the isolated item, set by the static deflection plus the dynamic amplitude (\(\delta\) and \(X/Y\)).
VibrationShock

Sources of vibration include fluid-structure interaction (wind, vortex shedding, hydrodynamic forces, pressure waves; the Tacoma Narrows bridge collapse of 1940 is the classic case), rotating machinery, internal combustion engines and reciprocating compressors, vibrating machines (screens and exciters driven by rotating eccentric masses), impacts (crushers), earthquakes, and vibration carried through the ground by traffic or through buildings.

03Vibration absorbers

A vibration absorber (also called a tuned mass damper) is a second mass-spring system attached to the system to be protected. It turns a 1DOF system into a 2DOF one (an MDOF system into one with an extra DOF), tuned so that the added mass vibrates instead of the original one.

Figure 3.1: The lecture example is an optical table on legs (the primary mass on stiffness \(k\)) with an absorber \(m_a\), \(k_a\) attached. A harmonic force acts on the primary mass.

Equations of motion with an absorber\[ \begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m_a \end{bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix} \ddot x \\ \ddot x_a \end{Bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} k + k_a & -k_a \\ -k_a & k_a \end{bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix} x \\ x_a \end{Bmatrix} = \begin{Bmatrix} F_0\sin\omega t \\ 0 \end{Bmatrix} \]
\(m, k\)
Primary mass (kg) and stiffness (N/m).
\(m_a, k_a\)
Absorber mass (kg) and stiffness (N/m).
\(x, x_a\)
Displacements of the primary and absorber masses (m).

Try \(x = X\sin\omega t\), \(x_a = X_a\sin\omega t\), and invert the 2×2 matrix:

Steady-state amplitudes\[ X = \frac{(k_a - m_a\omega^2)F_0}{(k + k_a - m\omega^2)(k_a - m_a\omega^2) - k_a^2}, \qquad X_a = \frac{k_aF_0}{(k + k_a - m\omega^2)(k_a - m_a\omega^2) - k_a^2} \]
\(X\)
Amplitude of the primary mass (m).
\(X_a\)
Amplitude of the absorber mass (m).
\(F_0\)
Force amplitude (N).
Watch out

The Lecture 11 notes print the numerator of \(X\) as \((k_a - m\omega^2)F_0\). It is \((k_a - m_a\omega^2)F_0\), with the absorber mass, as on the slides and in the matrix inverse just above it.

Tuning

Choose \(k_a\) and \(m_a\) so that \(k_a - m_a\omega^2 = 0\), that is \(\omega_a = \sqrt{k_a/m_a} = \omega\). Then \(X = 0\): the primary mass does not move. The denominator becomes \(-k_a^2\), so

Tuned absorber\[ \frac{k_a}{m_a} = \omega^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad X = 0, \qquad X_a = -\frac{F_0}{k_a}, \qquad k_ax_a(t) = -F_0\sin\omega t \]
\(X_a\)
Absorber amplitude: negative means in antiphase with the force.
\(k_ax_a\)
Force the absorber spring applies to the primary mass (N): equal and opposite to the excitation.

Figure 3.2: Worked example 3.1: the force on the primary mass (amber) and the absorber (teal) move in antiphase, so the absorber spring cancels the force and the primary mass stays at rest.

A successful undamped absorber depends on:

  • the driving frequency being well known and not drifting much;
  • the absorber spring withstanding the full excitation force, \(k_a|X_a| = F_0\), and the absorber having room to move \(F_0/k_a\);
  • geometry and physical size;
  • little damping: the more damping, the less effective the absorber.

Normalised form and bandwidth

Define the mass ratio \(\mu = m_a/m\), the primary and absorber natural frequencies \(\omega_p = \sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\omega_a = \sqrt{k_a/m_a}\), and the frequency ratio \(\beta = \omega_a/\omega_p\), so that \(k_a/k = \mu\beta^2\). Dividing top and bottom by \(kk_a\):

Normalised primary amplitude\[ \frac{Xk}{F_0} = \frac{1 - (\omega/\omega_a)^2}{\left[1 + \mu\beta^2 - (\omega/\omega_p)^2\right]\left[1 - (\omega/\omega_a)^2\right] - \mu\beta^2} \]
\(\mu\)
Mass ratio \(m_a/m\), typically 0.05 to 0.25.
\(\beta\)
Frequency ratio \(\omega_a/\omega_p\); 1 when the absorber is tuned to the primary resonance.
\(\omega_p, \omega_a\)
Natural frequencies of the primary system alone and of the absorber alone (rad/s).
Watch out

The slide version has \(1 - \omega^2/\omega_n^2\) in the numerator; it should be \(\omega_a\), as above. The bracket structure in the lecture notes is also garbled, and the tutorial solutions point out that Inman's equation 5.24 has a misprint, corrected in his example 5.3.2. The form above follows directly from the matrix inverse.

Figure 3.3: It starts at the slide case, μ = 0.25 and β = 1. Teal: |Xk/F₀| with the absorber; grey: the primary system alone. The amplitude is zero at ω = ωₐ, and the shaded band is where it is below the static deflection (the "useful operating range"). The single resonance of the primary system is replaced by two, either side of ωₐ. A larger μ moves them apart and widens the band.

Worked example 3.1 Absorber for a pump near resonance

A 50 kg pump on mounts of 200 kN/m runs at 60 rad/s with an unbalance force of 100 N. Design an absorber with \(\mu = 0.1\), find its amplitude, the new natural frequencies, and the useful operating range.

1
\(\omega_p = \sqrt{200000/50} = 63.25\) rad/s: 60 rad/s is close to resonance. Without an absorber \(X = F_0/(k - m\omega^2) = 100/20000 = 5.0\) mm.
2
\(m_a = 0.1(50) = 5\) kg; tune \(k_a = m_a\omega^2 = 5(60)^2 = 18\) kN/m (so \(\beta = 60/63.25 = 0.949\)).
3
\(X = 0\) at 60 rad/s; \(X_a = F_0/k_a = 100/18000 = 5.56\) mm, in antiphase with the force.
4
New natural frequencies (from \(\det(K - \omega^2M) = 0\)): 52.7 and 72.0 rad/s.
5
\(|Xk/F_0| \lt 1\) between 57.8 and 62.9 rad/s (and above 92.8 rad/s).
A 5 kg mass on an 18 kN/m spring. If the speed drifts up to 63 rad/s the pump moves 0.51 mm, against 64.5 mm without the absorber; but a start-up from rest now passes through the new resonance at 52.7 rad/s.

Damping, and absorbers in practice

Adding damping to the absorber means \(X \neq 0\) at tuning (no perfect absorption), but it limits the two new resonance peaks and widens the effective bandwidth. Examples: the Stockbridge damper on power lines (against wind-induced vibration), the 660 t tuned mass damper in the 500 m Taipei 101 tower, and the tuned mass damper in the nose of the 2005 Renault F1 car, which reduced bounce so the front wing stayed close to the ground, and was banned by the FIA in 2006.

Summary

An absorber turns 1DOF into 2DOF. Tuned and undamped, the net force on the primary mass is zero and it does not move; the force is "absorbed" by the motion of the absorber mass. Take care if \(\omega\) varies (robustness). A larger mass ratio widens the useful band; damping prevents the new resonances from being severe and also widens the band.

Problem 4 (SDOF FRF). (a) \(H = \dfrac{1/m}{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2) + j2\zeta\omega_n\omega}\). (b) As \(\omega \to 0\), \(H \to 1/(m\omega_n^2) = 1/k\): the inertia and damping forces are negligible and the mass "feels" only the spring. 05Practice problems

Problem 5.1. A machine mounted on springs sags 5 mm. Above what speed (rpm) does the mount isolate, and what isolation does it give at 1500 rpm (undamped)?

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{9.81/0.005} = 44.29\) rad/s. Isolation needs \(\omega \gt \sqrt2\omega_n = 62.6\) rad/s = 598 rpm. At 1500 rpm, \(r = 157.1/44.29 = 3.546\), T.R. \(= 1/(r^2 - 1) = 0.086\): 91 % isolation.

Problem 5.2. Why does adding damping to an isolator make isolation worse at the operating speed, yet engineers still add some?

Above \(r = \sqrt2\) the damper force grows with frequency and passes force straight through, so T.R. increases with \(\zeta\). But every machine passes through \(r = 1\) when it starts and stops, where an undamped mount would give a very large response; some damping limits that peak (and helps with shocks).

Problem 5.3. An absorber is tuned to 50 Hz with \(k_a = 20\) kN/m. The excitation is 40 N. What is the absorber amplitude and mass?

\(|X_a| = F_0/k_a = 40/20000 = 2\) mm. \(m_a = k_a/\omega^2 = 20000/(2\pi \times 50)^2 = 203\) g.

Problem 5.4. For \(\mu = 0.1\) and \(\beta = 1\), find the two natural frequencies of the system with the absorber.

\(q^4 - (2 + \mu)q^2 + 1 = 0\) with \(q = \omega/\omega_p\): \(q^2 = 1 + \mu/2 \mp \sqrt{\mu + \mu^2/4}\), giving \(q = 0.854\) and \(1.171\).

Problem 5.5. A machine runs at a speed that wanders by ±10 %. Is an undamped absorber with \(\mu = 0.05\) a good choice?

For \(\mu = 0.05\), \(\beta = 1\), the useful band is only 0.977 to 1.025 \(\omega_a\) (about ±2.4 %), and the new resonances at 0.894 and 1.118 \(\omega_p\) lie inside the ±10 % range. No: use a larger mass ratio, add damping, or isolate instead.

Problem 5.6. In example 2.1, why is the transmitted force the same for every damping ratio chosen?

Each design was sized so that T.R. = 0.5 at 35 rad/s, and \(F_T = kYr^2\,\text{T.R.} = m\omega^2Y\,\text{T.R.} = 3(35)^2(0.01)(0.5) = 18.4\) N: the force is set by the mass, the base motion and the required transmissibility, not by \(k\) or \(c\) separately.
