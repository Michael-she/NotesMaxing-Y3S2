VIBRATION AND NOISE · A2 AND A3 ASSESSMENT BRIEFING

A2 and A3: format, the GA rubric and how marks are given

What the lecturer said about the A2 and A3 papers in the briefing recorded on 14 November 2026: the paper layout, how the graduate attribute (GA) questions are marked, the formula sheet and its corrected equation, and where marks come from. One GA-style question is worked at the end of section 2.

01Paper format

The A2 and A3 use exactly the same format as the A1. You answer every question on the question paper itself, in the space left under each question. Extra pages are available if you run out of space.

You do not need to memorise everything in the course. The lecturer's summary slides list what you should know, and the formula sheet (section 3) holds the formulas.

02The GA questions

Definition

A GA question is a question that is also marked against the graduate attribute rubric for application of engineering knowledge. The rubric result is separate from the question's marks.

Each paper has two GA questions. You must pass the rubric on one of them, so failing one GA question is acceptable.

The GA is judged on the rubric, not on the marks you score for the question. A score of 6 out of 10 or 7 out of 12 does not decide the GA outcome by itself. The two are related: full marks almost certainly passes the rubric, and zero almost certainly fails it, but everything between is judged against the rubric's descriptions.

Watch out

Any question in the paper can be a GA question, and the GA can be set on any section of the work. Do not try to predict where it will be and study only that section. Passing the GA while failing the module still means repeating the module.

The three criteria

The whole course is application of engineering knowledge: the maths you already know, applied to one area of dynamics. A GA question differs from an ordinary question mainly in how the information reaches you.

CriterionWhat it checksHow it shows up
CYou can read and apply appropriate empirical information.Data comes as a graph, a table or a formula you have not seen before, rather than being stated. There may be more information than you need: the A1 gave five or six peaks when two were enough.
BYou understand and apply the first-principle physical laws.Newton's laws to get the equation of motion for a single or two degree of freedom system; conservation of energy for energy methods; knowing that a frequency read off a decaying record is the damped natural frequency. Present in every question, not only GA questions.
AThe maths is done correctly.Correct equations, correct substitution, correct solution. The easiest criterion to satisfy.

A fail on the rubric means something is fundamentally wrong. Mixing up the natural frequency and the damped natural frequency is the lecturer's example: it is an error in the first-principle laws (criterion B), however small its effect on the numbers. A slip such as a mistyped calculator entry, where the method is visibly right, will be treated more leniently in the A2 and A3 than it was in the A1.

A GA-style question worked

The question below is built the way the lecturer described: the data is in a figure and a table, there is more of it than you need, and the trap is the one he named. It uses the logarithmic decrement from Week 1.

Figure 2.1: The solid teal line is the displacement and the dashed line is the decay envelope. The six peaks are numbered; peaks 1 and 6 (amber) are the only two the solution needs. The plot is computed from \(x(t) = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\) with the values found below.

From a decay record to the system parameters\[ T_d = \frac{t_{1+n} - t_1}{n}, \qquad \delta = \frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{x_1}{x_{1+n}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}}, \qquad \omega_n = \frac{\omega_d}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \]
\(T_d\)
Damped period, the time between successive peaks (s).
\(t_1, t_{1+n}\)
Times of the first peak used and of the peak \(n\) periods later (s).
\(n\)
Number of whole periods between the two peaks used.
\(\delta\)
Logarithmic decrement.
\(x_1, x_{1+n}\)
Heights of the two peaks used (m or mm, the same for both).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency, \(2\pi/T_d\) (rad/s).
Worked example 2.1 stiffness and damping from a decay record

A 4.0 kg mass on a spring and viscous damper is displaced 10 mm and released. Figure 2.1 shows the measured response and the table lists its peaks. Find the stiffness \(k\) and the damping coefficient \(c\).

Peak123456
Time (s)0.0000.2530.5050.7581.0101.263
Height (mm)10.005.322.831.500.800.43
1
Choose two peaks far apart, so reading errors are spread over several periods: peaks 1 and 6, which are \(n = 5\) periods apart. Peaks 2 to 5 are not needed (criterion C).
2
Damped period and damped natural frequency: \[ T_d = \frac{1.263 - 0.000}{5} = 0.2526\ \text{s}, \qquad \omega_d = \frac{2\pi}{0.2526} = 24.87\ \text{rad/s}. \]
3
Logarithmic decrement and damping ratio: \[ \delta = \frac{1}{5}\ln\frac{10.00}{0.43} = 0.6293, \qquad \zeta = \frac{0.6293}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + 0.6293^2}} = 0.09966. \]
4
The period read off the plot belongs to the damped motion (criterion B). Convert it to the undamped natural frequency before using \(\omega_n^2 = k/m\): \[ \omega_n = \frac{24.87}{\sqrt{1 - 0.09966^2}} = 25.00\ \text{rad/s}. \]
5
Stiffness and damping coefficient: \[ k = m\omega_n^2 = 4.0 \times 25.00^2 = 2500\ \text{N/m}, \qquad c = 2\zeta m\omega_n = 2 \times 0.09966 \times 4.0 \times 25.00 = 19.93\ \text{N s/m}. \]
\(k \approx 2.50\) kN/m and \(c \approx 19.9\) N s/m, with \(\zeta \approx 0.100\) and \(\omega_n \approx 25.0\) rad/s.
Watch out

Using \(\omega_d = 24.87\) rad/s directly in \(k = m\omega^2\) gives 2475 N/m, only 1% low. The number looks almost right, but on the rubric it is the fundamental error the lecturer described, not a calculation slip.

Other peak pairs

Any two peaks give the same answer within the rounding of the table: peaks 1 and 4 give \(k = 2499\) N/m, and peaks 2 and 6 give 2502 N/m.

03The formula sheet

You get a formula sheet in the A2 and A3. It is already on SUNLearn, near the top of the module page. It includes the beam table, so any beam calculation can be done from it without memorising beam results.

Any formula that is not on the sheet must be memorised or derived if you want to use it. The lecturer's example is the Euler formulas for converting between the forms of the free response. His advice is not to convert at all: know each form of the solution, use the form a question asks for, and otherwise use whichever form suits the task.

Exam

To sketch a response, use a trigonometric form such as \(Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\): a decaying envelope times a sine is easy to draw. The exponential form \(a_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + a_2e^{\lambda_2 t}\) with complex \(\lambda\) gives no direct picture of the motion.

The corrected equation

Watch out

An older version of the formula sheet has a sign error in one equation: a negative sign on a term involving the amplitude \(X\) and the force \(f\) is wrong. It was pointed out after the A1, and last year's A2 and A3 papers carried the same mistake (no question on them needed that equation). The sheet for the A2 and A3 is corrected. The recording only shows the lecturer pointing at the equation on screen, so it does not say which one it is. Download the current sheet from SUNLearn rather than using a copy from an old paper, and compare any equation with \(X\) and \(f\) in it against the derivation in the Week 3 notes.

04How marks are given

Mark allocation depends on the question, so the safe habit is to write down three things every time: the formula you are using, your working, and the answer. That covers what marks are usually given for. Not every question gives a mark for writing the formula, but some do.

Free body diagrams can carry marks. The lecturer does not put FBD marks on every question, so that a student who never draws them is not penalised several times for the same habit. In the A1, only one question gave marks for a free body diagram. The paper will not say which question does, so draw one wherever a free body diagram applies.

Key idea

Formula, working, answer, and a free body diagram wherever one applies. You lose nothing by including them and you may lose marks by leaving them out.

05Derivations

If a question asks you to derive something, you will have seen that derivation before: in class, in the lecture notes or in the prescribed content for the course.

A question may still give you an equation you have not seen before. In that case the equation is provided, and you are expected to work with it using the general theory (this is criterion C in the GA rubric). You will not be asked to derive it. The lecturer's view is that you should be able to derive new results, but not under the time pressure of an assessment.

Tutorial solutions

Partial solutions for Tutorials 3 and 4, and for the tutorial set on the day of the briefing, were not yet on SUNLearn at the time. The lecturer said he would post them. Review session

The lecturer holds a review session in the weeks before the exam. It will be announced on SUNLearn, in a conference room or a lecture hall depending on how many students are interested. Nothing is prepared in advance: you bring the questions. An A1 question, a tutorial question, or a difficult textbook problem are all suitable, and the questions are worked as a group.

Marking and feedback

The demis were still marking the later practical or tutorial submissions; the plan was to finish by the end of that week, without a promise. The first set of marks should already be on SUNLearn. The module feedback form is open and the lecturer asked for it to be filled in: part of this year's course was changed from last year's feedback.

07Key points

Paper

Same format as the A1. Answer on the question paper; extra pages are available.

Formula sheet provided, with the beam table. Use the corrected version from SUNLearn.

GA

Two GA questions per paper; pass the rubric on one. Judged on the rubric, not on the question's marks.

C: read supplied data. B: first-principle laws. A: correct maths. Fundamental errors fail; calculator slips are treated leniently.

Marks

Formula, working and answer every time. Free body diagrams sometimes carry marks, without warning.

Derivations

Only ones you have seen. Unseen equations are given to you to apply, not to derive.
