Base excitation, rotating unbalance and numerical simulation

A system on a moving base, displacement and force transmissibility and isolation, the response to a rotating unbalance, and solving equations of motion numerically with Runge-Kutta, ode45 and Simulink.

01Base excitation

In base excitation the force does not act on the mass directly: the support moves. Examples are a car body on its suspension driving over a rough road (the road is the base), or an engine on compliant mounts on a vibrating chassis. The aim is the motion of the mass, and the force passed through the suspension, as functions of the suspension parameters and the excitation.

Figure 1.1: The spring and damper act on the relative motion \(x - y\). Both \(x\) and \(y\) are measured upward from equilibrium.

Newton's second law on the mass, \(m\ddot x = -k(x - y) - c(\dot x - \dot y)\), gives:

Base excitation equation of motion\[ m\ddot x + c(\dot x - \dot y) + k(x - y) = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = c\dot y + ky \]
\(x\)
Absolute displacement of the mass (m).
\(y\)
Displacement of the base (m).
\(c\)
Damping coefficient (N s/m).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).

With harmonic base motion \(y = Y\sin\omega_bt\), the right side becomes \(cY\omega_b\cos\omega_bt + kY\sin\omega_bt\): the system is driven by two harmonic forces, one through the damper and one through the spring. Mass-normalised:

\[ \ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot x + \omega_n^2x = 2\zeta\omega_n\omega_bY\cos\omega_bt + \omega_n^2Y\sin\omega_bt. \]

Each term is a harmonic force of the kind solved in week 3, so each gives a particular solution with \(f_0 = 2\zeta\omega_n\omega_bY\) and \(f_0 = \omega_n^2Y\), both lagging by \(\theta_1\). Adding them:

Steady-state response to base motion\[ x_p(t) = \omega_nY\sqrt{\frac{\omega_n^2 + (2\zeta\omega_b)^2}{(\omega_n^2 - \omega_b^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_n\omega_b)^2}}\cos(\omega_bt - \theta_1 - \theta_2), \quad \theta_1 = \tan^{-1}\frac{2\zeta\omega_n\omega_b}{\omega_n^2 - \omega_b^2}, \quad \theta_2 = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega_n}{2\zeta\omega_b} \]
\(Y\)
Amplitude of the base motion (m).
\(\omega_b\)
Frequency of the base motion (rad/s).
\(\theta_1, \theta_2\)
Phase angles (rad); \(\theta_1\) runs from 0 to \(\pi\), so use atan2.

Displacement and force transmissibility

Divide the amplitude by \(Y\) and use \(r = \omega_b/\omega_n\):

Displacement transmissibility\[ \frac{X}{Y} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} \]
\(X/Y\)
Displacement transmissibility: amplitude of the mass divided by amplitude of the base.
\(r\)
Frequency ratio \(\omega_b/\omega_n\).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.

The force the suspension passes to the mass (and back to the base) is \(F(t) = k(x - y) + c(\dot x - \dot y) = -m\ddot x\). Its amplitude, relative to \(kY\), is the force transmissibility:

Force transmissibility\[ \frac{F_T}{kY} = r^2\sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} \]
\(F_T\)
Amplitude of the force transmitted through spring and damper (N).
\(kY\)
Spring force for a deflection equal to the base amplitude (N).

Figure 1.2: Top: X/Y starts at 1, peaks near r = 1 and drops below 1 for every damping ratio at r = √2 (shaded isolation zone). Above √2 less damping gives better isolation. Bottom: the force transmissibility is X/Y multiplied by r², so it keeps growing at high frequency when the damping is high.

Key idea

A suspension only pays for itself in the isolation zone, \(r \gt \sqrt2\), where the mass moves less than the base. Below \(\sqrt2\) it amplifies. Damping reduces the resonance peak but costs isolation above \(\sqrt2\): choose it as a compromise between running speed and the resonance the system passes through on the way up.

Design story from the lecture

A pump on a diamond-mining ship shares a deck with shakers running at a fixed frequency. Choosing mounts that put the pump at \(r = 2\) with little damping isolates it well. But when a shaker is restarted after maintenance, its frequency sweeps up from zero through the pump's resonance, so the mounts, pump and bolts must survive passing through \(r = 1\). That sets a minimum damping.

Complex response and transfer function methods

Let \(y = Ye^{j\omega_bt}\) and \(x_p = Xe^{j\omega_bt}\). Substituting into \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = c\dot y + ky\) and cancelling \(e^{j\omega_bt}\):

\[ \frac{X}{Y} = \frac{k + jc\omega_b}{k - m\omega_b^2 + jc\omega_b} = \frac{\omega_n^2 + j\,2\zeta\omega_n\omega_b}{\omega_n^2 - \omega_b^2 + j\,2\zeta\omega_n\omega_b}. \]

It has the form \((a + jb)/(c + jd)\), whose magnitude is \(\sqrt{(a^2 + b^2)/(c^2 + d^2)}\). Dividing top and bottom by \(\omega_n^4\) gives the displacement transmissibility above. The same ratio follows from the Laplace transform of the equation of motion: \((ms^2 + cs + k)X = (cs + k)Y\), so the transfer function is \(X/Y = (cs + k)/(ms^2 + cs + k)\); put \(s = j\omega_b\) and take the magnitude.

Exam

The lecture asks you to prove the magnitude of \(X/Y\) from the complex form: multiply by the complex conjugate of the denominator, or use \(|a + jb|/|c + jd|\) directly. Practise it once by hand.

Worked example 1.1 Car on a wavy road

A quarter-car model has \(m = 300\) kg, \(k = 30\) kN/m and \(\zeta = 0.3\). It drives at 54 km/h over a road with a sinusoidal profile of wavelength 6 m and amplitude 20 mm. Find the body amplitude and the force transmitted. At what speed is it worst?

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{30000/300} = 10\) rad/s.
2
Speed 15 m/s over a 6 m wavelength: \(f_b = 15/6 = 2.5\) Hz, \(\omega_b = 15.71\) rad/s, \(r = 1.571\).
3
\(X/Y = \sqrt{\dfrac{1 + (0.942)^2}{(1 - 2.467)^2 + (0.942)^2}} = 0.788\), so \(X = 15.8\) mm.
4
\(F_T = kY\,r^2(X/Y) = 30000(0.02)(2.467)(0.788) = 1166\) N.
5
Resonance at \(r = 1\): \(\omega_b = 10\) rad/s, \(V = \omega_b\lambda/2\pi = 9.55\) m/s (34.4 km/h), where \(X/Y = 1.94\).
At 54 km/h the body moves ±15.8 mm (isolated, since \(r \gt \sqrt2\)) and the suspension passes 1166 N. Around 34 km/h the body would move about ±39 mm.
Worked example 1.2 Choosing pump mounts

A 50 kg pump stands on a deck shaken at 25 Hz. Choose the mount stiffness for \(r = 2\), and find \(X/Y\) for \(\zeta = 0.05\) and \(\zeta = 0.3\), and at resonance during start-up for \(\zeta = 0.05\).

1
\(r = 2\) means \(f_n = 12.5\) Hz: \(k = m(2\pi f_n)^2 = 50(78.54)^2 = 308.4\) kN/m.
2
\(\zeta = 0.05\): \(X/Y = \sqrt{(1 + 0.04)/(9 + 0.04)} = 0.339\). \(\zeta = 0.3\): \(X/Y = 0.483\).
3
At \(r = 1\), \(X/Y = \sqrt{1 + (2\zeta)^2}/(2\zeta) = 10.05\) for \(\zeta = 0.05\).
Light damping isolates better at the operating point (0.339 against 0.483), but the pump briefly moves 10 times the deck amplitude while the shakers spin up.

Figure 1.3: It starts at worked example 1.1 (ζ = 0.3, r = 1.57). The teal curve is X/Y, the amber curve FT/(kY). Every X/Y curve passes through 1 at r = √2. Lowering ζ improves isolation above √2 but raises the resonance peak that the system passes through when it speeds up.

Figure 1.4: Below resonance (r = 0.5) the mass follows the base almost exactly. At resonance it moves 2.7 times as far and lags by about 68°. At r = 2 it barely moves: the spring absorbs the base motion.

02Rotating unbalance

A rotating machine whose mass centre is off its axis (a washing machine drum with clothes on one side, a fan with a damaged blade) is modelled as an eccentric mass \(m_0\) at radius \(e\), turning at \(\omega_r\), inside a machine of total mass \(m\). Guides allow only vertical motion; the mounting is a spring and damper.

Figure 2.1: The eccentric mass moves with the machine plus its own rotation, \(x + x_r\), where \(x_r = e\sin\omega_rt\). The force \(F_r\) between the eccentric mass and the rest of the machine (mass \(m - m_0\)) acts in opposite directions on the two bodies.

For the eccentric mass, \(m_0(\ddot x + \ddot x_r) = -F_r\) with \(\ddot x_r = -e\omega_r^2\sin\omega_rt\). For the rest of the machine, \((m - m_0)\ddot x = F_r - c\dot x - kx\). Eliminating \(F_r\):

Rotating unbalance equation of motion\[ m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = m_0e\omega_r^2\sin\omega_rt \]
\(m\)
Total machine mass, including \(m_0\) (kg).
\(m_0\)
Eccentric (unbalanced) mass (kg).
\(e\)
Eccentricity: radius of the unbalanced mass (m).
\(\omega_r\)
Rotation speed (rad/s).

This is the forced equation of week 3 with \(F_0 = m_0e\omega_r^2\): a force amplitude that grows with the square of speed. So:

Unbalance response amplitude\[ X = \frac{m_0e}{m}\,\frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2} \]
\(X\)
Steady-state amplitude of the machine (m).
\(m_0e/m\)
Amplitude at very high speed (m).
\(r\)
Frequency ratio \(\omega_r/\omega_n\).
\(\theta\)
Phase lag behind the unbalance force (rad).

Figure 2.2: At low speed the machine barely moves (the force is small). Near \(r = 1\) the amplitude peaks at about \(1/(2\zeta)\) times \(m_0e/m\). At high speed \(mX/(m_0e)\) tends to 1: the machine and the eccentric mass rotate about their common centre of mass.

Watch out

The notes say "substitution of equations 23 and 24 into 25"; they mean equations 24 and 25 into 26. Also note \(m\) is the total mass: the \(m_0\ddot x\) term from the eccentric mass recombines with \((m - m_0)\ddot x\).

Worked example 2.1 Washing machine

A 40 kg washing machine with an unbalance \(m_0e = 0.1\) kg m sits on mounts with \(k = 40\) kN/m and \(\zeta = 0.1\). Find the amplitude at 1200 rpm, at resonance during spin-up, and the force transmitted to the floor at 1200 rpm.

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{40000/40} = 31.62\) rad/s (5.03 Hz). 1200 rpm is \(\omega_r = 125.7\) rad/s, so \(r = 3.974\).
2
\(X = \dfrac{0.1}{40}\cdot\dfrac{15.79}{\sqrt{(-14.79)^2 + (0.795)^2}} = 2.665\) mm.
3
At resonance \(X = (m_0e/m)/(2\zeta) = 0.0025/0.2 = 12.5\) mm.
4
Floor force: \(F_T = X\sqrt{k^2 + (c\omega_r)^2}\) with \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km} = 253\) N s/m: \(F_T = 136\) N, against an unbalance force \(m_0e\omega_r^2 = 1579\) N.
About 2.67 mm at full speed, but 12.5 mm while passing 300 rpm on the way up. The mounts pass only 9 % of the unbalance force to the floor at full speed.

03Numerical simulation of the time response

Analytical solutions exist only for simple cases. A numerical solution is a discretised approximation: it steps forward in time from the initial conditions. Methods include Runge-Kutta, Newmark-β and generalised-α; the course uses Runge-Kutta.

The fourth-order Runge-Kutta method

For a first-order problem \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x, t)\), \(\mathbf x(0) = \mathbf x_0\), each step takes a weighted average of four slope estimates across the step:

Runge-Kutta (RK4) step\[ \mathbf x_{n+1} = \mathbf x_n + \frac{\Delta t}{6}\left(\mathbf k_{n1} + 2\mathbf k_{n2} + 2\mathbf k_{n3} + \mathbf k_{n4}\right) \] \[ \mathbf k_{n1} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x_n, t_n), \quad \mathbf k_{n2} = \mathbf f\!\left(\mathbf x_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_{n1}, t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right), \quad \mathbf k_{n3} = \mathbf f\!\left(\mathbf x_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_{n2}, t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right), \quad \mathbf k_{n4} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x_n + \Delta t\,\mathbf k_{n3}, t_n + \Delta t) \]
\(\mathbf x_n\)
State at step \(n\).
\(\Delta t\)
Time step (s); fixed, or adapted automatically to how fast the solution changes.
\(\mathbf k_{n1}\)
Slope at the start of the step.
\(\mathbf k_{n2}, \mathbf k_{n3}\)
Slopes at the midpoint, from the first and then the improved estimate.
\(\mathbf k_{n4}\)
Slope at the end of the step.

Writing the equation of motion as first-order equations

A vibration problem is second order, so introduce state variables \(x_1 = x\) and \(x_2 = \dot x\):

State-space form of the SDOF equation\[ \dot x_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot x_2 = -\frac{c}{m}x_2 - \frac{k}{m}x_1 \;\Big(+ \frac{F(t)}{m}\Big) \]
\(x_1\)
Displacement (m).
\(x_2\)
Velocity (m/s).
\(F(t)\)
Any applied force (N), included if present.
Worked example 3.1 One RK4 step by hand

Take lecture example 1, \(3\ddot x + \dot x + 2x = 0\), \(x(0) = 0\), \(\dot x(0) = 0.25\) m/s, and one step of \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s.

1
\(\mathbf f(x_1, x_2) = [x_2,\; -\tfrac23x_1 - \tfrac13x_2]\).
2
\(\mathbf k_1 = \mathbf f(0, 0.25) = [0.25, -0.08333]\).
3
\(\mathbf k_2 = \mathbf f(0.0125, 0.24583) = [0.24583, -0.09028]\).
4
\(\mathbf k_3 = \mathbf f(0.012292, 0.24549) = [0.24549, -0.09002]\).
5
\(\mathbf k_4 = \mathbf f(0.024549, 0.24100) = [0.24100, -0.09670]\).
6
\(\mathbf x_1 = [0, 0.25] + \tfrac{0.1}{6}(\mathbf k_1 + 2\mathbf k_2 + 2\mathbf k_3 + \mathbf k_4) = [0.024561, 0.240989]\).
After one step \(x = 24.561\) mm and \(\dot x = 0.240989\) m/s. The exact solution gives 24.5606 mm and 0.240989 m/s: RK4 matches to about \(10^{-8}\) m.

Solving it in MATLAB

MATLAB's ode23 (a three-stage, third-order Runge-Kutta method) and ode45 (six-stage, fifth order) adapt the step size automatically. Start with ode45 and try others only if it struggles; MathWorks publishes a solver-selection table. The call is [t, x] = ode45(odefun, tspan, x0).

function xdot = sdof(~, x)          % example 1: 3x'' + x' + 2x = 0
m = 3; c = 1; k = 2;
xdot = zeros(2,1);
xdot(1) = x(2);
xdot(2) = -(k/m)*x(1) - (c/m)*x(2);
end

[t, x] = ode45(@sdof, [0, 40], [0; 0.25]);

Example 2 adds Coulomb friction with the sign function, \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx + \mathrm{sgn}(\dot x)\mu N = 0\):

function xdot = mu_sdof(~, x)       % example 2: viscous + Coulomb
m = 3; c = 0.4; k = 2; mu = 0.003;
g = 9.81; N = m*g;
xdot = zeros(2,1);
xdot(1) = x(2);
xdot(2) = -(c/m)*x(2) - (k/m)*x(1) - (mu*N/m)*sign(x(2));
end

[t, x] = ode45(@mu_sdof, [0, 40], [0; 0.5]);

Figure 3.2: Solved here with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method of section 3 at a fixed step of 2 ms (ode45 uses an adaptive Runge-Kutta method of the same family). Top: viscous damping only (\(\zeta = 0.204\)); the motion decays smoothly towards zero. Bottom: with friction added the decay ends abruptly at about 14 s and the mass stays at -41 mm, inside the stick band \(\pm\mu N/k = \pm 44\) mm.

Watch out

In the Lecture 8 notes, example 2 is stated with \(\mu = 0.001\) "N/m", but the code uses mu = 0.003. The friction coefficient has no unit; the plotted results come from the code value, 0.003. The sign function also makes the right-hand side jump each time the velocity changes sign, so the solver takes many tiny steps there and may chatter once the mass sticks.

Figure 3.3: Slide Δt. Each dot is one RK4 step computed in your browser with the four slopes of section 3. The period of this system is 7.86 s: with a few steps per period RK4 stays close; by about Δt = 2.5 s (three steps per period) it drifts visibly and finally loses the oscillation. ode45 chooses Δt automatically to keep the error below a tolerance.

Figure 3.4: The sum block forms \(-c\dot x - kx\); the gain \(1/m\) turns it into \(\ddot x\); two integrators give \(\dot x\) and \(x\). Set the initial conditions inside the integrator blocks. 04Practice problems

Problem 4.1. A 2 kg instrument is mounted on a panel vibrating at 50 Hz with amplitude 0.5 mm. Its mounts have \(\zeta = 0.1\). What mount stiffness limits the instrument amplitude to 0.1 mm?

Need \(X/Y = 0.2\). Solve \((1 + 0.04r^2)/((1 - r^2)^2 + 0.04r^2) = 0.04\): \(0.04r^4 - 0.1184r^2 - 0.96 = 0\), so \(r^2 = 6.598\), \(r = 2.569\). Then \(\omega_n = 314.16/r = 122.3\) rad/s and \(k = m\omega_n^2 = 29.9\) kN/m or less.

Problem 4.2. Show that every displacement transmissibility curve passes through 1 at \(r = \sqrt2\).

Set \(X/Y = 1\): \(1 + (2\zeta r)^2 = (1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2\), so \((1 - r^2)^2 = 1\), giving \(r = 0\) or \(r^2 = 2\). The damping terms cancel, so the crossing is the same for every \(\zeta\).

Problem 4.3. A fan of mass 25 kg on mounts with \(\omega_n = 20\) rad/s and \(\zeta = 0.05\) has an unbalance \(m_0e = 0.01\) kg m. Find its amplitude at 600 rpm and at resonance.

\(\omega_r = 62.83\) rad/s, \(r = 3.142\). \(X = (0.01/25)\cdot 9.870/\sqrt{(1 - 9.870)^2 + 0.3142^2} = 0.4448\) mm. At resonance \(X = 0.0004/0.1 = 4\) mm.

Problem 4.4. Write the state-space function for \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t\).

xdot(1) = x(2); xdot(2) = (F0*cos(w*t) - c*x(2) - k*x(1))/m; Note the function now needs \(t\) as its first argument instead of ~.

Problem 4.5. Why does a force transmissibility curve with high damping rise again at large \(r\)?

At high frequency the damper force \(c(\dot x - \dot y) \approx -c\dot y\) grows with \(\omega_b\) while the spring force stays about \(kY\). So \(F_T/(kY) \to 2\zeta r\) for large \(r\): a stiff damper passes the base motion straight through.

Problem 4.6. With RK4 and \(\Delta t = 0.5\) s on lecture example 1, the error at \(t = 20\) s is \(2.6\times10^{-5}\) m. Roughly what error do you expect at \(\Delta t = 0.25\) s?

RK4 has global error proportional to \(\Delta t^4\), so halving the step divides the error by about 16: roughly \(1.6\times10^{-6}\) m. (At \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s it is \(2.2\times10^{-8}\) m.)

05Formula sheet

Base excitation

\(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = c\dot y + ky\)

\(X/Y = \sqrt{(1 + (2\zeta r)^2)/((1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2)}\)

\(F_T/(kY) = r^2\,X/Y\)

Isolation for \(r \gt \sqrt2\)

\(X/Y = (cs + k)/(ms^2 + cs + k)\), \(s = j\omega_b\)

Rotating unbalance

\(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = m_0e\omega_r^2\sin\omega_rt\)

\(X = \frac{m_0e}{m}\frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}\)

At \(r = 1\): \(X = m_0e/(2\zeta m)\); \(r \to \infty\): \(X \to m_0e/m\)

Numerical

\(x_1 = x\), \(x_2 = \dot x\): \(\dot x_1 = x_2\), \(\dot x_2 = -\frac{c}{m}x_2 - \frac{k}{m}x_1\)

RK4: \(x_{n+1} = x_n + \frac{\Delta t}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)\)

[t, x] = ode45(@f, tspan, x0)
