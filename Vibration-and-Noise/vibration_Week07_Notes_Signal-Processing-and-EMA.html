Signal processing and experimental modal analysis

Sampling, the DFT and FFT, leakage and windowing, power spectral densities and averaging, then frequency response functions and how natural frequencies, damping and mode shapes are measured from them.

01Why the frequency domain

Signal processing transforms a signal from one form into another so that features or patterns become easy to see, for example to extract physical information buried in noise. The most important transformation in vibration work is from the sampled time domain to the frequency domain.

  • Some operations are easier in the frequency domain: convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency, so a response is simply \(X(f) = H(f)F(f)\).
  • For forced vibration we need the structure's response at each frequency, the frequency response function (FRF), which follows directly from frequency-domain signals.
  • When several sources act at once, each shows up as its own peak in the spectrum.
Example from the notes

An acceleration record from the polar vessel SA Agulhas II in calm water looks like noise in the time domain. Its spectrum shows broad peaks from the ship's first three vertical bending modes (about 2, 4 and 5 Hz), a narrow peak at about 9 Hz from the four-bladed propeller's blade pass, and a sharp peak at exactly 12.5 Hz from a diesel generator turning at 750 rpm (750/60 = 12.5 Hz).

Exam

For the assessments you must understand the derivations, but you will not be asked to compute transforms by hand. You should be able to explain why the frequency domain is useful, what the DFT does and that it is computed with the FFT, its periodicity, leakage and windowing, how the PSD differs from the DFT, and read information off DFT and PSD plots.

02Time-domain sampling

A sensor's analogue output \(x(t)\) is sampled at regular intervals \(\Delta t\), giving the digital signal \(x(n) = x(n\Delta t)\), \(n = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1\).

Sampling parameters\[ f_s = \frac{1}{\Delta t}, \qquad T = N\Delta t, \qquad f_s \ge 2f_{max} \]
\(f_s\)
Sample rate (Hz).
\(\Delta t\)
Time between samples (s).
\(T\)
Measurement duration (block length) (s).
\(N\)
Number of samples in the block.
\(f_{max}\)
Highest frequency present in the signal (Hz).

Figure 2.1: Twenty samples cover the 1 s block; five samples per period are enough to represent this signal.

The sample rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal (the sampling theorem). Half the sample rate, \(f_s/2\), is the Nyquist frequency. A component above it is not lost but disguised: it appears at a false, lower frequency. This is aliasing. Measurement systems prevent it with an analogue low-pass anti-aliasing filter before the sampler, so no physical frequencies above \(f_s/2\) remain.

Figure 2.2: The samples of an 18 Hz cosine (grey) are identical to those of a 2 Hz cosine (amber dashed), since \(\cos(2\pi 18n/20) = \cos(2\pi n - 2\pi 2n/20) = \cos(2\pi 2n/20)\). After sampling, nothing can tell them apart.

03The discrete Fourier transform

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) turns a sampled block into a spectrum:

Discrete Fourier transform\[ X(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x(n)e^{-j2\pi kn/N} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x(n)\left[\cos\frac{2\pi kn}{N} - j\sin\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\right], \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1 \]
\(X(k)\)
DFT at frequency line (bin) \(k\): complex, with magnitude and phase.
\(x(n)\)
Sample \(n\) of the block.
\(N\)
Number of samples.
\(k\)
Frequency line.
Inverse DFT\[ x(n) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X(k)e^{j2\pi kn/N} \]
\(x(n)\)
Recovered sample.
\(1/N\)
Scaling that pairs with the unscaled forward DFT above (the MATLAB convention).
Watch out

The slides write the inverse DFT without the \(1/N\) and with the sum over \(n\) instead of \(k\); the class notes have it right, as above. The slides also put \(1/(2\pi)\) in front of the continuous Fourier transform in \(f\); with \(e^{-j2\pi ft}\) neither the forward nor the inverse transform needs a \(2\pi\) factor. Only one of the DFT and IDFT carries \(1/N\); conventions differ between texts.

Two properties follow from the definition:

  • The DFT is complex (real and imaginary parts, or magnitude and phase). For an even signal like a cosine it is real; for an odd one like a sine it is imaginary.
  • The DFT is periodic: \(X(k + N) = X(k)\), and the inverse gives \(x(n + N) = x(n)\). The DFT is really the spectrum of the block repeated end to end for ever.

What the DFT does: basis functions

The functions \(\cos(2\pi kn/N)\) and \(\sin(2\pi kn/N)\) are the basis functions: line \(k\) has exactly \(k\) periods in the block. The DFT multiplies the signal sample by sample with each basis function and sums. Because sampled cosines are orthogonal,

\[ \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\cos\frac{2\pi pn}{N}\cos\frac{2\pi qn}{N} = \begin{cases} 0, & p \neq q \\ C, & p = q \end{cases}, \qquad \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\cos\frac{2\pi pn}{N}\sin\frac{2\pi qn}{N} = 0, \]

the sum is non-zero only where the signal contains a component with that many periods in the block. The DFT is a "search function" that looks for each basis function in the signal.

Frequency increment, negative frequencies, scaling

Frequency of line k\[ f_k = \frac{k}{T} = k\Delta f, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{N\Delta t} = \frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad f_{N/2} = \frac{f_s}{2} \]
\(f_k\)
Physical frequency of line \(k\) (Hz).
\(\Delta f\)
Frequency increment (resolution) (Hz): refine it only by measuring longer.
\(f_s/2\)
Nyquist frequency, at line \(N/2\).

Lines above \(N/2\) repeat the lines below (line \(N - k\) has the same samples as line \(k\)), so only \(N/2 + 1\) lines carry information. The upper half is usually shifted to the left and called the negative frequencies. With an anti-aliasing filter in place, they are discarded and a single-sided spectrum is used.

Amplitude scaling\[ X_{\text{scaled}}(k) = \frac{1}{N}X(k), \qquad \text{amplitude of a component} = 2|X_{\text{scaled}}(k)| \;\text{(single-sided, } 0 \lt k \lt N/2) \]
\(1/N\)
Makes each two-sided peak equal half the amplitude.
\(2\)
Adds back the matching negative-frequency peak in a single-sided spectrum.
Worked example 3.1 Reading the lecture DFT

A unit cosine at 2 Hz is sampled at \(f_s = 20\) Hz with \(N = 20\). The unscaled DFT shows lines of height 10 at \(k = 2\) and \(k = 18\). Explain every number.

1
\(\Delta f = 20/20 = 1\) Hz, so line 2 is 2 Hz: the cosine, with two periods in the 1 s block.
2
Line 18 is the repetition of line \(20 - 18 = 2\): the negative frequency −2 Hz.
3
Height \(10 = N/2\): scaled by \(1/N\) it is 0.5 at +2 Hz and 0.5 at −2 Hz; the single-sided amplitude is \(2 \times 0.5 = 1\).
One cosine of amplitude 1 at 2 Hz, exactly as sampled.

The fast Fourier transform

The FFT is an efficient algorithm for the DFT, not a different transform (which is why DFTs are often just called FFTs). The direct sum needs about \(N^2\) operations; the FFT, by exploiting the symmetry of \(e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\), needs about \(N\log_2N\). For \(N = 4096\): \(16.8\times10^6\) against 49 152, a saving factor of 341 (for \(N = 2048\): 186).

fs = 32; N = 32; t = (0:N-1)'/fs;     % 1 s block
y = 2*cos(2*pi*8*t);                   % amplitude 2 at 8 Hz
Y = (1/N)*fft(y);                      % scaled DFT
Ys = [Y(N/2+2:end); Y(1:N/2+1)];       % negative frequencies to the left
df = fs/N; f = (-fs/2+df:df:fs/2);     % frequency axis
stem(f, abs(Ys))                       % peaks of 1 at -8 and +8 Hz

Figure 3.1: It starts at the lecture example: 2 Hz, N = 20, no window, so the block holds exactly two periods and the DFT has a single line of height 1 at 2 Hz. Change N to 24 to get the leakage example (peak 0.74 instead of 1, energy spread into the neighbouring lines), then switch the Hanning window on: the peak rises to 0.90 and the far lines drop. The spectrum is scaled by 2/N (single-sided) and by the window correction factor A_w.

04Leakage and windowing

Leakage

The DFT assumes the block repeats. If the signal does not have a whole number of periods in the block, the repeated block has a jump at every join, and the spectrum is wrong: the peak is too low and energy appears at lines where the signal has none. This bias is leakage. Equivalently, the finite measurement multiplies the signal by a rectangular window, which smears each spectral line.

Worked example 4.1 Leakage in numbers (class notes example)

The 2 Hz unit cosine sampled at 20 Hz, now with \(N = 24\).

1
\(\Delta f = 20/24 = 0.833\) Hz: 2 Hz lies between line 2 (1.67 Hz) and line 3 (2.50 Hz). The block holds 2.4 periods.
2
Scaled two-sided DFT: \(|X(2)| = 0.369\), \(|X(3)| = 0.264\), and every other line is non-zero (e.g. \(|X(0)| = 0.075\)), instead of 0.5 at 2 Hz and zero elsewhere.
3
With a Hanning window and correction: \(|X(2)| = 0.452\), \(|X(3)| = 0.393\), \(|X(0)| = 0.008\).
Without a window the peak reads 0.37 (26 % low); with the Hanning window 0.45, and the lines far from 2 Hz almost vanish.

There is no leakage only if the signal is exactly periodic in the block (rare for real signals) or is a transient that dies out inside the block. Otherwise (periodic, random, or long transient signals) leakage occurs.

Windowing

Multiplying the block by a window that tapers smoothly to zero at both ends removes the jump at the joins. The common choice is the Hanning window. The window also reduces the signal's amplitude, so the windowed DFT is corrected:

Hanning window and amplitude correction\[ w_h(n) = \sin^2\frac{\pi n}{N} = \frac12\left(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right), \qquad A_w = \frac{N}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w(n)}, \qquad X_w(k) = A_w X(k) \]
\(w_h(n)\)
Hanning window value at sample \(n\).
\(A_w\)
Window correction factor; 2 for the Hanning window.
\(X_w(k)\)
Corrected DFT of the windowed block.
w  = hann(N);                 % Hanning window
xw = w.*x;                    % windowed block
Aw = N/sum(w);                % correction factor
Xw = (Aw/N)*fft(xw);          % scaled, corrected DFT
Trade-off

A window reduces leakage but widens each peak: a bin-centred sine that would give one line gives three under a Hanning window (the neighbours at half height). Windowing trades frequency resolution for amplitude accuracy and lower leakage far from the peak.

05Power spectral density

A random signal has no discrete frequency components: it contains all frequencies and has a continuous spectrum. It is described by a spectral density. The power spectral density (PSD) is defined so that its area over a band equals the mean-square value of the signal in that band:

Power spectral density\[ \text{rms} = \sqrt{\int_{f_1}^{f_2}G_{xx}(f)\,df}, \qquad G_{xx}(f) = X(f)X(f)^* = |X(f)|^2, \qquad G_{xy}(f) = X(f)Y(f)^* \]
\(G_{xx}\)
Auto-power spectral density, real and non-negative (units²/Hz).
\(G_{xy}\)
Cross-power spectral density between two signals, complex.
\(^*\)
Complex conjugate.

Auto and cross spectral densities are what FRFs are estimated from (section 6).

Frequency-domain averaging

All measured signals contain random error (the signal is random, or it is contaminated by noise), so the PSD of a single block, \(|X_w(k)|^2\), is a poor estimate. Instead, split the record into \(M\) blocks, window each, and average the squared spectra frequency by frequency (Welch's method):

Averaged PSD estimates\[ \hat G_{xx}(k) = \frac{S_p}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|X_{w,m}(k)|^2, \qquad \hat G_{xy}(k) = \frac{S_p}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{w,m}(k)Y_{w,m}(k)^* \]
\(M\)
Number of averaged blocks.
\(S_p\)
Scaling for window and units: \(2A_w^2/(N^2B_{en})\) for \(k \neq 0\) (single-sided), \(A_w^2/(N^2B_{en})\) at \(k = 0\).
\(B_{en}\)
Equivalent noise bandwidth, \(N\Delta f\sum w^2(n)/(\sum w(n))^2\) (Hz).
Random error\[ \varepsilon_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt M}, \qquad \text{with overlap: } \varepsilon_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_e}}, \; M_e \lt M \]
\(\varepsilon_r\)
Normalised random error of the PSD estimate.
\(M_e\)
Effective number of averages when blocks overlap (they share samples, so they are partly correlated).

Shorter blocks give more averages but a coarser \(\Delta f = f_s/N\). Overlap recovers averages without shortening blocks; for a Hanning window the optimal overlap is 50 %.

Figure 4.1: The record of the lecture example: a 2 Hz cosine with 10 % noise, \(f_s = 32\) Hz, \(N = 8192\), Hanning blocks of 512 samples. One block (grey) gives a ragged noise floor. Welch averaging with 50 % overlap uses 31 blocks and smooths the floor; the 2 Hz peak is the same height in both. Computed here with the same steps as pwelch.

[Gxx, f] = pwelch(x, hann(512), 0.5*512, 512, fs);   % auto-PSD, 0 to fs/2
[Gxy, f] = cpsd(x, y, hann(512), 0.5*512, 512, fs);  % cross-PSD
DFT or PSD?

Use the scaled DFT for periodic signals: its peaks read amplitudes directly. Use the PSD for random (and noisy) signals: it is a density, so its value depends on \(\Delta f\) for a sine but not for broadband noise, and averaging makes it a reliable estimate. Integrate the PSD over a band to get the rms in that band.

06Experimental modal analysis and FRFs

Experimental modal analysis (EMA) uses measured forces and vibration responses to identify the modal properties (natural frequencies, damping ratios, mode shapes) of real structures: cars, aircraft, wind turbine blades. It is done to identify the dynamic properties of complex structures, and to validate computational models such as finite element models. The lecture example is an aircraft control surface fitted with accelerometers and struck with an impact hammer.

EMA measures frequency response functions and extracts the modal parameters from them.

Figure 6.1: In time, the response is the convolution of the impulse response function (IRF) with the force, \(x(t) = \int_0^th(t - \tau)f(\tau)\,d\tau\). In frequency, it is a product with the FRF.

The SDOF FRF

Laplace-transform \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = f(t)\): \((ms^2 + cs + k)X(s) = F(s)\). The transfer function is \(H(s) = 1/(ms^2 + cs + k)\), and putting \(s = j\omega\) gives the FRF. With \(c = 2\zeta\omega_nm\) and \(k = \omega_n^2m\) it has three equivalent forms:

SDOF receptance FRF\[ H(\omega) = \frac{X(\omega)}{F(\omega)} = \frac{1}{-\omega^2m + jc\omega + k} = \frac{1/m}{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2) + j\,2\zeta\omega_n\omega} = \frac{A}{j\omega - \lambda} + \frac{A^*}{j\omega - \lambda^*} \] \[ \lambda, \lambda^* = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}, \qquad A = \frac{1}{2jm\omega_d} \]
\(H(\omega)\)
Receptance (displacement per force) FRF (m/N).
\(\lambda\)
Pole of the system: the root of the characteristic equation of week 1.
\(A\)
Residue of the pole.
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency (rad/s).
Watch out

The slides write the poles as \(-\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\); the \(j\) is missing. The poles are complex: real part \(-\zeta\omega_n\) (decay), imaginary part \(\pm\omega_d\) (oscillation).

FRFDefinitionName
Displacement\(X/F\)Receptance (dynamic flexibility)
Velocity\(V/F = j\omega X/F\), magnitude \(\omega|X/F|\)Mobility
Acceleration\(A/F = -\omega^2X/F\), magnitude \(\omega^2|X/F|\)Accelerance (inertance)

Accelerometers measure accelerance directly. The three differ only at low and high frequency: receptance tends to \(1/k\) at low frequency, accelerance to \(1/m\) at high frequency.

Four ways to plot an FRF, and reading fₙ and ζ

The FRF is complex, so it is plotted as magnitude and phase (Bode form), or as real and imaginary parts. Each shows the natural frequency:

  • Magnitude: a peak at about \(f_d\) (exactly at \(f_n\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\)); below it the curve follows \(1/k\), above it \(1/(m\omega^2)\).
  • Phase: passes through −90° at \(f_n\).
  • Real part: crosses zero at \(f_n\).
  • Imaginary part: reaches its extreme (minimum for receptance) at about \(f_n\).
Half-power rule\[ \zeta \approx \frac{\omega_b - \omega_a}{2\omega_n}, \qquad |H(\omega_a)| = |H(\omega_b)| = \frac{|H|_{max}}{\sqrt2} \]
\(\omega_a, \omega_b\)
Frequencies either side of the peak where the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt2\) of the peak (half the power) (rad/s, or use Hz throughout).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency; the slides use \(\omega_d\), which is the same for light damping.

Figure 5.2: m = 1 kg. Magnitude (log scale): peak near fₙ, low-frequency line 1/k, high-frequency fall-off 1/(mω²), and the half-power points (amber) where the magnitude is the peak divided by √2. Phase: −90° at fₙ. Real part: crosses zero at fₙ. Imaginary part: its extreme is at fₙ. The readout compares the half-power estimate of ζ with the true value.

Watch out

The MDOF slide writes the half-power rule as \(\zeta_r = (\omega_{rb} - \omega_{ra})/\omega_r\), dropping the 2. The SDOF slide (and the lecture's own numbers, \(\zeta = 0.0502, 0.1021, 0.2042\) for true values 0.05, 0.1, 0.2) use \(2\omega\) in the denominator, as above. The rule is accurate for light damping and well-separated peaks only.

07Measuring FRFs and MDOF modal parameters

FRF estimators and coherence

The FRF is the ratio of the Fourier transforms, \(H = X/F\). With noise on the measurements, it is estimated from averaged spectral densities instead:

FRF estimators\[ \hat H_1(\omega) = \frac{G_{xf}(\omega)}{G_{ff}(\omega)}, \qquad \hat H_2(\omega) = \frac{G_{xx}(\omega)}{G_{fx}(\omega)}, \qquad \gamma^2 = \frac{\hat H_1}{\hat H_2} = \frac{|G_{xf}|^2}{G_{xx}G_{ff}} \]
\(\hat H_1\)
Best when the input is exact and the output is noisy.
\(\hat H_2\)
Best when the input is noisy and the output is exact.
\(G_{xf} = XF^*\)
Cross-spectral density of response and force.
\(\gamma^2\)
Coherence, 0 to 1: close to 1 means a good-quality FRF at that frequency.
Watch out

The slides write \(\hat H_1 = G_{fx}/G_{ff}\) with \(G_{fx} = FX^*\), which gives the complex conjugate of \(X/F\): the magnitude is right but the phase has the wrong sign. With \(G_{xf} = XF^*\) in the numerator of \(\hat H_1\), and \(G_{fx}\) in the denominator of \(\hat H_2\), both give \(X/F\). The coherence is unaffected.

Measuring FRFs is a three-step chain: measure the force \(f(n)\) and response \(x(n)\) (impact hammer or shaker, accelerometers or other sensors, DAQ); process the signals (FFT, auto and cross PSDs); calculate the FRFs.

MDOF FRFs

An \(N\)-DOF system has \(N^2\) FRFs, collected in the FRF matrix: \(H_{pq}(\omega) = X_p/F_q\) is the response at \(p\) to a force at \(q\). Measured over \(N_f\) frequencies the data is \(N \times N \times N_f\). A 2DOF system has four FRFs:

\[ H(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix} H_{11}(\omega) & H_{12}(\omega) \\ H_{21}(\omega) & H_{22}(\omega) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad H_{21} = H_{12} \;\text{(reciprocity: the matrix is symmetric)}. \]

\(H_{11}\) and \(H_{22}\), with force and response at the same point, are driving point FRFs. Between their peaks they show an anti-resonance, a deep dip where that point barely moves.

By modal superposition, each FRF is a sum of SDOF-like terms, one per mode:

Modal superposition\[ H_{pq}(\omega) = \sum_{r=1}^{N}\frac{u_{rp}u_{rq}/m_r}{(\omega_r^2 - \omega^2) + j\,2\zeta_r\omega_r\omega} = \sum_{r=1}^{N}\left(\frac{A_r}{j\omega - \lambda_r} + \frac{A_r^*}{j\omega - \lambda_r^*}\right) \]
\(u_{rp}\)
Mode shape \(r\) at point \(p\).
\(m_r\)
Modal mass, used to scale the mode shapes (unity modal mass is common).
\(\omega_r, \zeta_r\)
Natural frequency and damping ratio of mode \(r\).

Natural frequencies and damping need only one FRF: read each peak and apply the half-power rule to it. Mode shapes need one row or one column of the FRF matrix (one force position with responses everywhere, or one response position with the force moved around). Near \(\omega_r\) one term dominates, so with \(m_r = 1\):

Mode shapes from FRF peaks\[ |H_{11}(\omega_r)| \approx \frac{u_{r1}^2}{2\zeta_r\omega_r^2} \Rightarrow |u_{r1}| \approx \sqrt{2\zeta_r\omega_r^2|H_{11}(\omega_r)|}, \qquad |u_{r2}| \approx \frac{2\zeta_r\omega_r^2|H_{21}(\omega_r)|}{|u_{r1}|} \]
\(u_{r1}, u_{r2}\)
Mode shape \(r\) at points 1 and 2 (mass-normalised).
\(H_{11}, H_{21}\)
Driving point and transfer FRFs at the peak of mode \(r\).

The signs come from the phase or imaginary part: at the peak, points moving in phase have imaginary parts of the same sign. A point where a mode has zero amplitude is a node: an FRF measured or excited at a node shows no peak for that mode, so avoid nodes when placing sensors and hammer hits.

Watch out

On the mode 2 slide the denominator of \(|H_{11}(\omega_2)|\) is written with \(\zeta_1\omega_1^2\); it should be \(\zeta_2\omega_2^2\). On the 2DOF derivation slide the coupling terms in the first equation should carry \(k_2\) and \(c_2\) (not \(k_1\), \(c_1\)), and the second Laplace equation should read \((m_2s^2 + c_2s + k_2)X_2 = (k_2 + c_2s)X_1\). With that, \(H_{11} = (m_2s^2 + c_2s + k_2)/D(s)\) with \(D = (m_1s^2 + (c_1 + c_2)s + k_1 + k_2)(m_2s^2 + c_2s + k_2) - (k_2 + c_2s)^2\).

Worked example 7.1 Modal parameters from two FRFs

The exercise system (\(m_1 = 8\) kg, \(m_2 = 2\) kg, \(k_1 = 24\) N/m, \(k_2 = 3\) N/m) with light damping \(c_1 = c_2 = 0.1\) N s/m. Using \(H_{11}\) and \(H_{21}\) computed on a fine frequency grid, estimate \(\omega_r\), \(\zeta_r\) and the mode shapes.

1
Mode 1: peak of \(|H_{11}|\) at 1.1103 rad/s, height 0.4004 m/N; half-power points 1.0922 and 1.1246 rad/s, so \(\zeta_1 \approx 0.0146\).
2
Mode 2: peak at 1.9069 rad/s, height 1.3475 m/N; half-power points 1.8852 and 1.9286 rad/s, so \(\zeta_2 \approx 0.0114\).
3
Mode 1 shape: \(u_{11} = \sqrt{2(0.0146)(1.1103)^2(0.4004)} = 0.1202\); \(|H_{21}| = 2.2026\) gives \(u_{12} = 0.6612\). Imaginary parts of \(H_{11}\) and \(H_{21}\) have the same sign: in phase.
4
Mode 2 shape: \(u_{21} = 0.3337\), \(|u_{22}| = 0.2346\); the imaginary parts have opposite signs, so \(u_{22} = -0.2346\).
5
Exact (state-space eigenvalues): \(\omega_r = 1.1123, 1.9071\) rad/s, \(\zeta_r = 0.0142, 0.0114\); undamped mass-normalised modes \((0.1170, 0.6672)\) and \((0.3336, -0.2341)\).
The FRF estimates agree with the exact values to within about 3 %. With the exercise's heavier damping (\(c = 1\) N s/m, giving \(\zeta_r = 0.144, 0.113\)) the peaks overlap and the half-power rule on \(H_{11}\) fails for mode 1; there, curve-fitting the modal superposition formula is needed.

Figure 7.1: Teal: driving point FRF H₁₁ (response of m₁ to a force on m₁). Amber: transfer FRF H₂₁ (response of m₂ to a force on m₁). The driving point FRF has an anti-resonance between the two peaks; the transfer FRF does not. 08What to know for assessments

The module's overview for this week lists what you must understand for the assessments (the lectures cover more):

  • Experimental modal analysis: why it is done (identify dynamic properties, validate models) and, in principle, how (measure force and response, compute FRFs, extract modal parameters).
  • FRFs: how the FRF is derived; the four ways it is plotted (magnitude, phase, real, imaginary); displacement, velocity and acceleration FRFs; reading natural frequencies and damping from FRFs; in principle, deriving mode shapes from FRFs; the layout of the FRF matrix for MDOF systems; nodes in mode shapes.

09Practice problems

Problem 9.1. Lecture exercise 1: sample \(x(t) = 3\cos(2\pi 20t) + 5\cos(2\pi 40t)\) at \(f_s = 512\) Hz with \(N = 1024\), take a Hanning-windowed, scaled DFT. Is there leakage?

\(\Delta f = 512/1024 = 0.5\) Hz and \(T = 2\) s, so 20 Hz and 40 Hz are lines 40 and 80, with 40 and 80 whole periods in the block: no truncation leakage. With the Hanning window and \(A_w\), the single-sided spectrum reads exactly 3 and 5 at 20 and 40 Hz, with 1.5 and 2.5 on the lines either side: that spread is the window's own main lobe, not leakage. Without a window the spectrum would be exactly 3 and 5 on single lines. (The slides print the signal as \(3\cos(220t) + 5\cos(240t)\); the class notes show the \(2\pi\).)

Problem 9.2. Lecture exercise 2: PSD of a free response at \(f_s = 32\) Hz, \(N = 2048\), with \(\Delta f = 0.125\) Hz, Hanning, 50 % overlap. What block length and how many averages?

Block length \(N_b = f_s/\Delta f = 32/0.125 = 256\) samples (8 s). Step 128 samples: \(M = (2048 - 256)/128 + 1 = 15\) blocks. The random error is about \(1/\sqrt{M_e}\), a little over \(1/\sqrt{15} = 0.26\).

Problem 9.3. A signal sampled at 1 kHz contains a 700 Hz component and there is no anti-aliasing filter. Where does it appear?

Nyquist is 500 Hz. 700 Hz aliases to \(1000 - 700 = 300\) Hz.

Problem 9.4. You need 0.1 Hz resolution at \(f_s = 2048\) Hz. What block size and duration?

\(T = 1/\Delta f = 10\) s and \(N = f_sT = 20\,480\) samples (use 32 768 for a power of two, giving 0.0625 Hz).

Problem 9.5. An SDOF receptance FRF peaks at 12.0 Hz with half-power points at 11.7 and 12.3 Hz, and its low-frequency level is \(2\times10^{-6}\) m/N. Estimate \(\zeta\), \(k\) and \(m\).

\(\zeta \approx (12.3 - 11.7)/(2 \times 12.0) = 0.025\). \(k = 1/(2\times10^{-6}) = 500\) kN/m. \(m = k/\omega_n^2 = 500000/(2\pi \times 12)^2 = 88.0\) kg.

Problem 9.6. Why does the driving point FRF \(H_{11}\) of the exercise system have an anti-resonance at \(\sqrt{k_2/m_2}\)?

The numerator of \(H_{11}\) is \(m_2s^2 + c_2s + k_2\), which (for light damping) vanishes at \(\omega = \sqrt{k_2/m_2} = 1.225\) rad/s. There mass 2 on spring 2 vibrates so that the force it exerts on mass 1 cancels the applied force: it acts as a tuned absorber, and mass 1 barely moves.

Problem 9.7. A mode has a node at sensor position 2. What do you see in \(H_{21}\) and \(H_{22}\) near that mode?

No peak for that mode in either: \(u_{r2} = 0\) removes the mode's term from every FRF with point 2 as response or force. Move the sensor or the excitation point.

10Formula sheet

Sampling and DFT

\(f_s = 1/\Delta t\), \(T = N\Delta t\), \(f_s \ge 2f_{max}\)

\(X(k) = \sum x(n)e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\), \(x(n) = \frac1N\sum X(k)e^{j2\pi kn/N}\)

\(\Delta f = 1/T = f_s/N\); lines \(0\ldots N/2\) unique

Amplitude \(= 2|X(k)|/N\) (single-sided)

FFT: \(N\log_2N\) against \(N^2\)

Windows and PSD

Hanning \(w = \frac12(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi n}{N})\), \(A_w = N/\sum w\)

\(G_{xx} = |X|^2\), \(G_{xy} = XY^*\), rms \(= \sqrt{\int G_{xx}df}\)

\(\hat G_{xx} = \frac{S_p}{M}\sum|X_{w,m}|^2\), \(\varepsilon_r = 1/\sqrt M\)

Overlap 50 % for Hanning

FRFs

\(H = \frac{1/m}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2 + j2\zeta\omega_n\omega}\)

Mobility \(\times j\omega\), accelerance \(\times(-\omega^2)\)

\(\zeta \approx (\omega_b - \omega_a)/(2\omega_n)\) at \(|H|_{max}/\sqrt2\)

\(\hat H_1 = G_{xf}/G_{ff}\), \(\hat H_2 = G_{xx}/G_{fx}\), \(\gamma^2 = \hat H_1/\hat H_2\)

MDOF

\(H_{pq} = \sum_r\frac{u_{rp}u_{rq}/m_r}{\omega_r^2 - \omega^2 + j2\zeta_r\omega_r\omega}\)

\(|u_{r1}| \approx \sqrt{2\zeta_r\omega_r^2|H_{11}(\omega_r)|}\)

\(|u_{r2}| \approx 2\zeta_r\omega_r^2|H_{21}(\omega_r)|/|u_{r1}|\)

\(H_{pq} = H_{qp}\); node: \(u_{rp} = 0\)
