Beams in bending and vibration-based condition monitoring

The rod and shaft equations derived, the Euler-Bernoulli beam with its boundary conditions and the Table 6.6 procedure, Tutorial 4 worked in full, and how vibration monitoring diagnoses faults in rotating machinery.

01Rods and shafts revisited

Week 9 stated that rods and shafts obey the wave equation. The Lecture 13 notes derive both with the same four steps used for the string: free-body diagram of an element, force (or moment) balance, a constitutive law from Strength of Materials, and the limit.

Figure 2.1: The rod element of length \(dx\) carries axial force \(F\) on one face and \(F + dF\) on the other; the shaft element carries torque \(\tau\) and \(\tau + d\tau\).

Rod in longitudinal vibration

Newton's law on the element, with mass \(\rho A\,dx\) (\(\rho\) here is the volume density):

\[ (F + dF) - F = \rho A(x)\,dx\,\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial t^2}. \]

Hooke's law gives \(F = \sigma A = EA\,\partial w/\partial x\), and \(dF = (\partial F/\partial x)dx\), so

Rod equation of motion\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[EA(x)\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right] = \rho A(x)\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial t^2} \;\xrightarrow{A\text{ constant}}\; \frac{\partial^2w}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial t^2}, \quad c = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}} \]
\(w(x, t)\)
Axial displacement of the section at \(x\) (m).
\(E\)
Young's modulus (Pa).
\(A(x)\)
Cross-sectional area (m²).
\(\rho\)
Volume density (kg/m³).

Shaft in torsion

The moment balance on the element, with polar mass moment of inertia \(\rho J\,dx\), and the torsion law \(\tau = GJ\,\partial\theta/\partial x\) give

Shaft equation of motion\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[GJ(x)\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\right] = \rho J(x)\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial t^2} \;\xrightarrow{\text{uniform}}\; \frac{G\gamma}{\rho J}\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial t^2} \]
\(\theta(x, t)\)
Angle of twist (rad).
\(G\)
Shear modulus (Pa).
\(J\)
Polar second moment of area (m⁴).
\(\gamma\)
Torsional constant (m⁴), Inman Table 6.3; equal to \(J\) for a circular section.

Both are solved by separation of variables exactly as for the string. Inman Table 6.2 (rods) and Table 6.5 (shafts) list the frequencies and mode shapes for the common end conditions; the fixed-free and fixed-fixed cases are in the week 9 table.

Watch out

In the shaft equation the notes write \(GJ(x)\) in the derivation but \(G\gamma\) in the uniform form. They are the same thing for a circular shaft. For other sections the stiffness term is \(G\gamma\) while the inertia term stays \(\rho J\), so the general equation should read \(\partial_x[G\gamma\,\partial_x\theta] = \rho J\,\partial_t^2\theta\).

02Transverse vibration of beams

A rod carries axial load, a shaft torsion, and a beam transverse load. Bending of a beam gives a fourth-order equation:

Euler-Bernoulli beam, free vibration\[ \frac{\partial^2w(x, t)}{\partial t^2} + c^2\frac{\partial^4w(x, t)}{\partial x^4} = 0, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho A}} \]
\(w(x, t)\)
Transverse deflection (m).
\(E\)
Young's modulus (Pa).
\(I\)
Second moment of area about the bending axis (m⁴).
\(\rho A\)
Mass per unit length (kg/m).
\(c\)
Here a constant with units m²/s, not a wave speed.
Slender beams only

Euler-Bernoulli theory assumes the beam is slender: length at least 10 times the width or height. Otherwise shear deformation and rotary inertia matter and Timoshenko theory is needed. This course uses slender beams only.

Four spatial derivatives need four boundary conditions, two at each end, chosen from deflection \(w\), slope \(\partial w/\partial x\), bending moment \(EI\,\partial^2w/\partial x^2\) and shear force \(\partial_x(EI\,\partial^2w/\partial x^2)\). Two initial conditions are still needed for the time part.

Figure 3.1: Each end type fixes two of the four quantities to zero: what is prevented (deflection, slope) at a support, or what cannot be transmitted (moment, shear) at an unrestrained end.

  2. Look up \(\beta_nl\).

  6. Plot the mode shapes (engineering judgement or MATLAB).

Worked example 4.1 Clamped-sliding aluminium tube

A clamped-sliding aluminium beam (\(E = 80\) GPa, \(\rho = 2100\) kg/m³) is a tube of span 10 m, outside diameter 120 mm and wall thickness 6 mm. Find the first five natural frequencies and the mode shapes. (Lecture 13 example and Tutorial 4, additional problem 2a.)

Hollow circle: \(A = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.12^2 - 0.108^2) = 21.49\times10^{-4}\) m², \(I = \frac{\pi}{64}(0.12^4 - 0.108^4) = 3.5005\times10^{-6}\) m⁴.

Additional problem 1: pinned-sliding string

Worked example 5.1 String pinned at one end, sliding at the other

A string of length \(l\) has \(w(0, t) = 0\) and \(\partial w(l, t)/\partial x = 0\). (a) Find its natural frequencies. (b) Sketch the first three mode shapes. (c) Describe the response to \(w(x, 0) = \sin(\pi x/2)\), released from rest.

1
\(X = a_1\sin\sigma x + a_2\cos\sigma x\). \(X(0) = 0\) gives \(a_2 = 0\); \(X'(l) = a_1\sigma\cos\sigma l = 0\) gives \(\cos\sigma l = 0\).
2
\(\sigma_n = \frac{(2n - 1)\pi}{2l}\), so \(\omega_n = \frac{(2n - 1)\pi}{2l}\sqrt{\tau/\rho}\): only odd multiples of the fundamental.
3
Mode shapes \(\sin((2n - 1)\pi x/2l)\): a quarter sine, three quarters, five quarters, each reaching the sliding end with zero slope.
4
Released from rest, so all \(c_n = 0\). \(d_n = \frac{2}{l}\int_0^l\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}\sin\sigma_nx\,dx\). With \(l = 1\) the initial shape is exactly mode 1, and orthogonality gives \(d_1 = 1\), \(d_n = 0\) for \(n > 1\).
The string oscillates purely in its first mode, \(w = \sin(\pi x/2)\cos(\pi ct/2)\), with no other modes ever appearing. The length is not printed on the tutorial figure; the clean result relies on \(l = 1\). For any other length the shape is not a mode and several \(d_n\) are non-zero.

Additional problem 2: FRF estimate

Part (a) is example 4.1. For part (b) the hammer strikes at \(x_f = L/10 = 1\) m and the accelerometer is at \(x_a = L/3\). The accelerance is a sum of modal terms (week 6), each scaled by the mode shape values at the two points:

\[ A(\omega) = \sum_r\frac{-\omega^2\,\phi_r(x_a)\phi_r(x_f)}{\omega_r^2 - \omega^2 + 2j\zeta_r\omega_r\omega}, \]

with \(\phi_r\) the mass-normalised mode shapes (\(\int_0^L\rho A\phi_r^2dx = 1\)).

Figure 5.1: Peaks appear at 2.2, 12.0 and 29.6 Hz. The mode 4 peak near 55 Hz is almost absent because \(x_a = 3.33\) m is only 0.012 m from a node of mode 4 (at 3.345 m) (mass-normalised \(\phi_4(x_a)\) is 1.4% of \(\phi_2(x_a)\)). Hammer close to the clamp: all modes are weakly excited.

Additional problem 3: fan on an I-beam

Worked example 5.2 Locating an unbalanced fan

A fan running at 900 rpm (15 Hz) is to be mounted between \(x = 1.5\) m and 8.5 m on a 10 m steel I-beam (\(A = 85.5\) cm², \(I_{yy} = 25\,000\) cm⁴, \(I_{zz} = 200\) cm⁴, \(E = 200\) GPa, \(\rho = 7850\) kg/m³). The fan rotates in the YZ plane, so its unbalance force acts in both y and z. (a), (b) First three clamped-clamped frequencies about each axis. (c) Choose a location. (d) What changes if the ends are pinned?

1
\(\rho A = 67.12\) kg/m. About yy: \(\sqrt{EI/\rho A} = 863.1\) m²/s; about zz: 77.20 m²/s.
2
Clamped-clamped (\(\beta_nl\) = 4.7300, 7.8532, 10.9956): yy 30.7, 84.7, 166.1 Hz; zz 2.75, 7.58, 14.85 Hz.
3
The fan at 15 Hz sits almost exactly on the third zz mode (14.85 Hz). It cannot be moved off resonance, so place it where that mode does not respond: at a node. Nodes of clamped-clamped mode 3 are at \(x/L = 0.358\) and 0.642, that is 3.58 m and 6.42 m, both in the allowed range.
4
Pinned-pinned (\(\beta_nl = n\pi\)): yy 13.6, 54.2 Hz; zz 1.21, 4.85, 10.91, 19.4 Hz. All frequencies drop (softer supports).
Mount at about 3.6 m (or 6.4 m) from an end. With pinned ends the first yy mode falls to 13.6 Hz, within 10% of 15 Hz, and mode 1 has no interior node: the beam would vibrate strongly in that direction wherever the fan is placed, so pinned ends would need a stiffer beam or a speed change.

06Vibration-based condition monitoring

Maintenance philosophies

PhilosophyBasis
Breakdown (on-line failure)Fix it when it fails.
Preventive (fixed time)Maintenance on calendar or running hours.
Predictive (condition based)Maintenance based on the known, measured condition.
Proactive (design out)Find the root cause and design it out.

Plotted against the number of failure events, preventive maintenance costs a lot to prevent few failures, corrective maintenance costs a lot in failures; total cost is lowest in the condition-based middle. Machines are classed as critical (high safety risk, repair cost or efficiency impact: proactive or predictive, possibly preventive), essential (moderate: preventive or predictive) and general purpose (low risk, little effect on production: breakdown or predictive).

Predictive maintenance

It works like medical diagnosis: faults are diagnosed from monitored symptoms and corrective action is prescribed. Advantages: no unnecessary disassembly (which can itself cause poor assembly), less unscheduled downtime, fewer wrecks, lower insurance cost, longer machine life. Disadvantages: needs very knowledgeable staff or contractors, and specialised equipment and training.

Monitor machines with a problem history daily, most machines monthly, proven machines quarterly, and more often once problems appear. Always measure under repeatable conditions: full load if possible and the same speed (about 10% speed difference is acceptable for fans and pumps). Techniques: vibration analysis (best for rotating and turbo machinery), acoustic emission (cracks in pipelines), oil and wear particle analysis, infrared thermography, ultrasound, and performance evaluation.

Vibration monitoring

Vibration monitoring is the regular measurement of machine vibration as part of a predictive maintenance programme. Readings are compared with past levels; a significant change indicates a developing fault. The objective is lead time for maintenance planning. It can be continuous (permanent transducers, costly), periodic (more cost-effective) or trended against a baseline.

  • Where: usually axial and radial on each bearing housing; mark the positions so they are repeatable; the transducer must be perpendicular to the surface; a triaxial sensor helps in tight spots.
  • Mounting, in order of preference: stud, adhesive, magnetic base, hand-held probe or stinger. Stud mounting gives the highest mounted resonance and so the widest usable frequency range; avoid uneven or rough surfaces and flexible couplings between sensor and bearing.
  • Time domain: single metrics such as RMS and peak, trended over time; they summarise a wide band of frequencies.
  • Frequency domain: FFT or PSD; individual peaks can be traced to particular components. Waterfall plots and spectrograms show how the spectrum changes with time or speed. Octave and 1/3-octave filters give band levels for stationary signals.
  • Instruments: permanently installed sensors give guiding metrics; handheld vibrometers and analysers are then used to diagnose a flagged fault; a laser vibrometer measures without contact (hot, moving or hazardous surfaces, or delicate finishes).
  • Linking the two domains: the area under the power spectral density equals the mean square, \(\int S(f)\,df = x_{rms}^2\), so the spectrum shows both which frequencies are present and how much each contributes. On a fan, the blade pass frequency (number of blades × shaft speed) is a typical peak to trend.
  • Acceptable levels: ISO 2372 machine classes, API 610/11/12/16/17, AGMA 6000-B96, IRD severity charts.

Diagnosing rotating machinery faults

Figure 6.1: Orders of running speed: 1× is the shaft rotation frequency. Unbalance gives a dominant, clean 1× peak; angular misalignment a 1× peak with harmonics, mainly axial; parallel misalignment a dominant 2× peak, mainly radial.

FaultSpectrumDirection and phase
Unbalance (rotation and geometric centrelines not coincident)Dominant 1×; amplitude grows with speed squared; clean waveformRadial. Vertical readings at the two bearings in phase, horizontal out of phase (static unbalance)
Bent shaft1× if bent near the centre, 2× if bent near the endsHigh axial (dominant) and radial; 180° axial phase difference; no radial phase difference between ends
Angular misalignment1× dominant, 2× and 3× may appearHigh axial at 1× and 2×; 180° axial phase across the coupling
Parallel misalignment2× dominant (two hits per revolution)Radial higher than axial; 180° radial phase across the coupling
Gear defectsGear mesh frequency \(GMF = N_{teeth} \times\) rpm with running-speed sidebands; 1× and 2×Distributed faults (eccentricity, misalignment) give sidebands close to GMF; a cracked tooth gives sidebands spread widely; a rising GMF peak indicates uniform wear

The unbalance force is \(F = m_0e\omega^2\) (week 4, rotating unbalance), which is why its 1× amplitude grows with the square of speed. Gear sidebands come from amplitude modulation: a damaged tooth meshes less smoothly once per revolution, modulating the mesh vibration and creating components at the sum and difference frequencies around the GMF.

Other faults that vibration can detect: eccentricity, looseness, belt drive problems, electrical faults, oil whip and whirl, cavitation, shaft cracks, rotor rubs, resonance, hydraulic and aerodynamic forces. Remedies for the two most common: balancing (adding small masses until the measured vibration is minimised, as in wheel balancing) and alignment. Thermography complements vibration: it shows a motor winding fault or a loose busbar as a local hot spot, ineffective cooling fins (possibly low oil), and the fill level of a tank, all without contact.

Rolling element bearing wear

The spectrum is split into zones: A, running speed and harmonics; B, bearing defect frequencies; C, bearing component natural frequencies (30 to 120 kHz); D, high frequency detection (above 120 kHz). Defect frequencies: BPFO and BPFI (ball pass, outer and inner race), FTF (cage) and BSF (ball spin).

  1. Stage 1: ultrasonic energy (above 20 kHz) only, hard to see with accelerometers; raceways look dull but undamaged.
  2. Stage 2: minute pits; rolling elements passing them ring the bearing natural frequencies (zone C); sidebands at defect frequency ± rpm appear late in the stage; zone D may double.
  3. Stage 3: defect frequencies and harmonics with many sidebands; wear visible; zone D may double again.
  4. Stage 4: pits merge into spalling; 1× rises; discrete peaks merge into a broadband noise floor; just before failure energy grows to extreme levels and the bearing is hot and noisy.
Watch out

The stage 3 slide says "1% to 1 hour of remaining life left". In the usual four-stage description (Technical Associates, Berry) that range belongs to stage 4; stage 3 is commonly put at roughly 1% to 5% of life remaining. Check with the lecturer which is meant.

A pinion with 20 teeth runs at 1480 rpm, and the accelerometer is on the pinion bearing. Where are the gear mesh peak and its first sidebands?

1
Shaft speed \(1480/60 = 24.67\) Hz.
2
\(GMF = 20 \times 24.67 = 493\) Hz.
3

Peak at 493 Hz with sidebands 24.7 Hz either side. Growing sidebands point to a fault on the gear whose shaft speed sets their spacing.

07Practice problems

Problem 7.1. A steel cantilever (clamped-free) is a 20 mm × 20 mm square bar, 0.5 m long. Find its first two bending natural frequencies (\(E = 200\) GPa, \(\rho = 7850\) kg/m³).

\(I = b^4/12 = 1.333\times10^{-8}\) m⁴, \(\rho A = 3.14\) kg/m, \(\sqrt{EI/\rho A} = 29.14\) m²/s. \(f_1 = 1.8751^2/0.25 \times 29.14/(2\pi) = 65.2\) Hz, \(f_2 = 409\) Hz. Slenderness 25, so Euler-Bernoulli is fine.

Problem 7.2. By what factor do the beam natural frequencies change if the length is doubled? If the section depth is doubled (bending about the width)?

Length: \(\omega \propto 1/l^2\), so they fall to a quarter. Depth: \(I \propto h^3\) and \(A \propto h\), so \(\sqrt{I/A} \propto h\) and the frequencies double.

Problem 7.3. Why are the clamped-clamped beam frequency ratios 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 (pinned-pinned 1 : 4 : 9) rather than 1 : 2 : 3 as for a string?

Beam frequencies scale with \(\beta_n^2\), and \(\beta_n\) grows roughly linearly with \(n\): a fourth-order equation in space against second order in time. The string's \(\omega_n\) is linear in \(\sigma_n\).

Problem 7.4. A pump shows a dominant 2× radial peak with 180° radial phase across the coupling. Diagnosis?

Parallel misalignment.

Problem 7.5. A fan shows a dominant 1× peak that quadruples when the speed doubles, with horizontal readings at the two bearings out of phase. Diagnosis and remedy?

Unbalance (force grows with speed squared). Balance the rotor.

Problem 7.6. Why is stud mounting preferred to a magnetic base for bearing monitoring?

The stud gives the stiffest connection and the highest mounted resonance, so the usable frequency range reaches the high frequencies where early bearing damage shows. A magnetic base lowers the resonance and the upper useful frequency.

08Formula sheet

Rods and shafts

Rod: \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho}\) (\(\rho\) in kg/m³)

Shaft: \(c = \sqrt{G\gamma/\rho J}\); circular \(\gamma = J\)

Beams

\(\ddot w + \frac{EI}{\rho A}\partial_x^4w = 0\); slender: \(l \geq 10h\)

\(\omega_n = (\beta_nl)^2\sqrt{EI/\rho A}/l^2\)

Clamped: \(w = w' = 0\); pinned: \(w = w'' = 0\); free: \(w'' = w^{(3)} = 0\); sliding: \(w' = w^{(3)} = 0\)

\(\beta_1l\): CF 1.8751, CS 2.3650, CP 3.9266, CC 4.7300, PP \(\pi\)

Condition monitoring

Unbalance 1×; angular misalignment 1× axial; parallel 2× radial; bent shaft 1× or 2×, axial

\(GMF = N_{teeth}\times\) shaft speed, sidebands \(\pm\) shaft speed

Bearing zones A (rpm), B (defects), C (30 to 120 kHz), D (> 120 kHz)
