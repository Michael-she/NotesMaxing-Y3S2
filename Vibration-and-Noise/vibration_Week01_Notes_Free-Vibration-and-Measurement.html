02The single-degree-of-freedom equation of motion

Vibration is the part of dynamics that deals with repetitive motion. It is described by a frequency (in rad/s or Hz) and an amplitude (a displacement, velocity or acceleration).

A single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) system needs one coordinate to describe its motion. The simplest is a mass \(m\) hanging on a spring of stiffness \(k\).

Figure 2.1: The spring stretches by \(\delta_{st}\) under the weight. The coordinate \(x(t)\) is measured downward from the static equilibrium position, so the total stretch is \(\delta_{st} + x\).

The spring force depends on the total extension from the spring's free length:

Spring force\[ f_k(t) = k\big(\delta_{st} + x(t)\big) \]
\(f_k\)
Spring force (N).
\(k\)
Spring stiffness (N/m).
\(\delta_{st}\)
Static deflection under the weight (m).
\(x\)
Displacement from static equilibrium, positive downward (m).

Newton's second law in the \(x\) direction gives \(mg - k(\delta_{st} + x) = m\ddot x\). At static equilibrium the spring carries the weight, \(k\delta_{st} = mg\), so the gravity terms cancel:

Undamped SDOF equation of motion\[ m\ddot x(t) + kx(t) = 0 \]
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(\ddot x\)
Acceleration of the mass (m/s²).
\(k\)
Spring stiffness (N/m).
\(x\)
Displacement from static equilibrium (m).
Key idea

Measure \(x\) from the static equilibrium position and gravity drops out of the equation of motion. Gravity only sets where equilibrium is. The relation \(k\delta_{st} = mg\) also gives the natural frequency from a static deflection: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\).

Other SDOF systems with the same form

Any system whose equation of motion has the form (inertia) × (acceleration) + (stiffness) × (coordinate) = 0 behaves the same way.

Shaft and disc (torsion)\[ J\ddot\theta + k\theta = 0, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{J}} \]
\(J\)
Mass moment of inertia of the disc (kg m²).
\(\ddot\theta\)
Angular acceleration (rad/s²).
\(k\)
Torsional stiffness of the shaft (N m/rad).
\(\theta\)
Angle of twist (rad).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).
Simple pendulum (small angles)\[ \ddot\theta + \frac{g}{l}\theta = 0, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{l}} \]
\(\ddot\theta\)
Angular acceleration (rad/s²).
\(g\)
Gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s².
\(l\)
Pendulum length (m).
\(\theta\)
Angle from the vertical (rad).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).
Watch out

The pendulum equation uses \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\), so it holds only for small swings. The full equation is \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\), which is nonlinear.

03Undamped free response

Free vibration is motion after an initial disturbance, with no external force acting. The disturbance is described by the initial conditions: the initial displacement \(x_0 = x(0)\) and initial velocity \(v_0 = \dot x(0)\).

Observation suggests the motion is periodic, so try \(x(t) = A\sin(\omega_n t + \phi)\). Then \(\ddot x = -\omega_n^2 A\sin(\omega_n t + \phi)\). Substituting into \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\) gives \(-m\omega_n^2 A\sin(\cdot) = -kA\sin(\cdot)\), which holds for all \(t\) when

Natural frequency, period\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f_n} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_n} \]
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(f_n\)
Natural frequency in hertz (Hz).
\(T\)
Period of one oscillation (s).
Definition

The natural frequency is the frequency at which a system vibrates after being disturbed from equilibrium, with no other periodic force acting on it. It depends only on the system (\(k\) and \(m\)), not on the initial conditions.

The amplitude \(A\) and phase \(\phi\) are the two constants of integration. Setting \(t = 0\) gives \(x_0 = A\sin\phi\) and \(v_0 = \omega_n A\cos\phi\). Solving:

Undamped free response\[ x(t) = A\sin(\omega_n t + \phi), \qquad A = \frac{\sqrt{\omega_n^2x_0^2 + v_0^2}}{\omega_n}, \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega_n x_0}{v_0} \]
\(x(t)\)
Displacement at time \(t\) (m).
\(A\)
Amplitude (m).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).
\(t\)
Time (s).
\(\phi\)
Phase (rad).
\(x_0\)
Initial displacement (m).
\(v_0\)
Initial velocity (m/s).
Watch out

\(\tan^{-1}\) on a calculator returns an angle between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\). The phase must satisfy both \(\sin\phi = x_0/A\) and \(\cos\phi = v_0/(\omega_n A)\). If \(v_0 \lt 0\), add \(\pi\) to the calculator result (or use atan2(wn*x0, v0)). If \(v_0 = 0\), \(\phi = \pi/2\) for \(x_0 \gt 0\).

Worked example 3.1 Undamped free response

A 2 kg mass on a spring of stiffness 800 N/m is released from \(x_0 = 5\) mm with \(v_0 = 0.3\) m/s. Find \(\omega_n\), \(f_n\), the period, the amplitude, the phase and the peak velocity and acceleration.

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{800/2} = 20\) rad/s, \(f_n = 20/2\pi = 3.183\) Hz, \(T = 2\pi/20 = 0.314\) s.
2
\[ A = \frac{\sqrt{20^2(0.005)^2 + 0.3^2}}{20} = \frac{\sqrt{0.01 + 0.09}}{20} = 0.01581\text{ m} \]
3
\(\phi = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{20 \times 0.005}{0.3} = \tan^{-1}(0.3333) = 0.3218\) rad. Here \(v_0 \gt 0\), so no quadrant correction is needed.
4
Peak velocity \(\omega_n A = 0.316\) m/s; peak acceleration \(\omega_n^2 A = 6.32\) m/s².
\(x(t) = 15.81\sin(20t + 0.3218)\) mm, with \(f_n = 3.18\) Hz and \(T = 0.314\) s. The first maximum is at \(t = (\pi/2 - 0.3218)/20 = 0.0625\) s.

Figure 3.1: The motion starts at 5 mm and rises, because \(v_0\) is positive, to the amplitude of 15.81 mm (amber lines). With no damping the amplitude never decays. The period between maxima is 0.314 s.

Complex exponential form

A more general method assumes \(x(t) = ae^{\lambda t}\). Substituting gives the characteristic equation \(m\lambda^2 + k = 0\), so \(\lambda = \pm\sqrt{-k/m} = \pm\omega_n j\). A second-order equation has two independent solutions, and they add:

Complex form of harmonic motion\[ x(t) = a_1e^{j\omega_n t} + a_2e^{-j\omega_n t} \]
\(a_1, a_2\)
Complex constants of integration (m), a conjugate pair for a real \(x\).
\(j\)
Imaginary unit, \(j^2 = -1\).
\(\omega_n\)
Natural frequency (rad/s).

The Euler relations \(2j\sin\theta = e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}\) and \(2\cos\theta = e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta}\) turn this into \(x(t) = (a_1 + a_2)\cos\omega_n t + j(a_1 - a_2)\sin\omega_n t\). Writing the real form as \(A\sin(\omega_n t + \phi) = A_1\sin\omega_n t + A_2\cos\omega_n t\) and comparing:

Real and complex constants\[ A_1 = (a_1 - a_2)j, \quad A_2 = a_1 + a_2, \quad A = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2}, \quad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{A_2}{A_1} \]
\(A_1, A_2\)
Real amplitudes of the sine and cosine parts (m).
\(a_1, a_2\)
Complex constants (m); \(a_1 = (A_2 - A_1j)/2\), \(a_2 = (A_2 + A_1j)/2\).
\(A\)
Amplitude (m).
\(\phi\)
Phase (rad).
Why bother

The exponential trial solution is the method that carries over to damped systems, where the roots \(\lambda\) become complex with a negative real part. For the undamped case, \(A_1 = v_0/\omega_n\) and \(A_2 = x_0\).

04Quantifying vibration levels

A single number is often needed to describe how much something vibrates. Three are used:

  • The peak value, the largest magnitude reached.
  • The mean (average) value. For an oscillation about equilibrium it is zero, so it says little about vibration level.
  • The root-mean-square (rms) value, which relates to the energy in the signal.
Mean and rms\[ \bar x = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,dt, \qquad x_{rms} = \left[\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x^2(t)\,dt\right]^{1/2} \]
\(\bar x\)
Mean value (same unit as \(x\)).
\(T\)
Averaging time (s).
\(x(t)\)
The signal: displacement, velocity or acceleration.
\(x_{rms}\)
Root-mean-square value (same unit as \(x\)).

For a sine of amplitude \(X\), the mean is 0 and the rms is \(X/\sqrt2 = 0.707X\).

Figure 4.1: Two periods of a sine of amplitude \(X\). The rms (light teal) sits at 70.7 % of the peak (amber). The mean is zero because the positive and negative halves cancel.

Velocity and acceleration levels differ by orders of magnitude, so levels are often given on a logarithmic decibel (dB) scale relative to a reference value:

Decibel level\[ \text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{x_{rms}}{x_{ref}}\right)^2 = 20\log_{10}\frac{x_{rms}}{x_{ref}} \]
\(x_{rms}\)
Rms value of the measured quantity.
\(x_{ref}\)
Reference value (ISO 1683:2015): \(v_{ref} = 10^{-9}\) m/s for velocity, \(a_{ref} = 10^{-6}\) m/s² for acceleration.
Worked example 4.1 Levels in dB

A machine casing vibrates harmonically at 50 Hz with a displacement amplitude of 0.1 mm. Find the rms velocity and acceleration and their levels in dB.

1
\(\omega = 2\pi(50) = 314.2\) rad/s.
2
Peak velocity \(\omega X = 314.2 \times 10^{-4} = 0.03142\) m/s, so \(v_{rms} = 0.03142/\sqrt2 = 0.02221\) m/s.
3
Peak acceleration \(\omega^2 X = 9.870\) m/s², so \(a_{rms} = 6.979\) m/s².
4
\(L_v = 20\log_{10}(0.02221/10^{-9}) = 146.9\) dB; \(L_a = 20\log_{10}(6.979/10^{-6}) = 136.9\) dB.
\(v_{rms} = 22.2\) mm/s (146.9 dB re \(10^{-9}\) m/s) and \(a_{rms} = 6.98\) m/s² (136.9 dB re \(10^{-6}\) m/s²).
Watch out

Always state the reference with a dB value. The same vibration gives a different number of decibels for velocity and for acceleration, because the references differ.

05Viscously damped free vibration

Real free vibration always dies away. Friction, air resistance and other effects dissipate the energy, mostly as heat. This is damping. The simplest model is the viscous damper, like a shock absorber, where oil forced through a small opening resists motion with a force proportional to velocity.

Figure 5.1: The mass slides on a frictionless surface, so the weight \(mg\) is carried by the normal forces \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) and does not enter the horizontal equation. For a positive displacement and velocity, both the spring and the damper pull the mass back toward the wall.

Viscous damping force\[ f_c(t) = c\,\dot x(t) \]
\(f_c\)
Damping force (N).
\(c\)
Viscous damping coefficient (N s/m).
\(\dot x\)
Velocity of the mass (m/s).

Newton's second law, \(-f_k - f_c = m\ddot x\), gives:

Damped SDOF equation of motion\[ m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0 \]
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(\ddot x\)
Acceleration (m/s²).
\(c\)
Viscous damping coefficient (N s/m).
\(\dot x\)
Velocity (m/s).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(x\)
Displacement (m).

Try \(x = ae^{\lambda t}\) again. The characteristic equation is \(m\lambda^2 + c\lambda + k = 0\), with roots

\[ \lambda_{1,2} = -\frac{c}{2m} \pm \frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{c^2 - 4km}. \]

The sign of the discriminant \(c^2 - 4km\) decides the type of motion. It is zero at the critical damping coefficient, and the ratio of the actual damping to this value is the damping ratio:

Critical damping and damping ratio\[ c_{cr} = 2m\omega_n = 2\sqrt{km}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{c_{cr}} = \frac{c}{2m\omega_n} = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} \]
\(c_{cr}\)
Critical damping coefficient (N s/m).
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio (dimensionless).
\(c\)
Damping coefficient (N s/m).

In terms of \(\zeta\) and \(\omega_n\) the roots are

Characteristic roots\[ \lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} \]
\(\lambda_{1,2}\)
Roots of the characteristic equation (1/s).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
CaseDamping ratioRootsMotion
Undamped\(\zeta = 0\)\(\pm\omega_n j\)Oscillates for ever at \(\omega_n\)
Underdamped\(0 \lt \zeta \lt 1\)Complex conjugate pairDecaying oscillation at \(\omega_d\)
Critically damped\(\zeta = 1\)Repeated real, \(-\omega_n\)No oscillation; the least damping that gives none
Overdamped\(\zeta \gt 1\)Two distinct negative realNo oscillation; slow creep back to zero

Underdamped motion, \(0 \lt \zeta \lt 1\)

The discriminant is negative: \(\sqrt{\zeta^2-1} = j\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), so \(\lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\,j\). The solution \(x = e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\big(a_1e^{j\omega_d t} + a_2e^{-j\omega_d t}\big)\) becomes, with the Euler relations:

Underdamped free response\[ x(t) = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi), \qquad \omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} \] \[ A = \sqrt{\frac{(v_0 + \zeta\omega_n x_0)^2 + (x_0\omega_d)^2}{\omega_d^2}}, \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{x_0\omega_d}{v_0 + \zeta\omega_n x_0} \]
\(A\)
Amplitude constant: the height of the decay envelope at \(t = 0\) (m).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(t\)
Time (s).
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(\phi\)
Phase (rad).
\(v_0, x_0\)
Initial velocity (m/s) and displacement (m).
Intuition

The response is a sine at the damped natural frequency \(\omega_d\), slightly lower than \(\omega_n\), squeezed inside the decaying envelope \(\pm Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\). The envelope decays with time constant \(1/(\zeta\omega_n)\): the larger \(\zeta\omega_n\), the faster the vibration dies.

Watch out

The same quadrant rule applies to \(\phi\): if \(v_0 + \zeta\omega_n x_0 \lt 0\), add \(\pi\) to the calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\). The lecture's MATLAB code uses atan, which has this weakness; atan2 does not.

Worked example 5.1 Underdamped response and its first extreme

A 2 kg mass is supported by a spring (\(k = 50\) N/m) and a damper (\(c = 2\) N s/m). It is pulled to \(x_0 = 50\) mm and released from rest. Classify the damping, write \(x(t)\), and find the time and value of the first velocity zero after release.

1
\(\omega_n = \sqrt{50/2} = 5\) rad/s; \(c_{cr} = 2\sqrt{50 \times 2} = 20\) N s/m; \(\zeta = 2/20 = 0.1\). Underdamped.
2
\(\omega_d = 5\sqrt{1 - 0.01} = 4.975\) rad/s, so the damped period is \(T_d = 2\pi/\omega_d = 1.263\) s.
3
\(\zeta\omega_n x_0 = 0.1(5)(0.05) = 0.025\) m/s and \(x_0\omega_d = 0.2487\) m/s, so \[ A = \frac{\sqrt{0.025^2 + 0.2487^2}}{4.975} = 0.05025\text{ m}, \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{0.2487}{0.025} = 1.471\text{ rad.} \]
4
Set the velocity to zero: \[ \dot x = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\big[-\zeta\omega_n\sin(\omega_d t+\phi) + \omega_d\cos(\omega_d t+\phi)\big] = 0 \] This gives \(\tan(\omega_d t + \phi) = \omega_d/(\zeta\omega_n) = 9.950\), so \(\omega_d t + \phi = 1.471 + n\pi\). The \(n = 0\) solution is \(t = 0\) (release from rest), so the next is \(t = \pi/\omega_d = 0.6315\) s.
5
\[ x(0.6315) = 0.05025\,e^{-0.5(0.6315)}\sin(4.975 \times 0.6315 + 1.471) = -0.0365\text{ m} \]
\(x(t) = 50.25\,e^{-0.5t}\sin(4.975t + 1.471)\) mm. Half a damped period later, at \(t = 0.632\) s, the mass reaches its first minimum of \(-36.5\) mm. Each full period the envelope shrinks by \(e^{-0.5 \times 1.263} = 0.532\).
Lecture example

The lecture's example (\(m = 1\) kg, \(k = 20\) N/m, \(c = 1\) N s/m, \(x_0 = 0.1\) m, \(v_0 = 2\) m/s) uses the same method for the maximum displacement: \(\zeta = 0.1118\), \(\omega_n = 4.472\) rad/s, \(\omega_d = 4.444\) rad/s, \(A = 0.4720\) m, \(\phi = 0.2135\) rad, and the first peak is at \(t = \big[\tan^{-1}(\omega_d/\zeta\omega_n) - \phi\big]/\omega_d = 0.2802\) s with \(x = 0.408\) m. These values check out.

Overdamped and critically damped motion

For \(\zeta \gt 1\) both roots are real and negative, and the response is a sum of two decaying exponentials. It does not oscillate.

Overdamped free response, \(\zeta \gt 1\)\[ x(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(a_1e^{-\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\,t} + a_2e^{\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\,t}\right) \] \[ a_1 = \frac{-v_0 + \left(-\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\right)\omega_n x_0}{2\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}, \qquad a_2 = \frac{v_0 + \left(\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\right)\omega_n x_0}{2\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}} \]
\(a_1, a_2\)
Real constants of integration (m); \(a_1 + a_2 = x_0\).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio, greater than 1.
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(v_0, x_0\)
Initial velocity (m/s) and displacement (m).

For \(\zeta = 1\) the roots are equal, \(\lambda_{1,2} = -\omega_n\), and the second solution carries a factor \(t\):

Critically damped free response, \(\zeta = 1\)\[ x(t) = (a_1 + a_2t)e^{-\omega_n t}, \qquad a_1 = x_0, \quad a_2 = v_0 + \omega_n x_0 \]
\(a_1\)
Constant equal to the initial displacement (m).
\(a_2\)
Constant (m/s).
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
Key idea

Critical damping is the smallest amount of damping that gives a nonoscillatory response, and it returns to zero fastest of the nonoscillatory cases. More damping than that makes the return slower, not faster.

Worked example 5.2 Overdamped and critically damped constants

A system with \(\omega_n = 10\) rad/s is released from rest at \(x_0 = 10\) mm. Find \(x(0.2\text{ s})\) for \(\zeta = 1.5\) and for \(\zeta = 1\).

1
\(\zeta = 1.5\): \(\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} = 1.118\). Roots \(\lambda = -15 \mp 11.18\), that is \(-26.18\) and \(-3.820\) s⁻¹.
2
\(a_1 = \dfrac{0 + (-1.5 + 1.118)(10)(0.01)}{2(10)(1.118)} = -1.708\) mm; \(a_2 = \dfrac{0 + (1.5 + 1.118)(10)(0.01)}{22.36} = 11.71\) mm. Check: \(a_1 + a_2 = 10\) mm ✓.
3
\[ x(0.2) = -1.708e^{-26.18(0.2)} + 11.71e^{-3.820(0.2)} = 5.44\text{ mm} \]
4
\(\zeta = 1\): \(a_1 = 10\) mm, \(a_2 = 0 + 10(10) = 100\) mm/s. \(x(0.2) = (10 + 100 \times 0.2)e^{-2} = 4.06\) mm.
\(x(0.2) = 5.44\) mm when overdamped (\(\zeta = 1.5\)) and 4.06 mm when critically damped: the critically damped system is closer to equilibrium.

06Measuring damping: the logarithmic decrement

Damping cannot be calculated from a drawing the way mass and stiffness can. It is measured. For underdamped motion, the ratio of two displacements one damped period \(T\) apart depends only on the damping, because the sine part repeats exactly:

\[ \frac{x(t)}{x(t+T)} = \frac{Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)}{Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n (t+T)}\sin(\omega_d t + 2\pi + \phi)} = e^{\zeta\omega_n T}. \]

The natural log of this ratio is the logarithmic decrement. With \(T = 2\pi/\omega_d\) it gives \(\delta = 2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), which inverts to:

Logarithmic decrement\[ \delta = \ln\frac{x(t)}{x(t+T)} = \frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{x(t)}{x(t+nT)}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}} \]
\(\delta\)
Logarithmic decrement (dimensionless).
\(x(t)\)
Displacement at a peak (m).
\(x(t+T)\)
Displacement at the next peak, one damped period later (m).
\(n\)
Number of periods between the two peaks used.
\(x(t+nT)\)
Displacement \(n\) peaks later (m).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
Watch out

Use peaks on the same side of zero, a whole number of periods apart. Measuring over several periods (\(n \gt 1\)) is more accurate, because the ratio is larger and reading errors matter less. The \(\delta\) here is not the static deflection \(\delta_{st}\) of section 2.

Figure 6.1: The data of worked example 6.1, drawn with a damped frequency of 2 Hz. The peaks (dots) all touch the dashed envelope. The ratio of any two peaks \(n\) periods apart is \(e^{n\delta}\), whichever pair you choose.

Worked example 6.1 Damping ratio from a decay

A free-decay record shows a peak of 12.0 mm and, five cycles later, a peak of 4.1 mm. Find \(\delta\) and \(\zeta\).

1
\(\delta = \dfrac{1}{5}\ln\dfrac{12.0}{4.1} = \dfrac{1.0739}{5} = 0.2148\).
2
\(\zeta = \dfrac{0.2148}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + 0.2148^2}} = 0.03416\).
\(\delta = 0.215\) and \(\zeta = 0.0342\) (3.4 % of critical). For light damping \(\zeta \approx \delta/2\pi = 0.0342\) as well.

07Vibration measurement

Measurement turns the motion of a real structure into numbers that can be compared with a model. The material follows the measurement lecture: how measurements are represented, what is measured, how, and why.

Types of signal, sampling and spectra

  • Periodic signals consist of discrete frequencies and repeat over a fixed time interval. Example: a washing machine or fan running at constant speed.
  • Random signals have values at any instant that are independent of the values at any other instant. Example: a car driving on a rough gravel road.
  • Transient signals decay over some time period. Example: a structure struck once and left to ring down, the free response of section 5.

Figure 7.1: The periodic signal is the sum of sines at 1, 4 and 12 Hz, so the same pattern repeats every second. The random signal never repeats. The transient is a 3 Hz decay that sinks into a small level of measurement noise.

Sensors output an analogue signal, usually a voltage. The data acquisition hardware converts it to the digital time domain by sampling it at regular intervals \(\Delta t\):

Sample rate\[ f_s = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \]
\(f_s\)
Sample rate or sampling frequency (Hz, samples per second).
\(\Delta t\)
Time between samples (s).

Any time-domain signal can also be represented in the frequency domain, called its spectrum. One such representation is the power spectral density (PSD), which shows how the power of the signal is spread over frequency, in units of (signal unit)²/Hz.

Signal typeWhat its PSD looks like
PeriodicSharp peaks at the discrete frequencies it contains, nothing in between
RandomPower spread over a band of frequencies (roughly flat for white noise)
Transient (free decay)A peak at the damped natural frequency, on a broad noise background

The frequency domain is useful when analysing forced vibration, when a signal contains vibration from several sources (each shows up at its own frequency), and when quantifying noise.

What we measure

The commonly measured quantities are displacement, velocity, acceleration and strain. For harmonic motion at frequency \(\omega\) they are related by differentiation:

Harmonic displacement, velocity, acceleration\[ x(t) = A\cos\omega t, \qquad \dot x(t) = -\omega A\sin\omega t, \qquad \ddot x(t) = -\omega^2A\cos\omega t \]
\(A\)
Displacement amplitude (m).
\(\omega\)
Frequency of vibration (rad/s).
\(\dot x\)
Velocity (m/s), amplitude \(\omega A\).
\(\ddot x\)
Acceleration (m/s²), amplitude \(\omega^2A\).

Figure 7.2: At a fixed velocity amplitude, displacement falls by a factor 10 per decade of frequency and acceleration rises by a factor 10. At 1 Hz the displacement is 159 µm and the acceleration 6.3 mm/s²; at 10 kHz the displacement is only 0.016 µm while the acceleration is 63 m/s². Displacement suits low frequencies, acceleration suits high ones, and velocity is the most even across the range.

The metrics of section 4 are used to quantify the level of a measured signal: the peak, the mean and the rms value.

Measurement chain, sensors and DAQ

Figure 7.3: The sensor converts motion into a voltage. The data acquisition (DAQ) system removes spurious frequency components with a low-pass filter and converts the analogue signal to digital samples. Software stores the samples and processes them, for example into a spectrum.

QuantitySensor
DisplacementDisplacement probe, or linear variable differential transformer (LVDT)
VelocityLaser Doppler vibrometer (LDV), a non-contact optical sensor
AccelerationAccelerometer
StrainStrain gauge

The DAQ system has two jobs: convert the analogue sensor output to a digital time-domain signal, and remove spurious frequency components with a low-pass filter before sampling.

Accelerometers

The most common vibration sensor is the piezoelectric accelerometer. Some materials, such as certain ceramics and quartz crystal, produce an electric charge when deformed. This is the piezoelectric effect:

Piezoelectric effect\[ q = K \times F \]
\(q\)
Charge produced by the crystal (C).
\(K\)
Material constant (C/N).
\(F\)
Force applied to the crystal (N).

Figure 7.4: The stiff base is mounted on the test structure. The crystal sits on the base with the seismic mass on top. As the sensor accelerates, the mass exerts a force \(F = ma\) on the crystal, so the charge is proportional to acceleration. The charge is converted to a voltage and measured.

DC accelerometers use a mass on a flexure and sense its deflection with resistors (resistive principle) or capacitor plates (capacitive principle). Unlike piezoelectric sensors, they respond down to 0 Hz, so they can measure constant (DC) and very slow accelerations.

An accelerometer is described by four characteristics:

  • Sensitivity converts the output voltage to the physical quantity. It is found by calibration with a known input. Example: at 100 mV/g, an output of 1 V means 10 g.
  • Dynamic range is the ratio between the smallest and the largest amplitude the sensor can measure, given as a ratio or in decibels.
  • Noise floor is the lowest level the sensor can measure. Vibration below it is lost in the sensor's own noise.
  • Frequency characteristics are the range of frequencies the sensor can measure, usually given as lower and upper limits.
Worked example 7.1 Sensitivity and dynamic range

An accelerometer has a sensitivity of 10 mV/(m/s²). (a) It outputs 0.35 V rms; find the acceleration and its level. (b) Another sensor measures from 0.0005 g to 500 g; find its dynamic range.

1
(a) \(a_{rms} = 0.35/0.010 = 35\) m/s². Level \(20\log_{10}(35/10^{-6}) = 150.9\) dB re \(10^{-6}\) m/s².
2
(b) Ratio \(500/0.0005 = 10^6\), which is \(20\log_{10}(10^6) = 120\) dB.
(a) 35 m/s² rms, 150.9 dB. (b) A dynamic range of \(10^6\) : 1, or 120 dB.

The slide comparing PSDs of acceleration shows DC accelerometers levelling out at a higher noise floor than piezoelectric ones, which keep resolving the low-level peaks.

Practical considerations

Mounting, from least to most secure: a probe pressed onto the surface, a magnet, adhesive, a threaded stud. Typical mounting errors come from an uneven surface, a rough surface, or mounting on a flexible component that vibrates on its own.

Placement: where the acceleration level is highest, with the sensor aligned to the direction of interest, accessible, protected from the environment, and with cables and connections secured.

Choice of sensor: sensitivity, frequency range, physical size, signal conditioning, environmental conditions and cost.

Calibration: calibrate accelerometers regularly by applying a known input and comparing the sensor's response with it.

Mass loading rule

An accelerometer's mass should not exceed 10 % of the test structure's mass. A heavier sensor adds mass to the structure and lowers the natural frequency you are trying to measure (\(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}\)).

08System parameters from a measured response

A main reason to measure is to identify a system's dynamic characteristics (mass, damping, stiffness, natural frequency) and to compare the measurement with an analytical model. In the practical, an accelerometer on a mass-spring rig records the free decay after the mass is disturbed. If the mass is known (weighed), the rest follows.

Identification from a free decay\[ \omega_d = N\frac{2\pi}{T_N}, \qquad \delta = \frac{1}{N}\ln\frac{\ddot x(t)}{\ddot x(t+T_N)} \] \[ \zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}}, \qquad \omega_n = \frac{\omega_d}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \] \[ k = m\omega_n^2, \qquad c = \zeta c_{cr} = 2\zeta\sqrt{km} \]
\(\omega_d\)
Damped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(N\)
Number of complete cycles counted.
\(T_N\)
Time taken by those \(N\) cycles (s).
\(\delta\)
Logarithmic decrement.
\(\ddot x(t), \ddot x(t+T_N)\)
Acceleration peaks \(N\) cycles apart (m/s²).
\(\zeta\)
Damping ratio.
\(\omega_n\)
Undamped natural frequency (rad/s).
\(k\)
Stiffness (N/m).
\(m\)
Mass (kg).
\(c\)
Damping coefficient (N s/m).
Intuition

The log decrement can use acceleration peaks instead of displacement peaks. Differentiating \(Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\) twice gives another decaying sine with the same envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\) and the same period, so the ratio over \(N\) periods is still \(e^{N\delta}\).

Watch out

The slides use \(N\cdot 2\pi/T_N\) for \(\omega_d\) on one slide and for \(\omega_n\) on another (with \(k = m\omega_n^2\) directly). Counting cycles gives the damped frequency. Treating it as \(\omega_n\) is a good approximation only for light damping: at \(\zeta = 0.05\), \(\omega_d\) is 0.13 % below \(\omega_n\). For a heavily damped system, correct it with \(\omega_n = \omega_d/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\).

Worked example 8.1 Identifying a rig from its acceleration decay

A 0.8 kg mass on a spring is tapped. The accelerometer record shows 6 complete cycles in 0.75 s. An acceleration peak of 9.2 m/s² is followed 6 cycles later by a peak of 1.6 m/s². Find \(\omega_d\), \(\zeta\), \(\omega_n\), \(k\) and \(c\).

1
\(\omega_d = 6 \times 2\pi/0.75 = 50.27\) rad/s (8.00 Hz).
2
\(\delta = \tfrac{1}{6}\ln(9.2/1.6) = 0.2915\); \(\zeta = 0.2915/\sqrt{4\pi^2 + 0.2915^2} = 0.0463\).
3
\(\omega_n = 50.27/\sqrt{1 - 0.0463^2} = 50.32\) rad/s (8.01 Hz).
4
\(k = 0.8 \times 50.32^2 = 2026\) N/m.
5
\(c = 2(0.0463)\sqrt{2026 \times 0.8} = 3.73\) N s/m.
\(\zeta = 0.046\), \(\omega_n = 50.3\) rad/s, \(k \approx 2030\) N/m and \(c \approx 3.73\) N s/m. Using \(\omega_d\) in place of \(\omega_n\) would give \(k = 2021\) N/m, only 0.2 % low.

09Practice problems

Problem 9.1. A disc with \(J = 0.05\) kg m² is fixed to a shaft of torsional stiffness 200 N m/rad. Find its natural frequency in rad/s and Hz.

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/J} = \sqrt{200/0.05} = 63.25\) rad/s; \(f_n = 63.25/2\pi = 10.07\) Hz.

Problem 9.2. Find the period of a 0.5 m simple pendulum swinging through small angles.

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{9.81/0.5} = 4.429\) rad/s, so \(T = 2\pi/4.429 = 1.419\) s.

Problem 9.3. A machine sags 2 mm on its spring mounts under its own weight. Estimate its natural frequency.

\(k\delta_{st} = mg\), so \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}} = \sqrt{9.81/0.002} = 70.04\) rad/s, \(f_n = 11.15\) Hz. The mass and stiffness are not needed separately.

Problem 9.4. An undamped system with \(\omega_n = 40\) rad/s starts at \(x_0 = -3\) mm with \(v_0 = -0.2\) m/s. Find \(A\) and \(\phi\).

\(A = \sqrt{40^2(0.003)^2 + 0.2^2}/40 = 5.831\) mm. The calculator gives \(\tan^{-1}\big(40(-0.003)/(-0.2)\big) = \tan^{-1}(0.6) = 0.540\) rad, but \(\sin\phi = x_0/A\) is negative and \(\cos\phi = v_0/(\omega_nA)\) is negative, so \(\phi\) is in the third quadrant: \(\phi = 0.540 - \pi = -2.601\) rad. So \(x(t) = 5.831\sin(40t - 2.601)\) mm. Check: \(x(0) = 5.831\sin(-2.601) = -3.00\) mm ✓.

Problem 9.5. A 5 kg mass sits on a 2000 N/m spring. What damping coefficient makes it critically damped, and what gives \(\zeta = 0.05\)? What is \(\omega_d\) at \(\zeta = 0.05\)?

\(c_{cr} = 2\sqrt{2000 \times 5} = 200\) N s/m. For \(\zeta = 0.05\), \(c = 0.05 \times 200 = 10\) N s/m. \(\omega_n = \sqrt{2000/5} = 20\) rad/s and \(\omega_d = 20\sqrt{1 - 0.0025} = 19.97\) rad/s.

Problem 9.6. A critically damped system with \(\omega_n = 10\) rad/s starts at \(x_0 = 0\) with \(v_0 = 0.5\) m/s. Find the largest displacement and when it occurs.

\(a_1 = 0\), \(a_2 = 0.5\) m/s, so \(x = 0.5te^{-10t}\). \(\dot x = 0.5e^{-10t}(1 - 10t) = 0\) at \(t = 0.1\) s. \(x_{max} = 0.5(0.1)e^{-1} = 18.4\) mm.

Problem 9.7. A structure has \(\zeta = 0.02\). How many cycles does its free vibration take to decay to 10 % of its starting amplitude?

\(\delta = 2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} = 0.1257\). We need \(e^{-n\delta} = 0.1\), so \(n = \ln 10/\delta = 2.303/0.1257 = 18.3\). About 18 to 19 cycles.

Problem 9.8. Why does a piezoelectric accelerometer give a poor result on a structure swaying at 0.2 Hz, and what would you use instead?

Piezoelectric accelerometers cannot measure low frequencies: their lower frequency limit is above such slow motion. In addition, at 0.2 Hz the acceleration is small for a given displacement (\(\omega^2A\) with \(\omega = 1.26\) rad/s). Use a DC accelerometer, which responds down to 0 Hz, or measure displacement directly.

Problem 9.9. A 50 g accelerometer is to be glued to a thin plate of 0.3 kg. Is that acceptable?

The limit is 10 % of 0.3 kg, which is 30 g. A 50 g sensor (17 %) is too heavy: it would noticeably lower the plate's natural frequencies. Choose a smaller sensor or a non-contact method such as a laser Doppler vibrometer.

Equations of motion

\(m\ddot x + kx = 0\); \(J\ddot\theta + k\theta = 0\); \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\theta = 0\)

\(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0\)

\(k\delta_{st} = mg\)

Undamped response

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}\), \(f_n = \omega_n/2\pi\), \(T = 2\pi/\omega_n\)

\(x = A\sin(\omega_n t + \phi)\)

\(A = \sqrt{\omega_n^2x_0^2 + v_0^2}/\omega_n\), \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(\omega_nx_0/v_0)\)

Damping

\(c_{cr} = 2m\omega_n = 2\sqrt{km}\), \(\zeta = c/c_{cr}\)

\(\lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\)

\(\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\)

Underdamped, \(\zeta \lt 1\)

\(x = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\)

\(A = \sqrt{(v_0 + \zeta\omega_nx_0)^2 + (x_0\omega_d)^2}\,/\,\omega_d\)

\(\phi = \tan^{-1}\big(x_0\omega_d/(v_0 + \zeta\omega_nx_0)\big)\)

Overdamped and critical

\(\zeta \gt 1\): \(x = e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}(a_1e^{-\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}t} + a_2e^{\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}t})\)

\(a_{1,2} = \dfrac{\mp v_0 + (\mp\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2-1})\omega_nx_0}{2\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}\)

\(\zeta = 1\): \(x = (x_0 + (v_0 + \omega_nx_0)t)e^{-\omega_n t}\)

Log decrement and identification

\(\delta = \frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{x(t)}{x(t+nT)}\), \(\zeta = \delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}\)

\(\omega_d = N\,2\pi/T_N\), \(\omega_n = \omega_d/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\)

\(k = m\omega_n^2\), \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km}\)

Levels and measurement

\(x_{rms} = X/\sqrt2\) for a sine; velocity \(\omega X\), acceleration \(\omega^2X\)

dB \(= 20\log_{10}(x_{rms}/x_{ref})\); \(v_{ref} = 10^{-9}\) m/s, \(a_{ref} = 10^{-6}\) m/s²

\(f_s = 1/\Delta t\); \(q = KF\); sensor mass \(\le\) 10 % of structure
