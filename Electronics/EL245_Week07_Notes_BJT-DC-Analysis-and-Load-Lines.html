BJT DC analysis, load lines and transfer curves

How to find the DC currents and voltages in a bipolar transistor circuit, decide which mode the transistor is in, draw its load line and transfer curve, and design simple bias circuits that still work when the resistors are off by 10 %.

01The common-emitter circuit

Reading Neamen 5.2.1; Lecture 14, slides 3 to 9

A transistor circuit is named after the terminal shared by its input and output. In the common-emitter (CE) circuit the input drives the base, the output is taken at the collector, and the emitter is common to both. The other two configurations are common-base (CB) and common-collector (CC).

DC analysis finds the quiescent values: the currents and voltages with no signal applied. Together they are the Q-point.

Definition

Forward-active mode. The base-emitter junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased. In this mode the transistor is modelled as a fixed junction voltage \(V_{BE(on)}\) between base and emitter and a dependent current source \(\beta I_B\) from collector to emitter.

npn analysis

For the npn circuit in Figure 1.1, the base loop contains only \(V_{BB}\), \(R_B\) and the junction, so it gives \(I_B\) on its own. The collector current then follows from \(\beta\), and the collector loop gives \(V_{CE}\).

Figure 1.1: \(V_{BB}\) drives the base through \(R_B\). The collector current flows from \(V_{CC}\) through \(R_C\) into the collector, and \(I_E = I_B + I_C\) leaves the emitter to ground.

npn common emitter, forward-active\[ I_B = \frac{V_{BB} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_B} \qquad I_C = \beta I_B \qquad I_E = (1+\beta) I_B \qquad V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_C R_C \]
\(I_B\)
Base current (A).
\(V_{BB}\)
Supply driving the base resistor (V).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
Base-emitter turn-on voltage, 0.7 V unless stated otherwise.
\(R_B\)
Base resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(I_C\)
Collector current (A).
\(\beta\)
Common-emitter current gain (no unit).
\(I_E\)
Emitter current (A).
\(V_{CE}\)
Collector-emitter voltage (V).
\(V_{CC}\)
Collector supply voltage (V).
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).

The power the transistor turns into heat is the sum over its two junctions:

Transistor power dissipation\[ P_T = I_B V_{BE} + I_C V_{CE} \approx I_C V_{CE} \]
\(P_T\)
Power dissipated in the transistor (W).
\(I_B\)
Base current (A).
\(V_{BE}\)
Base-emitter voltage (V).
\(I_C\)
Collector current (A).
\(V_{CE}\)
Collector-emitter voltage (V).

The approximation holds when \(I_C \gg I_B\), which is true in forward-active mode. It does not hold in saturation, where \(V_{CE}\) is small and the base term is no longer negligible.

Worked example 1.1 npn Q-point and power

In Figure 1.1, \(V_{BB} = 3\ \text{V}\), \(R_B = 150\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 2.2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_{CC} = 12\ \text{V}\), \(\beta = 120\) and \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Find \(I_B\), \(I_C\), \(I_E\), \(V_{CE}\) and \(P_T\).

1
\(V_{BB} \gt V_{BE(on)}\), so the B-E junction is forward biased: \(I_B = (3 - 0.7)/150\ \text{k}\Omega = 15.33\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
Assume forward-active: \(I_C = 120 \times 15.33\ \mu\text{A} = 1.840\ \text{mA}\) and \(I_E = 121 \times 15.33\ \mu\text{A} = 1.855\ \text{mA}\).
3
Collector loop: \(V_{CE} = 12 - (1.840\ \text{mA})(2.2\ \text{k}\Omega) = 7.95\ \text{V}\).
4
Check: \(V_{CE} = 7.95\ \text{V} \gt V_{BE(on)}\), so the B-C junction is reverse biased and the assumption holds.
5
\(P_T = (15.33\ \mu\text{A})(0.7) + (1.840\ \text{mA})(7.95) = 14.6\ \text{mW}\).
\(I_B = 15.3\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 1.84\ \text{mA}\), \(I_E = 1.86\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CE} = 7.95\ \text{V}\), \(P_T = 14.6\ \text{mW}\): forward-active, with almost all the power in the collector junction.

pnp analysis

The pnp transistor works the same way with every polarity reversed. The emitter-base junction is forward biased (\(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\)), current flows out of the base and collector and into the emitter, and the output voltage is \(V_{EC}\). Circuits are usually drawn with the emitter at the top so that positive supplies can be used (Figure 1.2).

Figure 1.2: The emitter sits at \(V^+\). The base is pulled below it through \(R_B\) towards \(V_{BB}\), so \(I_B\) flows out of the base. \(I_C\) flows out of the collector through \(R_C\) to ground.

Base current, flowing out of the base (A).
\(V^+\)
Positive supply connected to the emitter (V).
\(V_{EB(on)}\)
Emitter-base turn-on voltage, 0.7 V.
\(V_{BB}\)
Voltage the base resistor returns to (V).
\(R_B\)
Base resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(I_C\)
Collector current, flowing out of the collector (A).
\(\beta\)
Current gain.
\(V_{EC}\)
Emitter-collector voltage (V).
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).
Watch out

Write every KVL loop with the arrows and polarities of the actual device. Copying the npn equations and only swapping subscripts is the usual way pnp answers come out negative. If a current comes out negative, the transistor is not on, not "on backwards".

Worked example 1.2 pnp with the emitter at V+

In Figure 1.2, \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\), \(V_{BB} = 1.5\ \text{V}\), \(R_B = 300\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(\beta = 100\) and \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Find \(I_B\), \(I_C\) and \(V_{EC}\).

1
The emitter (5 V) is more than 0.7 V above \(V_{BB}\) (1.5 V), so the E-B junction is forward biased.
2
Base loop from \(V^+\) down to \(V_{BB}\): \(I_B = (5 - 0.7 - 1.5)/300\ \text{k}\Omega = 9.33\ \mu\text{A}\).
3
\(I_C = 100 \times 9.33\ \mu\text{A} = 0.933\ \text{mA}\).
4
\(V_{EC} = 5 - (0.933\ \text{mA})(4\ \text{k}\Omega) = 1.27\ \text{V}\), which is more than \(V_{EC(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\): forward-active is confirmed.
\(I_B = 9.33\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 0.933\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{EC} = 1.27\ \text{V}\).

02Load lines and modes of operation

Reading Neamen 5.2.2; Lecture 14, slides 10 to 15

A load line is the straight-line I-V relation that the external circuit forces on the transistor. Plotting it on top of the transistor's own characteristics shows the Q-point as the intersection, and shows at a glance how close the circuit is to cutoff or saturation.

Input load line

The base loop gives a line in the \(i_B\)-\(v_{BE}\) plane. It meets the axes at \(v_{BE} = V_{BB}\) (no current) and \(i_B = V_{BB}/R_B\) (junction shorted). With the ideal junction model the transistor characteristic is a vertical line at \(V_{BE(on)}\), so the Q-point is where the load line crosses 0.7 V.

Input load line\[ i_B = \frac{V_{BB} - v_{BE}}{R_B} \]
\(i_B\)
Base current (A).
\(V_{BB}\)
Base supply (V).
\(v_{BE}\)
Base-emitter voltage (V).
\(R_B\)
Base resistor (\(\Omega\)).

Figure 2.1: The teal line runs from 20 µA at \(v_{BE} = 0\) to zero at \(v_{BE} = 3\ \text{V}\). The dark line is the ideal junction: no current below 0.7 V, any current at 0.7 V. They meet at \(I_{BQ} = 15.3\ \mu\text{A}\).

Output load line

The collector loop gives the output load line. Its intercepts are \(V_{CC}\) on the voltage axis and \(V_{CC}/R_C\) on the current axis. The transistor's output characteristics are a family of curves, one per base current. The Q-point is where the load line crosses the curve for \(I_{BQ}\).

Output load line (CE, no emitter resistor)\[ i_C = \frac{V_{CC} - v_{CE}}{R_C} \]
\(i_C\)
Collector current (A).
\(V_{CC}\)
Collector supply (V).
\(v_{CE}\)
Collector-emitter voltage (V).
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).

Figure 2.2: Each thin curve is \(i_C = \beta i_B\) for one base current (ideal, flat in the active region). The teal load line runs from 5.45 mA to 12 V. Raising \(I_B\) moves the Q-point up the line towards saturation; lowering it moves it down towards cutoff.

Intuition

The load line does not depend on the transistor at all. Changing \(\beta\) or \(I_B\) only slides the Q-point along the same line. Changing \(R_C\) or \(V_{CC}\) moves the line itself.

Modes of operation

The two junctions can each be on or off, which gives four modes (Figure 2.3). Analogue amplifiers are biased in forward-active mode. Switching circuits move between cutoff and saturation.

Figure 2.3: For an npn device, forward-active needs \(v_{BE} \gt 0\) and \(v_{BC} \lt 0\). Inverse-active swaps the roles of collector and emitter and is rarely used. For a pnp device every voltage is reversed.

03Saturation and the assume-then-check method

Reading Neamen 5.2.2; Lecture 14, slides 13 to 18

You usually cannot tell from the circuit which mode the transistor is in. The lecture's method is to guess, solve and test.

Key idea

1. Assume forward-active: \(V_{BE} = V_{BE(on)}\), \(I_B \gt 0\), \(I_C = \beta I_B\).
2. Solve the now linear circuit.
3. Test the assumption: it fails if \(I_B \lt 0\) (the transistor is in cutoff) or if \(V_{CE} \lt V_{CE(sat)}\) (it is in saturation).
4. If it fails, assume the mode the test points to and solve again.

In saturation both junctions are forward biased. The collector-emitter voltage is fixed at \(V_{CE(sat)}\) (0.2 V in this course) and \(I_C = \beta I_B\) no longer holds: the external circuit sets \(I_C\), and it is smaller than \(\beta I_B\). The ratio actually present is the forced beta.

Saturation\[ V_{CE} = V_{CE(sat)} \qquad I_{C(sat)} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CE(sat)}}{R_C} \qquad \beta_{forced} = \frac{I_C}{I_B} \lt \beta \]
\(V_{CE}\)
Collector-emitter voltage (V).
\(V_{CE(sat)}\)
Saturation voltage, 0.2 V unless stated.
\(I_{C(sat)}\)
Collector current in saturation, set by the collector loop (A).
\(V_{CC}\)
Collector supply (V).
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(\beta_{forced}\)
Ratio \(I_C/I_B\) actually present in saturation (no unit).
\(I_C\)
Collector current (A).
\(I_B\)
Base current (A).
\(\beta\)
Current gain in forward-active mode.

In saturation, \(I_E = I_B + I_C\) still holds, because it is Kirchhoff's current law, not a transistor property.

Worked example 3.1 a transistor driven into saturation

A CE circuit like Figure 1.1 has \(V_{BB} = 5\ \text{V}\), \(R_B = 100\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 3\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_{CC} = 9\ \text{V}\), \(\beta = 80\), \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\) and \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\). Find the currents, \(\beta_{forced}\) and \(P_T\).

1
\(I_B = (5 - 0.7)/100\ \text{k}\Omega = 43.0\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
Assume forward-active: \(I_C = 80 \times 43.0\ \mu\text{A} = 3.44\ \text{mA}\), so \(V_{CE} = 9 - (3.44)(3) = -1.32\ \text{V}\). This is impossible, so the transistor is saturated.
3
Saturation: \(I_{C(sat)} = (9 - 0.2)/3\ \text{k}\Omega = 2.933\ \text{mA}\). The base current is unchanged at 43.0 µA because the base loop has not changed.
4
\(\beta_{forced} = 2.933/0.043 = 68.2\), which is less than 80, as it must be.
5
\(I_E = 2.933 + 0.043 = 2.976\ \text{mA}\); \(P_T = (43.0\ \mu\text{A})(0.7) + (2.933\ \text{mA})(0.2) = 0.617\ \text{mW}\).
\(I_B = 43.0\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 2.93\ \text{mA}\), \(I_E = 2.98\ \text{mA}\), \(\beta_{forced} = 68.2\), \(P_T = 0.617\ \text{mW}\). A saturated switch dissipates far less than the same transistor in the active region.
Worked example 3.2 the edge of saturation

For the circuit of worked example 3.1, what value of \(V_{BB}\) puts the transistor exactly at the edge of saturation?

1
At the edge both descriptions hold: \(V_{CE} = 0.2\ \text{V}\) and \(I_C = \beta I_B\). So \(I_C = 2.933\ \text{mA}\) and \(I_B = 2.933/80 = 36.7\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
Base loop: \[ V_{BB} = I_B R_B + V_{BE(on)} = (36.7\ \mu\text{A})(100\ \text{k}\Omega) + 0.7 = 4.37\ \text{V} \]
\(V_{BB} = 4.37\ \text{V}\). Above this the transistor saturates; below it (and above 0.7 V) it is forward-active.
Two ways to mark the edge

Lecture 15 (TYU 5.8) uses a different boundary: the point where \(V_{BC} = 0\), so \(V_{CE} = V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). For the circuit above that gives \(I_C = (9 - 0.7)/3\ \text{k}\Omega = 2.77\ \text{mA}\), \(I_B = 34.6\ \mu\text{A}\) and \(V_{BB} = 4.16\ \text{V}\). Read the question: "\(V_{BC} = 0\)" and "edge of saturation with \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\)" give different answers.

Exam

Always show the test of your assumption. A full-marks answer states the assumed mode, shows the contradiction or the confirmation (for example "\(V_{CE} = -1.32\ \text{V} \lt V_{CE(sat)}\), so saturated"), and only then gives the final values.

04Voltage transfer characteristics

Reading Neamen 5.2.3; Lecture 15

A voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) plots the output voltage against the input voltage over a whole range. It is built one mode at a time: find where the transistor turns on, write \(V_O\) as a function of \(V_I\) in the active region, and find where that expression reaches the saturation limit.

Worked example 4.1 VTC of an npn inverter

An npn transistor has its base driven from \(V_I\) through \(R_B = 120\ \text{k}\Omega\) and its collector connected to \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\) through \(R_C = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\); the emitter is grounded. \(\beta = 100\), \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\), \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\). Develop the VTC for \(0 \le V_I \le 5\ \text{V}\).

1
Cutoff: for \(V_I \le 0.7\ \text{V}\), \(I_B = I_C = 0\) and \(V_O = 5\ \text{V}\).
2
Active: \(I_B = (V_I - 0.7)/R_B\), so \[ V_O = 5 - \frac{\beta R_C}{R_B}(V_I - 0.7) = 5 - 3.33(V_I - 0.7) \] The slope (the voltage gain in this region) is \(-3.33\).
3
Saturation starts when \(V_O = 0.2\ \text{V}\): \(V_I = 0.7 + 4.8/3.33 = 2.14\ \text{V}\). Above that, \(V_O = 0.2\ \text{V}\).
\(V_O = 5\ \text{V}\) for \(V_I \le 0.7\ \text{V}\); falls linearly with slope \(-3.33\) to 0.2 V at \(V_I = 2.14\ \text{V}\); stays at 0.2 V beyond (Figure 4.1).

Figure 4.1: The three straight segments are cutoff, forward-active and saturation. The dots mark the two breakpoints, at 0.7 V and 2.14 V. A logic inverter uses the two flat ends; an amplifier is biased in the middle of the sloped part.

For a pnp transistor with its emitter at \(V^+\), the order is reversed: a high input turns it off and a low input saturates it.

Worked example 4.2 VTC of a pnp stage

A pnp transistor has its emitter at \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\), its base driven from \(V_I\) through \(R_B = 200\ \text{k}\Omega\), and its collector connected to ground through \(R_C = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(V_O\) is the collector voltage. \(\beta = 60\), \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\), \(V_{EC(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\).

1
The transistor turns on when the base is 0.7 V below the emitter: \(V_I \lt 4.3\ \text{V}\). Above that, \(V_O = 0\).
2
Active: \(I_B = (4.3 - V_I)/R_B\) and \(V_O = I_C R_C = \dfrac{\beta R_C}{R_B}(4.3 - V_I) = 3(4.3 - V_I)\).
3
Saturation when \(V_O = V^+ - V_{EC(sat)} = 4.8\ \text{V}\): \(4.3 - V_I = 1.6\), so \(V_I = 2.7\ \text{V}\).
\(V_O = 4.8\ \text{V}\) for \(V_I \le 2.7\ \text{V}\); falls with slope \(-3\) to 0 V at \(V_I = 4.3\ \text{V}\); 0 V beyond (Figure 4.2).

Figure 4.2: The curve is the mirror image of Figure 4.1: saturated for low inputs, cut off above \(V^+ - V_{EB(on)} = 4.3\ \text{V}\).

Watch out

On the slides, Example 5.6 states \(V_A = \infty\) (the Early voltage), which is why the active-region segments are straight. A real transistor, as in the lecture's NgSpice run, gives slightly curved segments and breakpoints that are not exactly at the hand-calculated values.

Emitter resistor and its load line

Adding an emitter resistor \(R_E\) puts \(I_E\) into the base loop, so \(I_B\) and \(I_E\) are no longer separate. Substitute \(I_E = (1+\beta)I_B\) and solve the base loop for \(I_B\).

Base loop with an emitter resistor\[ I_B = \frac{V_{BB} - V_{BE(on)} - V^-}{R_B + (1+\beta) R_E} \]

\(V_{BB}\)

Intuition

Seen from the base, \(R_E\) looks \((1+\beta)\) times larger, because every microampere of base current brings \((1+\beta)\) microamperes through \(R_E\). This term appears again and again in amplifier work.

The output loop now contains both \(R_C\) and \(R_E\). Writing \(I_E = \frac{1+\beta}{\beta} I_C\) gives a load line with an effective resistance slightly larger than \(R_C + R_E\).

Worked example 5.1 emitter resistor with split supplies

Analyse Figure 5.1 with \(\beta = 100\) and \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Then draw the load line.

1
Base loop from ground through \(R_B\), the junction and \(R_E\) to \(-5\ \text{V}\): \[ I_B = \dfrac{0 - 0.7 - (-5)}{100\ \text{k} + (101)(2\ \text{k})} = \dfrac{4.3}{302\ \text{k}\Omega} = 14.24\ \mu\text{A} \]
2
\(I_C = 100 I_B = 1.424\ \text{mA}\); \(I_E = 101 I_B = 1.438\ \text{mA}\).
3
\(V_{CE} = 5 - (1.424)(4) - (1.438)(2) - (-5) = 1.43\ \text{V}\). This exceeds 0.2 V, so forward-active is confirmed.
4
Load line: \(R_C + \frac{101}{100}R_E = 6.02\ \text{k}\Omega\), so \(V_{CE} = 10 - 6.02 I_C\) (mA, V). Intercepts: \(V_{CE} = 10\ \text{V}\) and \(I_C = 10/6.02 = 1.66\ \text{mA}\).
\(I_B = 14.2\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 1.42\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CE} = 1.43\ \text{V}\). The Q-point sits close to saturation (Figure 5.2), which leaves little room for the output to swing down.

Figure 5.2: The line runs from 10 V (the total supply) to 1.66 mA. Substituting the Q-point into \(V_{CE} = 10 - 6.02 I_C\) gives the same 1.43 V as the KVL loop, which is a quick check that the two methods agree.

Designing a common-base bias

Design reverses the analysis: the Q-point is given and the resistors are unknown. Write the base-emitter loop to find the emitter resistor, then the output loop to find the collector resistor.

Figure 5.3: The base is held near ground through \(R_B\). \(R_E\) and \(R_C\) are to be chosen. In a common-base amplifier the signal would enter at the emitter and leave at the collector.

Worked example 5.2 design for a given Q-point

In Figure 5.3, choose \(R_E\) and \(R_C\) so that \(I_{EQ} = 1\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{ECQ} = 3\ \text{V}\). \(\beta = 100\), \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
\(I_{BQ} = I_{EQ}/(1+\beta) = 1\ \text{mA}/101 = 9.90\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
E-B loop: \(V^+ = I_{EQ}R_E + V_{EB(on)} + I_{BQ}R_B\), so \[ R_E = \dfrac{5 - 0.7 - (9.90\ \mu\text{A})(20\ \text{k}\Omega)}{1\ \text{mA}} = 4.10\ \text{k}\Omega \]
3
\(I_{CQ} = \frac{\beta}{1+\beta}I_{EQ} = 0.990\ \text{mA}\).
4
Output loop: \(V^+ - V^- = I_{EQ}R_E + V_{ECQ} + I_{CQ}R_C\), so \(R_C = \dfrac{10 - 4.10 - 3}{0.990\ \text{mA}} = 2.93\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_E = 4.10\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_C = 2.93\ \text{k}\Omega\).

Resistor tolerances

A designed value such as 133.6 kΩ cannot be bought. You pick the nearest standard value, and that resistor is itself only within a tolerance (±10 % in the lecture's example). A good design checks the Q-point at the corners of the tolerance range.

Worked example 5.3 pnp design with ±10 % resistors

A pnp transistor has its emitter connected to \(V^+ = 6\ \text{V}\) through \(R_E = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), its collector grounded, and its base returned through \(R_B\) to \(V_{BB} = -1\ \text{V}\). \(\beta = 80\), \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Design \(R_B\) for \(V_{ECQ} = 3\ \text{V}\), choose a standard value and find the Q-point spread with ±10 % resistors.

A load resistor and Thevenin's theorem

When a load \(R_L\) (for example the next stage) hangs off the collector, the collector current splits between \(R_C\) and \(R_L\). Two methods give the same answer: KCL at the collector node, or replacing \(V^+\), \(R_C\) and \(R_L\) with their Thevenin equivalent.

Thevenin equivalent at the collector\[ R_{TH} = R_C \parallel R_L \qquad V_{TH} = \frac{R_L}{R_C + R_L}V^+ \]
\(R_{TH}\)
Thevenin resistance seen by the collector (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_L\)
Load resistor from collector to ground (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{TH}\)
Open-circuit voltage at the collector with the transistor removed (V).
\(V^+\)
Positive supply (V).

Figure 5.5: \(V_O\) is the collector voltage. \(R_L\) draws some of the current that flows through \(R_C\), so \(I_C\) is the difference between the current in \(R_C\) and the current in \(R_L\).

Worked example 5.4 loaded collector

Analyse Figure 5.5 with \(\beta = 120\) and \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
Base loop (unchanged by \(R_L\)): \[ I_B = \dfrac{0 - 0.7 + 5}{20\ \text{k} + (121)(4\ \text{k})} = \dfrac{4.3}{504\ \text{k}\Omega} = 8.53\ \mu\text{A} \]; \(I_C = 1.024\ \text{mA}\); \(I_E = 1.032\ \text{mA}\).
2
KCL at the collector: \(I_C = \dfrac{10 - V_O}{4\ \text{k}} - \dfrac{V_O}{4\ \text{k}}\), so \(V_O = \dfrac{10 - (1.024)(4)}{2} = 2.95\ \text{V}\).
3
Thevenin check: \(R_{TH} = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_{TH} = 5\ \text{V}\), so \(V_O = 5 - (1.024)(2) = 2.95\ \text{V}\). The two methods agree.
4
\(V_E = -5 + (1.032)(4) = -0.87\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{CE} = 2.95 - (-0.87) = 3.82\ \text{V}\): forward-active.
\(I_C = 1.02\ \text{mA}\), \(V_O = 2.95\ \text{V}\), \(V_{CE} = 3.82\ \text{V}\). The load line is \(V_{CE} = 10 - I_C(2 + \tfrac{121}{120}\cdot 4)\ \text{k}\Omega\), from 10 V to 1.66 mA.

07Practice problems

Problem 7.1 Thevenin input network

An npn transistor has \(R_C = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\) to \(V_{CC} = 10\ \text{V}\) and a grounded emitter. Its base connects to \(V_I\) through \(R_1 = 20\ \text{k}\Omega\) and to \(-10\ \text{V}\) through \(R_2 = 80\ \text{k}\Omega\). \(\beta = 100\). (a) Find \(V_I\) for \(V_{CEQ} = 5\ \text{V}\). (b) Find the range of \(V_I\) that keeps \(2 \le V_{CEQ} \le 8\ \text{V}\).

1
(a) \(I_{CQ} = (10 - 5)/2\ \text{k} = 2.5\ \text{mA}\), \(I_{BQ} = 25\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
Thevenin of the input network: \(R_{TH} = 20 \parallel 80 = 16\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(V_{TH} = \frac{80}{100}(V_I + 10) - 10 = 0.8V_I - 2\).
3
Base loop: \(V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + 0.7 = (25\ \mu\text{A})(16\ \text{k}) + 0.7 = 1.10\ \text{V}\), so \(V_I = (1.10 + 2)/0.8 = 3.875\ \text{V}\).
4
(b) \(V_{CEQ} = 2\ \text{V}\): \(I_C = 4\ \text{mA}\), \(I_B = 40\ \mu\text{A}\), \(V_{TH} = 1.34\ \text{V}\), \(V_I = 4.175\ \text{V}\). \(V_{CEQ} = 8\ \text{V}\): \(I_C = 1\ \text{mA}\), \(I_B = 10\ \mu\text{A}\), \(V_{TH} = 0.86\ \text{V}\), \(V_I = 3.575\ \text{V}\).
(a) \(V_I = 3.88\ \text{V}\). (b) \(3.58\ \text{V} \le V_I \le 4.18\ \text{V}\); a larger \(V_I\) gives a smaller \(V_{CEQ}\).
Problem 7.2 pnp with split supplies

A pnp transistor has its emitter at \(V^+ = 6\ \text{V}\), its base to ground through \(R_B = 200\ \text{k}\Omega\), and its collector to \(V^- = -6\ \text{V}\) through \(R_C = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\). \(\beta = 100\). Find \(I_{CQ}\) and \(V_{ECQ}\), confirm the mode, and give the load line intercepts.

1
E-B loop: \(I_{BQ} = (6 - 0.7)/200\ \text{k} = 26.5\ \mu\text{A}\); \(I_{CQ} = 2.65\ \text{mA}\).
2
\(V_C = -6 + (2.65)(2) = -0.70\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{ECQ} = 6 - (-0.70) = 6.70\ \text{V}\).
3
\(V_B = 5.3\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{CB} = -0.70 - 5.3 = -6.0\ \text{V}\): the C-B junction is reverse biased and \(V_{EB} \gt 0\), so forward-active.
4
Load line \(V_{EC} = 12 - 2I_C\) (V, mA): intercepts 12 V and 6 mA.
\(I_{CQ} = 2.65\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{ECQ} = 6.70\ \text{V}\), forward-active; load line from 6 mA to 12 V.
Problem 7.3 finding beta from a measurement

A pnp transistor has its emitter connected to \(+6\ \text{V}\) through \(R_E = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\), its collector connected to \(-6\ \text{V}\) through \(R_C = 6\ \text{k}\Omega\), and \(R_B = 500\ \text{k}\Omega\) connected between base and collector. The emitter is measured at \(V_E = 5.0\ \text{V}\). Find \(I_{EQ}\), \(V_{ECQ}\), \(I_{BQ}\), \(I_{CQ}\) and \(\beta\).

1
\(I_{EQ} = (6 - 5.0)/2\ \text{k} = 0.50\ \text{mA}\). Both \(I_B\) and \(I_C\) end up in \(R_C\), so \(I_{R_C} = I_{EQ}\) and \(V_C = -6 + (0.5)(6) = -3.0\ \text{V}\).
2
\(V_{ECQ} = 5.0 - (-3.0) = 8.0\ \text{V}\).
3
Loop from emitter through the junction and \(R_B\) to the collector: \(I_{BQ} = (5.0 - 0.7 - (-3.0))/500\ \text{k} = 14.6\ \mu\text{A}\).
4
\(I_{CQ} = 0.500 - 0.0146 = 0.485\ \text{mA}\); \(\beta = 0.485/0.0146 = 33.2\).
\(I_{EQ} = 0.50\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{ECQ} = 8.0\ \text{V}\), \(I_{BQ} = 14.6\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_{CQ} = 0.485\ \text{mA}\), \(\beta = 33.2\). The load line is \(I_C = 1.46 - 0.121V_{EC}\) (mA, V).
Problem 7.4 pnp VTC with an emitter resistor

A pnp transistor has its emitter connected to \(V_{CC} = 5\ \text{V}\) through \(R_E = 1\ \text{k}\Omega\), its base driven from \(V_I\) through \(R_B = 200\ \text{k}\Omega\), and its collector connected to ground through \(R_C = 3\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(v_O\) is the collector voltage. \(\beta = 80\), \(V_{EC(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\). Find the breakpoints of the VTC for \(0 \le V_I \le 5\ \text{V}\).

1
At \(V_I = 0\), assume forward-active: \(I_B = 4.3/(200\ \text{k} + 81 \times 1\ \text{k}) = 15.30\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 1.224\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{EC} = 5 - (1.239)(1) - (1.224)(3) = 0.088\ \text{V} \lt 0.2\ \text{V}\). So it is saturated.
2
Saturated at \(V_I = 0\): solve \(4.3 = 201\ \text{k}\, I_B + 1\ \text{k}\, I_C\) and \(4.8 = 1\ \text{k}\, I_B + 4\ \text{k}\, I_C\) to get \(I_B = 15.44\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_C = 1.196\ \text{mA}\), \(v_O = 3.588\ \text{V}\).
3
Edge of saturation (\(I_C = \beta I_B\) and \(V_{EC} = 0.2\)): \(I_B = 4.8/(81 \times 1\ \text{k} + 80 \times 3\ \text{k}) = 14.95\ \mu\text{A}\), and \(V_I = 4.3 - I_B(81\ \text{k} + 200\ \text{k}) = 0.098\ \text{V}\), with \(v_O = 3.589\ \text{V}\).
4
The transistor turns off at \(V_I = 5 - 0.7 = 4.3\ \text{V}\); above that \(v_O = 0\). In between, \(v_O = \beta R_C(4.3 - V_I)/(R_B + 81\ \text{k}) = 0.854(4.3 - V_I)\).
Saturated for \(0 \le V_I \le 0.098\ \text{V}\) (\(v_O \approx 3.59\ \text{V}\)); forward-active, falling with slope \(-0.854\), up to \(V_I = 4.3\ \text{V}\); off (\(v_O = 0\)) above 4.3 V. The emitter resistor makes \(I_{C(sat)}\) change slightly with \(V_I\), so the saturated level is not perfectly flat.
Problem 7.5 logic-level switch

A 3.3 V logic output drives an npn transistor through \(R_B = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\). The collector has \(R_C = 1\ \text{k}\Omega\) to 5 V. \(\beta = 100\). Is the transistor saturated? Find \(I_C\), \(\beta_{forced}\) and \(P_T\).

1
\(I_B = (3.3 - 0.7)/10\ \text{k} = 260\ \mu\text{A}\). Forward-active would need \(I_C = 26\ \text{mA}\) and give \(V_{CE} = 5 - 26 = -21\ \text{V}\): impossible, so saturated.
2
\(I_C = (5 - 0.2)/1\ \text{k} = 4.8\ \text{mA}\); \(\beta_{forced} = 4.8/0.26 = 18.5\).
3
\(P_T = (0.26)(0.7) + (4.8)(0.2) = 1.14\ \text{mW}\).
Saturated, \(I_C = 4.80\ \text{mA}\), \(\beta_{forced} = 18.5\), \(P_T = 1.14\ \text{mW}\). A \(\beta_{forced}\) well below \(\beta\) means the switch stays saturated even for a low-gain transistor.

08Formula sheet

Forward-active

\(V_{BE} = V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\) (npn), \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\) (pnp)

\(I_C = \beta I_B\), \(I_E = (1+\beta)I_B\), \(I_C = \frac{\beta}{1+\beta}I_E\)

Valid if \(I_B \gt 0\) and \(V_{CE} \gt V_{CE(sat)}\)

Saturation

\(V_{CE} = V_{CE(sat)} = 0.2\ \text{V}\)

\(I_C\) set by the collector loop; \(\beta_{forced} = I_C/I_B \lt \beta\)

\(I_E = I_B + I_C\) always

Bias loops

\(I_B = \dfrac{V_{BB} - V_{BE(on)} - V^-}{R_B + (1+\beta)R_E}\)

\(V_{CE} = (V^+ - V^-) - I_C\left(R_C + \frac{1+\beta}{\beta}R_E\right)\)

Load lines and power

Input: \(i_B = (V_{BB} - v_{BE})/R_B\)

Output: \(i_C = (V_{CC} - v_{CE})/R_C\)

\(P_T = I_B V_{BE} + I_C V_{CE}\)

Transfer curve (npn, grounded emitter)

Off for \(V_I \le 0.7\ \text{V}\)

\(V_O = V^+ - \frac{\beta R_C}{R_B}(V_I - 0.7)\)

Saturates when \(V_O = 0.2\ \text{V}\)

Thevenin at the collector

\(R_{TH} = R_C \parallel R_L\)

\(V_{TH} = \frac{R_L}{R_C + R_L}V^+\)
