BJT Biasing and Multistage Circuits

Biasing a BJT so the Q-point survives a change of β: single-resistor and voltage-divider bias, bias stability, two supplies, the current mirror, direct-coupled and cascode multistage circuits, and a BJT LED switch. Built from Lectures 18 to 20, Tutorial 9 and Practical Test 7.

01Why bias a transistor

A transistor amplifier only works while its DC operating point sits in the forward-active region and stays there. Biasing means choosing resistors and supplies so that the quiescent values \(I_{CQ}\), \(I_{BQ}\) and \(V_{CEQ}\) land where you want them, whichever individual transistor you happen to fit.

DefinitionQ-point (quiescent point): the DC currents and voltages in the transistor with no signal applied. On the output characteristic it is the point \((V_{CEQ},\, I_{CQ})\) where the DC load line meets the transistor curve for the chosen base current.

Every calculation this week uses the same ground rules for an npn transistor in the forward-active region. The base-emitter junction drops \(V_{BE(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\) (a pnp uses \(V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\)). The collector current is \(\beta\) times the base current, and the emitter current is the sum of the two. A coupling capacitor is an open circuit for DC, so the signal source and the capacitor drop out of every bias calculation.

Forward-active current relations\[ I_{CQ} = \beta\, I_{BQ} \qquad I_{EQ} = (1+\beta)\, I_{BQ} \]
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current, in A.
\(\beta\)
Current gain of the transistor. It is a ratio, so it has no unit.
\(I_{BQ}\)
Quiescent base current, in A.
\(I_{EQ}\)
Quiescent emitter current. It is slightly larger than \(I_{CQ}\) because it also carries the base current.

The collector circuit gives the DC load line. Without an emitter resistor the only drop between the supply and the transistor is across \(R_C\). With an emitter resistor, \(I_{EQ} \approx I_{CQ}\) when \(\beta\) is large, so both resistors act as one in the load line.

DC load line\[ V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ}R_C - I_{EQ}R_E \;\approx\; V_{CC} - I_{CQ}(R_C + R_E) \]
\(V_{CEQ}\)
Quiescent collector-emitter voltage, in V.
\(V_{CC}\)
Positive supply voltage, in V.
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current, in A.
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(I_{EQ}\)
Quiescent emitter current, in A.
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor, in \(\Omega\). It is zero for the single-resistor circuit.
Key ideaThe load line is fixed by the circuit, but the Q-point also depends on \(\beta\), and the same part number can have a \(\beta\) anywhere from 50 to 150 (the range the lecture uses). A good bias circuit is one whose Q-point hardly moves when \(\beta\) changes. The next three sections compare how well each circuit does that.

02Single-resistor biasing

The simplest bias circuit connects one resistor \(R_B\) from the supply to the base. The resistor sets the base current, and the transistor multiplies it by \(\beta\) to get the collector current. That is the weakness: whatever \(\beta\) is, \(I_{CQ}\) follows it.

Figure 2.1: The capacitor \(C_C\) blocks the DC level of the source \(v_s\), so the base only sees the current from \(R_B\).

Analysis\[ I_{BQ} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_B} \qquad I_{CQ} = \beta I_{BQ} \qquad V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ}R_C \]
\(I_{BQ}\)
Quiescent base current, in A.
\(V_{CC}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
Base-emitter turn-on voltage, 0.7 V.
\(R_B\)
Base resistor, in \(\Omega\). Typically in the megohm range.
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current, in A.
\(\beta\)
Current gain.
\(V_{CEQ}\)
Quiescent collector-emitter voltage, in V.
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor, in \(\Omega\).

To design the circuit you are given \(I_{CQ}\), \(V_{CEQ}\) and a nominal \(\beta\). Work from the collector side first, because the load line gives \(R_C\) directly, then find the base current and the base resistor.

Design\[ R_C = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CEQ}}{I_{CQ}} \qquad I_{BQ} = \frac{I_{CQ}}{\beta} \qquad R_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE(on)}}{I_{BQ}} \]
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor from the KVL loop through the collector, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{CC}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{CEQ}\)
Wanted collector-emitter voltage, in V.
\(I_{CQ}\)
Wanted collector current, in A.
\(I_{BQ}\)
Base current needed for that collector current, in A.
\(\beta\)
Nominal current gain.
\(R_B\)
Base resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.

Worked example 2.1 Single-resistor design and the effect of \(\beta\)

Design a single-resistor bias circuit for \(V_{CC} = 9\ \text{V}\), \(I_{CQ} = 0.5\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CEQ} = 4\ \text{V}\) with a nominal \(\beta = 120\). Then find the Q-point for \(\beta = 80\) and \(\beta = 160\).

2

3

4

5

\(R_C = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_B = 1.99\ \text{M}\Omega\). A \(\beta\) range of 80 to 160 moves \(I_{CQ}\) between 0.33 mA and 0.67 mA, a swing of \(\pm 33\ \%\) about the nominal 0.5 mA. The transistor stays active, but there is little room for a larger \(\beta\).
Watch outThe base resistor sets \(I_{BQ}\), not \(I_{CQ}\). Do not compute \(R_B\) from the collector current directly, and do not forget the \(0.7\ \text{V}\) drop when you do compute it from \(I_{BQ}\).
ExamDesign example 5.14 changes \(\beta\) from 50 to 150 and follows the Q-point. Be ready to do the same: recompute with the new \(\beta\), then check \(V_{CEQ}\) against \(V_{CE(sat)}\). If the formula gives \(V_{CEQ} \le 0\), the transistor is saturated and \(I_{CQ} = \beta I_{BQ}\) no longer holds.

03Voltage-divider biasing

The voltage-divider circuit fixes the base voltage with two resistors, so the base current no longer sets \(I_{CQ}\) on its own. The emitter resistor then does the stabilising: if the transistor tries to pass more current, the drop across \(R_E\) rises and pulls the base-emitter voltage down.

Figure 3.1: The Thevenin step replaces \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) by one source \(V_{TH}\) behind one resistor \(R_{TH}\). It leaves the transistor and its \(R_C\), \(R_E\) unchanged.

Thevenin equivalent of the divider\[ V_{TH} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\,V_{CC} \qquad R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Open-circuit voltage the divider would put on the base, in V.
\(R_2\)
Lower divider resistor, from base to ground, in \(\Omega\).
\(R_1\)
Upper divider resistor, from supply to base, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{CC}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(R_{TH}\)
Resistance the base sees looking back into the divider, in \(\Omega\).

Write one KVL loop from the Thevenin source, through \(R_{TH}\), the base-emitter junction and \(R_E\). Replace \(I_{EQ}\) by \((1+\beta)I_{BQ}\) and solve for the base current.

Divider bias, analysis\[ V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; I_{BQ} = \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E} \qquad I_{CQ} = \frac{\beta\,(V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)})}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E} \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Thevenin voltage of the divider, in V.
\(I_{BQ}\)
Quiescent base current, in A.
\(R_{TH}\)
Thevenin resistance of the divider, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(I_{EQ}\)
Quiescent emitter current, equal to \((1+\beta)I_{BQ}\).
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(\beta\)
Current gain.
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current, equal to \(\beta I_{BQ}\).
IntuitionThe term \((1+\beta)R_E\) is the emitter resistor as the base sees it: any base current is multiplied by \((1+\beta)\) on its way through \(R_E\). A large \((1+\beta)R_E\) makes the base current depend more on \(R_E\) and less on the details of \(\beta\), which is exactly what a stable circuit needs.

The Thevenin values are \(R_{TH} \approx 10.0\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(V_{TH} \approx 1.79\ \text{V}\). The table follows from the formulas above.

Worked example 3.1 Analysing a voltage-divider circuit

A circuit has \(V_{CC} = 9\ \text{V}\), \(R_1 = 47\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 1.8\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_E = 0.5\ \text{k}\Omega\). Find \(I_{CQ}\) and \(V_{CEQ}\) for \(\beta = 120\), then see how they change for \(\beta = 60\) and \(\beta = 180\).

1
Thevenin voltage: \(V_{TH} = 10/(47 + 10) \times 9 = 1.579\ \text{V}\).
2
Thevenin resistance: \(R_{TH} = 47\ \text{k}\Omega \parallel 10\ \text{k}\Omega = 8.25\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
Base current: \(I_{BQ} = (1.579 - 0.7)/(8.25\ \text{k}\Omega + 121 \times 0.5\ \text{k}\Omega) = 12.79\ \mu\text{A}\).
4
Collector and emitter currents: \(I_{CQ} = 120 \times 12.79\ \mu\text{A} = 1.534\ \text{mA}\), \(I_{EQ} = 1.547\ \text{mA}\).
5
Output voltage: \(V_{CEQ} = 9 - (1.534\ \text{mA})(1.8\ \text{k}\Omega) - (1.547\ \text{mA})(0.5\ \text{k}\Omega) = 5.46\ \text{V}\). This is well above \(V_{CE(sat)}\), so the active-region assumption holds.
6
Repeat steps 3 to 5 for the other two gains: \(\beta = 60\) gives \(I_{CQ} = 1.361\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ} = 5.86\ \text{V}\); \(\beta = 180\) gives \(I_{CQ} = 1.602\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ} = 5.31\ \text{V}\).
\(I_{CQ} = 1.53\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CEQ} = 5.46\ \text{V}\) at \(\beta = 120\). A threefold change in \(\beta\) (60 to 180) moves \(I_{CQ}\) by only 18 %, where a single-resistor circuit would move it by 200 %.
Watch outTest the answer before you trust it. The formulas assume the active region, so finish every analysis by checking that \(V_{CEQ}\) is above the saturation value. The assumption is only confirmed once the check passes.

04Bias stability

In the formula for \(I_{CQ}\), the base current is delivered through \(R_{TH}\) and \((1+\beta)R_E\) in series. If \(R_{TH}\) is much smaller than \((1+\beta)R_E\), then \(\beta\) cancels almost completely and the current is fixed by \(V_{TH}\), \(V_{BE(on)}\) and \(R_E\) alone.

Bias-stability condition\[ R_{TH} \ll (1+\beta)R_E \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; I_{CQ} \approx \frac{\beta\,(V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)})}{(1+\beta)R_E} \approx \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E} \]
\(R_{TH}\)
Thevenin resistance of the divider, in \(\Omega\).
\((1+\beta)R_E\)
Emitter resistor as seen from the base, in \(\Omega\).
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current, in A.
\(\beta\)
Current gain. For \(\beta \gg 1\), \(\beta/(1+\beta) \approx 1\).
\(V_{TH}\)
Thevenin voltage, in V.
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor, in \(\Omega\).
General ruleAim for \(R_{TH} \approx 0.1\,(1+\beta)R_E\). The factor 0.1 is a compromise: small enough to make \(I_{CQ}\) nearly independent of \(\beta\), large enough to keep \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) from loading the supply too much.

Figure 4.1: Computed from \(i_C = \beta(V_{TH}-V_{BE(on)})/(R_{TH}+(1+\beta)R_E)\) with a nominal \(\beta = 100\), so the horizontal axis is \(R_{TH}/(101R_E)\). The ratio is independent of \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_E\). At the 10 % rule the current changes by about 14 % across a threefold change in \(\beta\).

Design recipe

A design problem gives the supply, the resistors \(R_C\) and \(R_E\) (or one of them), the transistor and a target for the Q-point. The steps below solve it in order, and each step uses only values already found.

  1. Get \(I_{CQ}\) from the load line, using \(I_{EQ} \approx I_{CQ}\) for a large \(\beta\). For a Q-point at the centre, \(V_{CEQ} = V_{CC}/2\).
  2. Find the base and emitter currents: \(I_{BQ} = I_{CQ}/\beta\) and \(I_{EQ} = (1+\beta)I_{BQ}\).
  3. Set \(R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E\).
  4. Find \(V_{TH}\) from the base loop: \(V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E\).
  5. Split \(R_{TH}\) and \(V_{TH}\) into \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) with the box below.
Divider resistors from the Thevenin values\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{TH}}{V_{TH}}\,V_{CC} \qquad R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_{TH}}{R_1 - R_{TH}} \]
\(R_1\)
Upper divider resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(R_{TH}\)
Wanted Thevenin resistance, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{TH}\)
Wanted Thevenin voltage, in V.
\(V_{CC}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(R_2\)
Lower divider resistor, in \(\Omega\). It follows from \(R_{TH} = R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)\).

The first formula comes from noticing that \(V_{TH}/V_{CC} = R_2/(R_1+R_2) = R_{TH}/R_1\). The divider ratio and \(R_{TH}\) together give \(R_1\) in one step.

05Positive and negative supplies

Many circuits run from a positive and a negative supply. The base divider then sits between \(V^+\) and \(V^-\) instead of between \(V_{CC}\) and ground, and the emitter resistor returns to \(V^-\). The method does not change. Only the reference level of the Thevenin source does.

Figure 5.1: The output is taken at the collector, so with no signal \(V_o = V^+ - I_{CQ}R_C\).

To find \(V_{TH}\), disconnect the transistor. The divider carries \(I = (V^+ - V^-)/(R_1+R_2)\), and \(R_2\) drops \(I R_2\) above \(V^-\). The base loop then ends at \(V^-\) instead of at ground.

Thevenin source and base loop with two supplies\[ V_{TH} = V^- + \frac{R_2\,(V^+ - V^-)}{R_1 + R_2} \qquad V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E + V^- \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Thevenin voltage at the base, in V. It can be negative.
\(V^-\)
Negative supply, in V (a negative number).
\(R_2\)
Divider resistor from base to the negative supply, in \(\Omega\).
\(V^+\)
Positive supply, in V.
\(R_1\)
Divider resistor from the positive supply to the base, in \(\Omega\).
\(I_{BQ}\)
Quiescent base current, in A.
\(R_{TH}\)
\(R_1 \parallel R_2\), in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(I_{EQ}\)
Quiescent emitter current, equal to \((1+\beta)I_{BQ}\).
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor, in \(\Omega\).
Collector-emitter voltage with two supplies\[ V_{CEQ} = (V^+ - V^-) - I_{CQ}R_C - I_{EQ}R_E \]
\(V_{CEQ}\)
Quiescent collector-emitter voltage, in V.
\(V^+ - V^-\)
Total span of the supplies, in V.
\(I_{CQ}R_C\)
Drop across the collector resistor, in V.
\(I_{EQ}R_E\)
Drop across the emitter resistor, in V.

The lecture's example P5.17 asks for a quiescent output of zero volts with \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\), \(V^- = -5\ \text{V}\), \(R_C = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_E = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(\beta = 150\). Zero output means the collector sits at ground, so \(I_{CQ} = V^+/R_C = 0.50\ \text{mA}\), which gives \(V_{CEQ} = 3.99\ \text{V}\). The bias-stable design (\(R_{TH} = 30.2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_{TH} = −3.19\ \text{V}\)) then yields \(R_1 = 167\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_2 = 36.9\ \text{k}\Omega\).

Worked example 5.1 Two-supply design with a zero quiescent output

Design a bias-stable circuit for \(V^+ = 6\ \text{V}\), \(V^- = −6\ \text{V}\), \(R_C = 6\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_E = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(\beta = 100\) so that the quiescent output voltage is zero. Find \(I_{CQ}\), \(V_{CEQ}\), \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

1

2
Other currents: \(I_{BQ} = 1.00\ \text{mA}/100 = 10.0\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_{EQ} = 1.010\ \text{mA}\).
3
Voltage across the transistor: \(V_{CEQ} = 12 - (1.00)(6) - (1.010)(1.5) = 4.48\ \text{V}\), comfortably active.
4
Stability rule: \(R_{TH} = 0.1(101)(1.5\ \text{k}\Omega) = 15.15\ \text{k}\Omega\).
5

6

06Current-mirror biasing

One way to bias a transistor is with a constant current source \(I_Q\). Integrated circuits build that source from other transistors so that the number of resistors stays small. The standard source is the current mirror: a reference current is set in one transistor, and a matching transistor copies it.

Figure 6.1: Q1 has its collector tied to its base, so \(V_{CE1} = V_{BE1}\) and it is always active. \(R_B\) is the base resistor of \(Q_O\); the example sets it to zero.

The reference current \(I_1\) flows from ground through \(R_1\) and into Q1. A KVL loop through \(R_1\), the base-emitter junction of Q1 and the negative supply gives \(I_1\). If Q1 and Q2 are identical and at the same temperature, they share the same \(V_{BE}\), so they carry the same base and collector currents. Then \(I_1\) is the collector current plus both base currents.

\(V_{BE(on)}\)

07Multistage circuits

A multistage circuit connects the output of one transistor stage to the input of the next. In the lecture's circuits the stages are direct-coupled: the collector of the first transistor feeds the base of the second with no capacitor between them. The DC voltage of one node then fixes the bias of the next stage, so you cannot analyse the stages separately. You find one node voltage, and the rest follows.

An npn-pnp direct-coupled pair

Figure 7.1: The two transistors have opposite polarity so that the collector of Q1 sits at a level where the base of Q2 can follow it.

The method has four steps. Each one uses results from the previous one.

  1. Replace the divider \(R_1, R_2\) at the base of Q1 by its Thevenin equivalent (with \(V^-\) as the reference, as in section 5), then find \(I_{B1}\), \(I_{C1}\) and \(I_{E1}\) from the base loop.
  2. Write KCL at the collector node C1. The current through \(R_{C1}\) splits between \(I_{C1}\) and the base current of Q2: \(I_{R_{C1}} + I_{B2} = I_{C1}\). Express \(I_{B2}\) through the emitter of Q2, with \(V_{E2} = V_{C1} + V_{EB(on)}\), and solve for \(V_{C1}\).
  3. Find \(I_{E2}\), then \(I_{C2}\) and \(I_{B2}\).
  4. Find the remaining node voltages and the two \(V_{CE}\) values.
Collector-node voltage of Q1\[ V_{C1} = \frac{\dfrac{V^+}{R_{C1}} + \dfrac{V^+ - V_{EB(on)}}{(1+\beta)R_{E2}} - I_{C1}}{\dfrac{1}{R_{C1}} + \dfrac{1}{(1+\beta)R_{E2}}} \]
\(V_{C1}\)

\(V^+\)
Positive supply, in V.
\(R_{C1}\)
Collector resistor of Q1, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{EB(on)}\)
Emitter-base turn-on voltage of the pnp transistor Q2, 0.7 V.
\(\beta\)
Current gain of Q2.
\(R_{E2}\)
Emitter resistor of Q2, in \(\Omega\).
\(I_{C1}\)
Collector current of Q1 from step 1, in A.

This comes from \((V^+ - V_{C1})/R_{C1} + (V^+ - V_{C1} - V_{EB(on)})/((1+\beta)R_{E2}) = I_{C1}\), which is linear in \(V_{C1}\). You can solve it with the formula or by rearranging the equation in the exam; both are the same work.

Figure 7.2: \(C_{C1}\) feeds the signal into the base of Q1 and \(C_{C2}\) takes it out to \(R_L\). Neither capacitor takes part in the DC design.

What the marker's comments add

The test memo has written comments that go beyond the numbers.

  • The largest usable \(R_B\) sits at the change from forward-active to saturation. The window is small, and \(\beta\) and the resistor values both vary, so a design that sits at that edge cannot be relied on.
  • The smallest \(R_B\) is set by the base current available. A higher base current also means more power loss.
  • Choosing \(I_C/I_B \approx 0.5\,\beta\), a base current about twice the minimum, keeps the base current below the limit so the transistor does not heat up. It also allows for the tolerance on \(\beta\), and it leaves room to pick the best available \(R_B\).
  • Cutoff happens when \(I_B = 0\), which needs a very high \(R_B\). The memo adds that the model makes assumptions at that extreme.

Ground rules

\[ I_C = \beta I_B \quad I_E = (1+\beta)I_B \]

\[ V_{CEQ} \approx V_{CC} - I_{CQ}(R_C + R_E) \]

\(V_{BE(on)} = V_{EB(on)} = 0.7\ \text{V}\)

Single resistor

\[ I_{BQ} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_B} \quad I_{CQ} = \beta I_{BQ} \]

\[ R_C = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CEQ}}{I_{CQ}} \quad R_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE(on)}}{I_{CQ}/\beta} \]

Voltage divider

\[ V_{TH} = \frac{R_2 V_{CC}}{R_1 + R_2} \quad R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2 \]

\[ I_{BQ} = \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E} \]

\[ R_{TH} \approx 0.1(1+\beta)R_E \quad I_{CQ} \approx \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E} \]

\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{TH}V_{CC}}{V_{TH}} \quad R_2 = \frac{R_1R_{TH}}{R_1 - R_{TH}} \]

Two supplies

\[ V_{TH} = V^- + \frac{R_2(V^+ - V^-)}{R_1 + R_2} \]

\[ V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E + V^- \]

\[ V_{CEQ} = (V^+ - V^-) - I_{CQ}R_C - I_{EQ}R_E \]

Current mirror

\[ I_1 = \frac{-V^- - V_{BE(on)}}{R_1} \quad I_Q = \frac{I_1}{1 + 2/\beta} \]

\[ I_Q = \frac{1+\beta}{\beta}I_C(Q_O) \]

npn-pnp pair

\[ V_{C1} = \frac{V^+/R_{C1} + (V^+ - V_{EB(on)})/((1+\beta)R_{E2}) - I_{C1}}{1/R_{C1} + 1/((1+\beta)R_{E2})} \]

\[ I_{R_{C1}} + I_{B2} = I_{C1} \quad V_{E2} = V_{C1} + V_{EB(on)} \]

npn-npn pair

\[ I_{BQ2} = \frac{V^+ - I_{CQ1}R_{C1} - V_{BE(on)} - V^-}{R_{C1} + (\beta+1)R_{E2}} \]

Cascode
