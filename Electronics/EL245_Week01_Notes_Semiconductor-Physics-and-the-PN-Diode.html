ELECTRONICS 245 · CHAPTER 1 · SEMICONDUCTOR MATERIALS AND DIODES

Semiconductor physics and the pn diode

Where the carriers in silicon come from, how a pn junction turns them into a one-way device, and how to analyse a diode circuit exactly, with a model, and for small signals.

01Intrinsic semiconductors

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.1 (1)

A pure silicon (\(\text{Si}\)) or germanium (\(\text{Ge}\)) crystal is a tetrahedral lattice. Every atom has four valence electrons and shares each one with a neighbour in a covalent bond, so all four of its bonds are full.

Watch out

The summary calls the Si and Ge lattice "diamond/zincblende". Silicon and germanium are diamond lattices. Zincblende is the compound-semiconductor version, as in \(\text{GaAs}\). The bonding picture above is the same for both.

Energy bands

Electron energies in the crystal fall into bands. The valence band (top edge \(E_v\)) holds electrons bound to atoms. The conduction band (bottom edge \(E_c\)) holds electrons free to move through the lattice, and only these conduct. Between them lies a forbidden gap.

Definition

The bandgap is \(E_g = E_c - E_v\). Typical values are \(1.1\ \text{eV}\) for \(\text{Si}\), \(1.4\ \text{eV}\) for \(\text{GaAs}\) and \(0.66\ \text{eV}\) for \(\text{Ge}\).

At \(T = 0\ \text{K}\) every valence state is full and the conduction band is empty, so the crystal is a perfect insulator. At higher temperature, thermal energy lifts some electrons across \(E_g\). Each one leaves an empty valence state called a hole, which behaves as a mobile carrier of charge \(+e\). Electrons and holes are created in pairs.

Figure 1.1: The amber arrow is the thermal energy that lifts one valence electron into the conduction band. The filled amber dot is the free electron and the open circle is the hole it left behind.

Intrinsic carrier concentration

In a pure crystal the number of electrons per cm³ equals the number of holes. Call it \(n_i\). A wider gap makes it exponentially smaller, and a higher temperature makes it larger.

Intrinsic carrier concentration\[ n_i = B\,T^{3/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right) \]
\(n_i\)
Intrinsic carrier concentration (cm\(^{-3}\)).
\(B\)
Material coefficient: \(\text{Si}\) \(5.23\times10^{15}\), \(\text{Ge}\) \(1.66\times10^{15}\), \(\text{GaAs}\) \(2.10\times10^{14}\) (cm\(^{-3}\)K\(^{-3/2}\)).
\(T\)
Absolute temperature (K).
\(E_g\)
Bandgap (eV).
\(k\)
Boltzmann's constant, \(8.62\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV/K}\) (the summary writes \(86\times10^{-6}\)).

For silicon at \(300\ \text{K}\) the formula gives \(1.56\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), which the course rounds to \(n_i \approx 1.5\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). That figure is used in every later calculation.

Worked example 1.1 temperature and bandgap

Find \(n_i\) for silicon at \(350\ \text{K}\) and compare it with \(300\ \text{K}\). Then find \(n_i\) for germanium at \(300\ \text{K}\). Use \(E_g = 1.1\ \text{eV}\) for \(\text{Si}\) and \(0.66\ \text{eV}\) for \(\text{Ge}\).

1
Silicon at \(350\ \text{K}\): \(T^{3/2} = 6548\) and \(E_g/2kT = 1.1/(2\times8.617\times10^{-5}\times350) = 18.24\), so \(\exp(-18.24) = 1.20\times10^{-8}\).
2
\[ n_i = (5.23\times10^{15})(6548)(1.20\times10^{-8}) = 4.12\times10^{11}\ \text{cm}^{-3} \]
3
Ratio to \(300\ \text{K}\): \(4.12\times10^{11}/1.56\times10^{10} = 26.3\).
4
Germanium at \(300\ \text{K}\) with \(B = 1.66\times10^{15}\): \(n_i = 2.47\times10^{13}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), which is about 1600 times the silicon value.
\(n_i(\text{Si}, 350\ \text{K}) = 4.12\times10^{11}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\), 26.3 times its \(300\ \text{K}\) value for a rise of only \(50\ \text{K}\). Germanium's smaller gap gives it thousands of times more intrinsic carriers than silicon at the same temperature.

02Doping

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.1 (2)

Adding a small amount of impurity makes the carrier concentration far larger than \(n_i\) and decides which carrier dominates.

The fixed ions are immobile. Only the carriers move, and this is why a doped crystal stays electrically neutral until a junction is made.

Mass-action law\[ n_0\,p_0 = n_i^2 \]
\(n_0\)
Thermal-equilibrium electron concentration (cm\(^{-3}\)).
\(p_0\)

\(n_i\)

The law holds for any doping, so raising one carrier concentration lowers the other by the same factor. This is how the minority-carrier values in the table follow from the majority ones.

Worked example 2.1 majority and minority carriers

Silicon at \(300\ \text{K}\) (\(n_i = 1.5\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)) is doped (a) n-type with \(N_d = 5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) and (b) p-type with \(N_a = 8\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Find both carrier concentrations in each.

1
(a) Electrons are the majority carrier, so \(n_0 \approx N_d = 5\times10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).
2
Holes follow from mass action: \(p_0 = n_i^2/N_d = (1.5\times10^{10})^2/(5\times10^{15}) = 4.50\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).
3
(b) Holes are the majority carrier, so \(p_0 \approx N_a = 8\times10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).
4
Electrons: \(n_0 = n_i^2/N_a = 2.81\times10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).
(a) \(n_0 = 5\times10^{15}\) and \(p_0 = 4.5\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). (b) \(p_0 = 8\times10^{16}\) and \(n_0 = 2.8\times10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). In each case the minority carriers are about eleven orders of magnitude below the majority carriers.

03Drift and diffusion

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.1 (3)

Carriers move by two mechanisms. Drift is directed motion caused by an electric field. Diffusion is motion from high to low concentration. Both matter in a pn junction, and equilibrium is the state where they cancel.

Drift current\[ v_{dn} = -\mu_n E \qquad v_{dp} = +\mu_p E \qquad J_{\text{drift}} = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)E = \sigma E = \frac{E}{\rho} \]
\(v_{dn}, v_{dp}\)
Electron and hole drift velocities (cm/s). Electrons move against the field.
\(\mu_n, \mu_p\)
Electron and hole mobilities (cm\(^2\)/V·s). For low-doped silicon at room temperature, \(\mu_n \approx 1350\) and \(\mu_p \approx 480\).
\(E\)
Electric field (V/cm).
\(J_{\text{drift}}\)
Drift current density (A/cm\(^2\)).
\(e\)
Electron charge, \(1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\).
\(n, p\)
Electron and hole concentrations (cm\(^{-3}\)).
\(\sigma\)
Conductivity (\((\Omega\cdot\text{cm})^{-1}\)).
\(\rho\)
Resistivity, \(1/\sigma\) (\(\Omega\cdot\text{cm}\)).

Electrons move more easily than holes, so \(\mu_n \gt \mu_p\) and n-type material conducts better than p-type at the same doping. Drift velocity does not grow forever. Above about \(10^4\ \text{V/cm}\) it saturates at \(v_{\text{sat}} \approx 10^7\ \text{cm/s}\).

Diffusion current\[ J_{n,\text{diff}} = eD_n\frac{dn}{dx} \qquad J_{p,\text{diff}} = -eD_p\frac{dp}{dx} \]
\(J_{n,\text{diff}}, J_{p,\text{diff}}\)
Electron and hole diffusion current densities (A/cm\(^2\)).
\(D_n, D_p\)
Diffusion coefficients (cm\(^2\)/s).
\(dn/dx, dp/dx\)
Concentration gradients (cm\(^{-4}\)).
Einstein relation\[ \frac{D_n}{\mu_n} = \frac{D_p}{\mu_p} = \frac{kT}{e} = V_T \approx 0.026\ \text{V at } 300\ \text{K} \]
\(D_n, D_p\)
Diffusion coefficients (cm\(^2\)/s).
\(\mu_n, \mu_p\)
Mobilities (cm\(^2\)/V·s).
\(k\)
Boltzmann's constant (\(1.38\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}\)).
\(T\)
Absolute temperature (K).
\(e\)
Electron charge (C).
\(V_T\)

Worked example 3.1 conductivity, drift and diffusion

An n-type silicon bar has \(n \approx N_d = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) at \(300\ \text{K}\). Use the low-doped mobilities. Find its conductivity and resistivity, the electron drift velocity in a field of \(100\ \text{V/cm}\), and \(D_n\).

1
Holes are negligible, so \(\sigma = en\mu_n = (1.6\times10^{-19})(10^{16})(1350) = 2.16\ (\Omega\cdot\text{cm})^{-1}\).
2
\(\rho = 1/\sigma = 0.463\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}\).
3
\(|v_{dn}| = \mu_n E = 1350\times100 = 1.35\times10^{5}\ \text{cm/s}\), well below \(v_{\text{sat}}\).
4
Einstein relation: \(D_n = \mu_n V_T = 1350\times0.026 = 35.1\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}\).
\(\sigma = 2.16\ (\Omega\cdot\text{cm})^{-1}\), \(\rho = 0.463\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}\), \(v_{dn} = 1.35\times10^{5}\ \text{cm/s}\) and \(D_n = 35.1\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}\). At \(10^4\ \text{V/cm}\) the linear formula would give \(1.35\times10^{7}\ \text{cm/s}\), above \(v_{\text{sat}}\), so the velocity saturates there instead.
Mobility and doping

The summary quotes mobilities for low-doped silicon. In heavily doped material the mobility is lower, so the example above slightly overstates \(\sigma\) for \(10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Use the given values in this course.

04The pn junction in equilibrium

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.2 (1)

Join p-type and n-type material at a metallurgical junction at \(x = 0\). Holes are plentiful on the p side and scarce on the n side, so holes diffuse from p to n. Electrons diffuse from n to p for the same reason. Each carrier that leaves uncovers a fixed ion it used to cancel: negative acceptor ions \(N_a^-\) on the p side and positive donor ions \(N_d^+\) on the n side.

These ions form a space-charge region, also called the depletion region, because almost no mobile carriers remain in it. Its charge sets up an internal electric field pointing from n to p. That field pushes carriers back, and the junction reaches thermal equilibrium when the field's drift current exactly balances the diffusion current for both carrier types.

Figure 4.1: Circled signs are the fixed ions. Open circles are holes and filled dots are electrons. The amber arrow is the internal field. The lower plot is drawn from the depletion approximation with \(N_a = N_d\), so the potential rises smoothly by \(V_{bi}\) across the depletion region and is flat outside it.

The potential difference across the depletion region is the built-in potential.

Built-in potential\[ V_{bi} = V_T\ln\!\left(\frac{N_aN_d}{n_i^2}\right) \]
\(V_{bi}\)
Built-in potential barrier (V). Typically \(0.6\) to \(0.8\ \text{V}\) for silicon.
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage, \(kT/e \approx 0.026\ \text{V}\).
\(N_a, N_d\)
Acceptor and donor concentrations on the two sides (cm\(^{-3}\)).
\(n_i\)
Intrinsic carrier concentration (cm\(^{-3}\)).
Watch out

You cannot measure \(V_{bi}\) with a voltmeter. The metal contacts at the leads add contact potentials that cancel it, so no net voltage appears at the terminals of an unbiased diode.

Worked example 4.1 built-in potential

A silicon junction at \(300\ \text{K}\) has \(N_a = 10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\) and \(N_d = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Find \(V_{bi}\). Then find it again when \(N_a\) is raised tenfold.

1
\(N_aN_d/n_i^2 = 10^{33}/(1.5\times10^{10})^2 = 4.44\times10^{12}\).
2
\(V_{bi} = 0.026\ln(4.44\times10^{12}) = 0.026\times29.12 = 0.757\ \text{V}\).
3
With \(N_a = 10^{18}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\): \(V_{bi} = 0.817\ \text{V}\).
\(V_{bi} = 0.757\ \text{V}\), rising to \(0.817\ \text{V}\). Ten times more doping adds only \(V_T\ln 10 \approx 60\ \text{mV}\), because \(V_{bi}\) depends on the logarithm of the doping.

05Reverse bias

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.2 (2)

Reverse bias means a positive voltage \(V_R\) on the n region relative to the p region, so \(v_D = -V_R \lt 0\). The applied voltage adds to the built-in one, and the barrier becomes \(V_{bi} + V_R\). The internal field strengthens and the depletion region widens.

Depletion width and junction capacitance\[ W \propto \sqrt{V_{bi} + V_R} \qquad C_j = C_{j0}\left(1 + \frac{V_R}{V_{bi}}\right)^{-1/2} \]
\(W\)
Depletion region width (cm).
\(V_{bi}\)
Built-in potential (V).
\(V_R\)
Magnitude of the reverse bias (V).
\(C_j\)
Junction (depletion layer) capacitance at reverse bias \(V_R\) (F).
\(C_{j0}\)
Junction capacitance at zero bias (F).

Majority carriers cannot cross the larger barrier. Only a tiny reverse saturation current \(I_S\) flows, made of thermally generated minority carriers near the edges of the depletion region.

The depletion region is an insulating layer between two conducting regions, so it acts as a capacitor. A wider layer means a smaller capacitance. A varactor diode uses this to make a capacitor that a reverse voltage can tune.

Worked example 5.1 a tunable capacitance

A junction has \(C_{j0} = 2\ \text{pF}\) and \(V_{bi} = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Find \(C_j\) at \(V_R = 5\ \text{V}\) and the factor by which the depletion width grows.

1
\(1 + V_R/V_{bi} = 1 + 5/0.7 = 8.14\).
2
\(C_j = 2/\sqrt{8.14} = 0.701\ \text{pF}\).
3
\(W(5\ \text{V})/W(0) = \sqrt{(0.7+5)/0.7} = 2.85\).
\(C_j = 0.701\ \text{pF}\), about 35% of the zero-bias value. The depletion width grows by a factor of 2.85, and the capacitance falls by the same factor.

06Forward bias and the diode equation

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.2 (3 and 4)

Forward bias means a positive voltage \(v_D\) on the p region relative to the n region. It lowers the barrier to \(V_{bi} - v_D\), and large numbers of majority carriers diffuse across. Electrons from the n side enter the p side, and holes from the p side enter the n side. There they are excess minority carriers. They diffuse through the neutral regions and recombine with majority carriers, which carries a strong diffusion current.

Ideal diode equation\[ i_D = I_S\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{v_D}{nV_T}\right) - 1\right] \]
\(i_D\)
Diode current, positive from anode to cathode (A).
\(I_S\)
Reverse saturation current (A). It is proportional to the junction area and is typically \(10^{-18}\) to \(10^{-12}\ \text{A}\) for silicon.
\(v_D\)
Voltage from anode to cathode (V).
\(n\)
Emission coefficient, \(1 \le n \le 2\). Take \(n = 1\) unless recombination in the depletion region matters.
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage, \(kT/e \approx 26\ \text{mV}\) at \(300\ \text{K}\) (V).

Two limits cover almost every calculation:

Forward and reverse limits\[ v_D \gt 0.1\ \text{V}:\quad i_D \approx I_S\exp\!\left(\frac{v_D}{nV_T}\right),\quad v_D = nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{i_D}{I_S}\right) \qquad v_D \le -0.1\ \text{V}:\quad i_D \approx -I_S \]
\(i_D\)
Diode current (A).
\(v_D\)
Diode voltage (V).
\(I_S\)
Reverse saturation current (A).
\(n\)
Emission coefficient (dimensionless).
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage (V).
Key idea

Forward current grows by a factor of 10 for roughly every \(60\ \text{mV}\) increase in \(v_D\) (\(n = 1\), \(300\ \text{K}\)). Voltage depends on the logarithm of current, so a diode's forward voltage barely moves while its current changes a great deal. That is why \(0.6\) to \(0.7\ \text{V}\) is a usable figure for silicon.

Worked example 6.1 using the diode equation

A silicon diode has \(I_S = 10^{-14}\ \text{A}\), \(n = 1\) and \(V_T = 26\ \text{mV}\). Find the voltage at \(i_D = 2\ \text{mA}\), the current at \(v_D = 0.65\ \text{V}\) and the current at \(v_D = -0.3\ \text{V}\).

1
Forward voltage: \(v_D = 0.026\ln(2\times10^{-3}/10^{-14}) = 0.026\times26.02 = 0.6766\ \text{V}\).
2
Current at \(0.65\ \text{V}\): \(i_D = 10^{-14}\exp(0.65/0.026) = 0.720\ \text{mA}\).
3
At \(-0.3\ \text{V}\) the exponential is negligible, so \(i_D \approx -I_S = -10^{-14}\ \text{A}\).
4
Check the decade rule: \(V_T\ln 10 = 59.9\ \text{mV}\) per factor of 10.
\(v_D = 0.677\ \text{V}\) at \(2\ \text{mA}\); \(i_D = 0.72\ \text{mA}\) at \(0.65\ \text{V}\); \(i_D \approx -10\ \text{fA}\) at \(-0.3\ \text{V}\). Raising the voltage by \(26.6\ \text{mV}\) from \(0.65\ \text{V}\) to \(0.6766\ \text{V}\) multiplies the current by \(2.78\).

07Temperature and breakdown

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.2 (5 and 6)

The saturation current depends strongly on temperature, because \(I_S \propto n_i^2 \propto T^3\exp(-E_g/kT)\). In theory \(I_S\) doubles for about every \(5\,^\circ\text{C}\). In real diodes the measured reverse current doubles for about every \(10\,^\circ\text{C}\).

At a fixed forward current the opposite happens to the voltage. The exponential in the diode equation grows with temperature, but \(I_S\) grows faster, so the voltage needed for the same current falls.

Forward voltage temperature coefficient\[ \frac{dv_D}{dT} \approx -2.0\ \text{mV}/^\circ\text{C} \]
\(dv_D/dT\)
Change in forward voltage per unit temperature at constant diode current (mV/\(^\circ\)C).

At \(25\,^\circ\text{C}\) a diode has a reverse current of \(2\ \text{nA}\) and needs \(0.650\ \text{V}\) at a fixed forward current. Estimate both at the stated temperatures using the real-diode rules above.

1
Reverse current at \(85\,^\circ\text{C}\): \(60\,^\circ\text{C}\) higher is six doublings, so the factor is \(2^6 = 64\).
2
\(I_R = 2\ \text{nA}\times64 = 128\ \text{nA}\).
3

4
\(v_D = 0.650 - 0.100 = 0.550\ \text{V}\).
\(I_R \approx 128\ \text{nA}\) at \(85\,^\circ\text{C}\) and \(v_D \approx 0.550\ \text{V}\) at \(75\,^\circ\text{C}\). Leakage rises quickly with heat while forward voltage falls.

08Switching behaviour

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.2 (7)

Switch a diode quickly from forward conduction (current \(I_F\)) to reverse bias, with the reverse current limited by the circuit to \(I_R \approx V_R/R_R\). It does not turn off at once, because forward conduction stored excess minority carriers near the depletion region and they must be removed first.

Worked example 8.1 reverse recovery

A diode carrying \(I_F\) is switched to a \(5\ \text{V}\) reverse supply through \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\). It has \(t_s = 40\ \text{ns}\) and \(t_f = 25\ \text{ns}\).

1
\(I_R \approx V_R/R_R = 5/1000 = 5\ \text{mA}\), flowing backwards during \(t_s\).
2
\(t_{rr} = 40 + 25 = 65\ \text{ns}\).
The diode conducts backwards at about \(5\ \text{mA}\) for \(40\ \text{ns}\), and is fully off after about \(65\ \text{ns}\). A Schottky diode (section 11) has \(t_s \approx 0\) and avoids most of this.

09DC analysis and diode models

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.3

The standard circuit is a supply \(V_{PS}\) in series with a resistor \(R\) and a diode. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives \(V_{PS} = I_DR + V_D\), and the diode equation gives a second relation between \(I_D\) and \(V_D\). Four methods solve the pair. They trade accuracy for effort, and the exponential equation cannot be solved in closed form.

Iteration

Guess \(V_D\) (\(0.7\ \text{V}\) is a good start), find \(I_D = (V_{PS} - V_D)/R\), then update \(V_D = nV_T\ln(I_D/I_S)\). Repeat until \(V_D\) stops changing. It converges fast because \(V_D\) depends only on the logarithm of the current.

Load line

Load line\[ I_D = \frac{V_{PS}}{R} - \frac{V_D}{R} \]
\(I_D\)
Diode current (A).
\(V_{PS}\)
Supply voltage (V). It is the intercept on the voltage axis.
\(R\)
Series resistance (\(\Omega\)). The current-axis intercept is \(V_{PS}/R\) and the slope is \(-1/R\).
\(V_D\)
Diode voltage (V).

Plot this straight line over the diode's \(i_D\)-\(v_D\) curve. They cross at the quiescent point (Q-point) \((V_{DQ}, I_{DQ})\), the only pair of values that satisfies both the diode and the circuit.

Ideal and piecewise linear models

For hand work, replace the exponential by straight lines.

By hand \(r_f\) is usually taken as zero, so \(v_D \approx V_\gamma\) whenever \(i_D \gt 0\). The model is a source \(V_\gamma\) in series with \(r_f\) when ON and an open circuit when OFF.

10The small-signal model

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.4

Often a diode carries a DC bias (\(V_{PS}\), \(I_{DQ}\), \(V_{DQ}\)) plus a small time-varying signal \(v_i(t)\). Write each variable as its DC value plus the signal part: \(v_D(t) = V_{DQ} + v_d(t)\) and \(i_D(t) = I_{DQ} + i_d(t)\).

Near the Q-point the exponential curve looks like its tangent, so the signal part follows a straight-line law. That holds only while the signal is small.

Small-signal condition\[ v_d \ll nV_T \quad (\approx 26\ \text{mV}) \]
\(v_d\)
Signal part of the diode voltage (V).
\(n\)
Emission coefficient.
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage (V).

The slope of the tangent is the small-signal conductance, and its inverse is the diffusion resistance.

Small-signal conductance and diffusion resistance\[ g_d = \left.\frac{di_D}{dv_D}\right|_{Q} = \frac{I_{DQ}}{nV_T} \qquad r_d = \frac{1}{g_d} = \frac{nV_T}{I_{DQ}} \]
\(g_d\)
Small-signal conductance (S).
\(i_D, v_D\)
Total diode current (A) and voltage (V).
\(I_{DQ}\)
DC diode current at the Q-point (A).
\(n\)
Emission coefficient.
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage (V).
\(r_d\)
Diffusion resistance (\(\Omega\)).

At \(I_{DQ} = 1\ \text{mA}\), \(r_d = 26\ \text{mV}/1\ \text{mA} = 26\ \Omega\). At \(0.25\ \text{mA}\) it is \(104\ \Omega\). A diode is a variable resistor controlled by its bias current.

Figure 10.1: The amber line is the tangent at the Q-point, with slope \(g_d\). The signal below the voltage axis has an amplitude of \(10\ \text{mV}\), which is larger than a true small signal, so the current waveform to the right is visibly lopsided. The exponential curve rises faster above the Q-point than the tangent does. Keep \(v_d \ll 26\ \text{mV}\) and the lopsidedness disappears.

Capacitances and the full model

Forward bias stores minority-carrier charge, and that charge changes with voltage. The result is a diffusion capacitance proportional to the current.

Diffusion capacitance and small-signal admittance\[ C_d = \frac{dQ}{dv_D} \approx \frac{\tau_T}{V_T}\,I_{DQ} \qquad Y = g_d + j\omega\,(C_d + C_j) \]
\(C_d\)
Diffusion capacitance (F).
\(Q\)
Stored excess minority-carrier charge (C).
\(v_D\)
Diode voltage (V).
\(\tau_T\)
Minority-carrier lifetime or transit time (s).
\(V_T\)
Thermal voltage (V).
\(I_{DQ}\)
DC diode current (A).
\(Y\)
Small-signal admittance (S).
\(g_d\)
Small-signal conductance (S).
\(\omega\)
Signal angular frequency (rad/s).
\(C_j\)
Junction capacitance (F).

Under forward bias \(C_d\) is much larger than \(C_j\). The network is \(r_d\) in parallel with \(C_d + C_j\), all in series with the ohmic resistance \(r_s\) of the neutral semiconductor.

Figure 10.2: A is the anode and K the cathode. At low frequency the capacitors act as open circuits and only \(r_s + r_d\) remains.

Worked example 10.1 a signal on top of a bias

The circuit of example 9.1 (\(5\ \text{V}\), \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(I_S = 10^{-14}\ \text{A}\)) also has a \(10\ \text{mV}\) amplitude signal in series with the supply. Take \(\tau_T = 10\ \text{ns}\). Find \(r_d\), the signal current and voltage across the diode, and \(C_d\).

1
The bias is \(I_{DQ} = 4.30\ \text{mA}\), so \(r_d = 0.026/4.30\times10^{-3} = 6.04\ \Omega\).
2
The signal sees \(R\) and \(r_d\) in series: \(i_d = v_i/(R + r_d) = 0.010/(1000 + 6.04) = 9.94\ \mu\text{A}\).
3
\(v_d = i_dr_d = 60.1\ \mu\text{V}\), far below \(26\ \text{mV}\), so the small-signal model is valid.
4
\(C_d = (\tau_T/V_T)I_{DQ} = (10\times10^{-9}/0.026)(4.30\times10^{-3}) = 1.66\ \text{nF}\).
\(r_d = 6.04\ \Omega\), \(i_d = 9.94\ \mu\text{A}\) peak, \(v_d = 60.1\ \mu\text{V}\) peak and \(C_d = 1.66\ \text{nF}\). Nearly all the signal voltage drops across \(R\), because \(r_d\) is so small.

Worked example 11.1 an LED and a Schottky diode

(a) An LED with \(V_\gamma = 1.8\ \text{V}\) runs at \(10\ \text{mA}\) from a \(5\ \text{V}\) supply. Find the series resistor and the wavelength of the light, taking the photon energy as \(V_\gamma\) electron-volts and \(hc = 1240\ \text{eV·nm}\). (b) A rectifier passes \(2\ \text{A}\). 1
\(R = (5 - 1.8)/10\ \text{mA} = 320\ \Omega\).
2
\(\lambda = hc/E = 1240/1.8 = 689\ \text{nm}\), which is red.
3

\(R = 320\ \Omega\) and \(\lambda \approx 689\ \text{nm}\). The Schottky diode saves 0.8 W, which is more than half the loss.

12The diode thermometer

Reading Chapter 1 summary, section 1.6

A diode biased by a constant current source has a forward voltage that falls almost linearly with temperature, which makes it a sensor. Write the diode current at two temperatures, \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), with the same current in both. Their ratio is 1:

Constant-current condition\[ \frac{I_{D1}}{I_{D2}} = \frac{e^{-E_g/kT_1}\,e^{eV_{D1}/kT_1}}{e^{-E_g/kT_2}\,e^{eV_{D2}/kT_2}} = 1 \]
\(I_{D1}, I_{D2}\)
Diode current at \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (A).
\(E_g\)
Bandgap (eV).
\(k\)
Boltzmann's constant.
\(T_1, T_2\)
Two absolute temperatures (K).
\(e\)
Electron charge (C).
\(V_{D1}, V_{D2}\)
Diode voltages at \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (V).

Solving for \(V_{D2}\) gives the voltage at any temperature from one calibration point:

Diode voltage against temperature\[ V_{D2} = \frac{E_g}{e}\left(1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) + V_{D1}\,\frac{T_2}{T_1} \]
\(V_{D2}\)
Diode voltage at temperature \(T_2\) (V).
\(E_g/e\)
Bandgap in volts (\(1.12\ \text{V}\) for silicon).
\(T_1\)
Calibration temperature (K).
\(T_2\)
Temperature being measured (K).
\(V_{D1}\)
Diode voltage at the calibration temperature (V).

For silicon, \(E_g/e = 1.12\ \text{V}\), calibrated at \(T_1 = 300\ \text{K}\):

Silicon diode thermometer\[ V_D(T) = 1.12 - 0.522\left(\frac{T}{300}\right) \]
\(V_D(T)\)
Diode voltage at the constant bias current (V).
\(T\)
Absolute temperature (K).
Watch out

The constant \(0.522\) is \(1.12 - V_{D1}\), so the summary has assumed \(V_{D1} = 0.598\ \text{V}\) at \(300\ \text{K}\) without stating it. The slope is \(-0.522/300 = -1.74\ \text{mV/K}\), a little below the \(-2.0\ \text{mV}/^\circ\text{C}\) of section 7. The two agree only for a different calibration voltage, about \(0.52\ \text{V}\). The straight line also ignores the \(T^3\) factor in \(I_S\). Calibrate your own sensor at a known temperature.

Worked example 12.1 reading a temperature

A silicon diode follows \(V_D(T) = 1.12 - 0.522(T/300)\) at its bias current. Find \(V_D\) at \(350\ \text{K}\), and the temperature when \(V_D = 0.550\ \text{V}\).

1
\(V_D(350) = 1.12 - 0.522\times350/300 = 0.511\ \text{V}\).
2
Rearrange: \(T = 300(1.12 - V_D)/0.522 = 300(1.12 - 0.550)/0.522 = 327.6\ \text{K}\).
3
In degrees Celsius: \(327.6 - 273.15 = 54.4\,^\circ\text{C}\).
\(V_D(350\ \text{K}) = 0.511\ \text{V}\), and \(0.550\ \text{V}\) corresponds to \(327.6\ \text{K}\) (\(54.4\,^\circ\text{C}\)).

\[ \sigma = ep\mu_p = (1.6\times10^{-19})(3\times10^{17})(480) = 23.0\ (\Omega\cdot\text{cm})^{-1} \]

\(r_d = 0.026/3.78\times10^{-3} = 6.87\ \Omega\).

A \(12\ \text{V}\) supply and a \(5\ \text{mV}\) amplitude signal drive a diode (\(I_S = 10^{-14}\ \text{A}\)) through \(4.7\ \text{k}\Omega\). The diode has \(\tau_T = 4\ \text{ns}\). Find \(I_{DQ}\), \(r_d\), the signal current and voltage amplitudes, and \(C_d\).
