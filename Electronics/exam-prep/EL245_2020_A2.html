Memo for question 2

Approach. (a) is definitions: each mark is one idea, in your own words. (b) uses the fact that a dopant concentration far above \(n_i\) sets the majority carrier concentration, then the mass-action law \(n_o p_o = n_i^2\). (c) uses the diode equation without the −1 term.

(a)(i)Extrinsic semiconductor[2]

  1. A semiconductor into which controlled amounts of impurity atoms have been added: donors (group V, such as phosphorus) or acceptors (group III, such as boron).

    1

  2. Its electron and hole concentrations are set by the doping rather than by the material alone, so one carrier type dominates and the conductivity can be designed.

    1

(a)(ii)Majority charge carrier[2]

  1. The type of charge carrier present in the larger concentration in a doped semiconductor.

    1

  2. Electrons in n-type material (donor doped); holes in p-type material (acceptor doped).

    1

(a)(iii)Built-in potential[3]

  1. When p and n material meet, holes diffuse into the n side and electrons into the p side, leaving the fixed ionised acceptors and donors uncovered: the space-charge (depletion) region.

    1

  2. The charge there sets up an electric field from the n side to the p side, and a potential difference across the junction with no voltage applied: the built-in potential, \(V_{bi} = V_T \ln(N_a N_d / n_i^2)\).

    1

  3. In equilibrium it is a barrier that balances diffusion with drift, so no net current flows. It cannot be measured with a voltmeter.

    1

(a)(iv)Drift current[2]

  1. Current caused by charge carriers moving under an applied electric field: holes along the field, electrons against it.

    1

  2. Its density is \(J_{drf} = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)E\): proportional to the field, the carrier concentrations and their mobilities.

    1

(b)(i)Dopant type and concentration[2]

  1. Holes must be the majority carriers, so the material must be p-type: add acceptor impurities.

    1

  2. With \(N_a \gg n_i\), \(p_o \approx N_a\), so \(N_a = 5 \times 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).

    1
Acceptors, \(N_a = 5 \times 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)

(b)(ii)Electron concentration at 324 K[4]

  1. Intrinsic concentration: \(n_i = B T^{3/2} e^{-E_g/2kT}\), with \(B = 5.23 \times 10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\text{K}^{-3/2}\) and \(E_g = 1.1\) eV for silicon.

    1

  2. \(kT = (86.17 \times 10^{-6})(324) = 0.02792\) eV, so \(n_i = (5.23 \times 10^{15})(324)^{3/2} e^{-1.1/(2 \times 0.02792)} = 8.49 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).

    1

  3. Mass-action law: \(n_o p_o = n_i^2\), so \(n_o = n_i^2/p_o\).

    1

  4. \(n_o = \dfrac{(8.49 \times 10^{10})^2}{5 \times 10^{16}} = 1.44 \times 10^{5}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\).

    1
\(n_o = 1.44 \times 10^{5}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\)

Check. At 300 K the same steps give \(n_i = 1.57 \times 10^{10}\), the familiar \(1.5 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). The 24 K rise multiplies \(n_i\) by 5.4 and \(n_o\) by its square.

(c)Forward voltage for 20 times the current[5]

  1. Strong forward bias: \(e^{V_D/V_T} \gg 1\), so \(I_D \approx I_S e^{V_D/V_T}\).

    1

  2. Ratio of two currents: \(\dfrac{I_{D2}}{I_{D1}} = e^{(V_{D2} - V_{D1})/V_T} = 20\). \(I_S\) cancels.

    1

  3. So \(\Delta V_D = V_T \ln 20\).

    1

  4. \(V_T = kT/e = (86.17 \times 10^{-6})(315) = 0.02714\) V.

    1

  5. \(\Delta V_D = 0.02714 \times \ln 20 = 0.02714 \times 2.996 = 81.3\) mV.

    1
\(\Delta V_D = 81.3\) mV

Memo for question 4

Approach. (a) The two measured voltages give the emitter and base currents directly; the collector current is their difference. (b) Put the Q-point at the middle of the load line, then use the bias-stability rule \(R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E\) and KVL round the base loop. (a)\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) from measurements[8]

  1. Assume forward-active mode: emitter-base junction forward biased (given, \(V_{EB} = 0.7\) V) and collector-base junction reverse biased. To prove below.

    1

  2. Emitter current through the 10 kΩ: \(I_E = \dfrac{10 - 1.7}{10\ \text{k}\Omega} = 0.830\) mA.

    1

  3. Base voltage: \(V_B = V_E - V_{EB(on)} = 1.7 - 0.7 = 1.0\) V.

    1

  4. The base current flows out of the base through the 150 kΩ to ground: \(I_B = \dfrac{1.0}{150\ \text{k}\Omega} = 6.667\ \mu\text{A}\).

    1

  5. \(I_C = I_E - I_B = 0.830 - 0.00667 = 0.8233\) mA.

    1

  6. \(\beta = \dfrac{I_C}{I_B} = \dfrac{0.8233\ \text{mA}}{6.667\ \mu\text{A}} = 123.5\).

    1

  7. \(\alpha = \dfrac{I_C}{I_E} = \dfrac{0.8233}{0.830} = 0.9920\), which agrees with \(\beta/(1+\beta)\).

    1

  8. Proof: \(V_C = -10 + (0.8233\ \text{mA})(10\ \text{k}\Omega) = -1.767\) V. Then \(V_{EC} = 1.7 - (-1.767) = 3.47\) V > \(V_{EC(sat)}\), and \(V_B = 1.0\) V > \(V_C\), so the collector-base junction is reverse biased: forward-active.

    1
\(\beta = 123.5\), \(\alpha = 0.9920\)

(b)(i)Load line and Q-point[2]

  1. KVL round the collector-emitter loop (taking \(I_E = I_C(1+\beta)/\beta\)): \(V_{CE} = 20 - I_C\left(R_C + \tfrac{101}{100}R_E\right) = 20 - I_C(7.1515\ \text{k}\Omega)\). The load line runs from \(V_{CE} = 20\) V at \(I_C = 0\) to \(I_C = 2.797\) mA at \(V_{CE} = 0\).

    1

  2. Ignoring saturation, the largest symmetrical swing is from the middle of the load line: \(V_{CEQ} = 10\) V, \(I_{CQ} = 10/7.1515\ \text{k}\Omega = 1.398\) mA.

    1
\(V_{CEQ} = 10\) V, \(I_{CQ} = 1.398\) mA

The Q-point sits halfway along the load line, so the output can swing 10 V either way before reaching cutoff or (ignoring \(V_{CE(sat)}\)) the bottom of the line. Using \(I_E \approx I_C\) gives 20 V / 7.15 kΩ: the same Q-point to three figures.

(b)(ii)Bias-stable divider[7]

  1. Bias stability: \(R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E = 0.1(101)(150) = 1515\ \Omega\), so the base current barely affects the base voltage.

    1

  2. \(I_{BQ} = I_{CQ}/\beta = 13.98\ \mu\text{A}\) and \(I_{EQ} = (1+\beta)I_{BQ} = 1.4123\) mA.

    1

  3. KVL round the base loop, from the Thevenin source to \(V^-\): \(V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E + V^-\).

    1

  4. \(V_{TH} = (13.98\ \mu\text{A})(1515) + 0.7 + (1.4123\ \text{mA})(150) - 10 = -9.067\) V.

    1

  5. With the divider across \(V^+ - V^- = 20\) V: \(V_{TH} = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}(20) - 10\) and \(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2\). So \(R_1 = \dfrac{R_{TH}(20)}{V_{TH} + 10} = \dfrac{1515 \times 20}{0.933} = 32.47\ \text{k}\Omega\).

    1

  6. \(R_2 = \dfrac{R_{TH}R_1}{R_1 - R_{TH}} = 1.589\ \text{k}\Omega\).

    1

  7. Check: \(R_1 \parallel R_2 = 1515\ \Omega\) and \(\tfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}(20) - 10 = -9.067\) V, which give back \(I_{CQ} = 1.398\) mA and \(V_{CEQ} = 10.0\) V.

    1
\(R_1 = 32.47\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 1.589\ \text{k}\Omega\)

(c)Transfer curve of the pnp stage[8]

  1. The emitter is at 5 V. The transistor is off until \(V_{EB} = 0.7\) V, so for \(V_I \ge 4.3\) V: cutoff, \(I_C = 0\), \(V_O = 0\).

    1

  2. For \(V_I \lt 4.3\) V, base current flows out through \(R_B\): \(I_B = \dfrac{5 - 0.7 - V_I}{200\ \text{k}\Omega}\).

    1

  3. Forward active: \(V_O = \beta I_B R_C = \dfrac{100 \times 5k}{200k}(4.3 - V_I) = 2.5(4.3 - V_I)\).

    1

  4. Saturation starts when \(V_{EC} = 0.2\) V, that is \(V_O = 5 - 0.2 = 4.8\) V.

    1

  5. \(2.5(4.3 - V_I) = 4.8\) gives \(V_I = 4.3 - 1.92 = 2.38\) V.

    1

  6. For \(0 \le V_I \le 2.38\) V the transistor is saturated and \(V_O = 4.8\) V.

    1

  7. Sketch: flat at 4.8 V, a straight line of slope −2.5 down to 0 V at 4.3 V, then flat at 0 V.

    1

  8. Label the three modes: saturation, forward active, cutoff, with the break points (2.38 V, 4.8 V) and (4.3 V, 0 V).

    1
Saturation for \(V_I \le 2.38\) V (\(V_O = 4.8\) V); active, \(V_O = 2.5(4.3 - V_I)\); cutoff for \(V_I \ge 4.3\) V (\(V_O = 0\))

The pnp conducts when its base is pulled below the 5 V emitter. A low input saturates it, so the output sits 0.2 V below the supply. Between 2.38 V and 4.3 V the output follows the input with gain \(-\beta R_C/R_B = -2.5\).
