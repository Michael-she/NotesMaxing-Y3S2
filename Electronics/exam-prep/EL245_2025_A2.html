(a)Why reverse bias gives (almost) no current[4]

  1. Before bias: majority carriers diffuse across the junction (holes into the n side, electrons into the p side). They leave a depletion region of fixed ions whose field pushes them back. In equilibrium this diffusion is balanced by drift, so the net current is zero.

    1

  2. Reverse bias (positive on the n side) adds to the built-in field: the depletion region widens and the potential barrier rises by the applied voltage.

    1

  3. Majority carriers now cannot climb the barrier, so the diffusion current falls to practically zero.

    1

  4. Only minority carriers (electrons on the p side, holes on the n side) that reach the edge are swept across by the field. There are very few, so this reverse saturation current \(I_S\) is tiny (about \(10^{-15}\) to \(10^{-12}\) A in silicon) and does not grow with the reverse voltage: for practical purposes no current flows.

    1

The fixed ions (− on the p side, + on the n side) are the shaded region. Reverse bias uncovers more of them, widening it and strengthening the field, which stops the majority carriers (open circles: holes; dots: electrons) from crossing.

(b)Forward voltage for 20 times the current[5]

  1. Strong forward bias: \(e^{V_D/V_T} \gg 1\), so \(I_D \approx I_S e^{V_D/V_T}\).

    1

  2. Ratio of the two currents: \(\dfrac{I_{D2}}{I_{D1}} = e^{(V_{D2} - V_{D1})/V_T} = 20\); \(I_S\) cancels.

    1

  3. So \(\Delta V_D = V_T \ln 20\).

    1

  4. \(V_T = kT/e = (86.17 \times 10^{-6})(300) = 0.02585\) V.

    1

  5. \(\Delta V_D = 0.02585 \times 2.996 = 77.4\) mV (with \(V_T = 0.026\) V, 77.9 mV).

    1
\(\Delta V_D \approx 77.4\) mV

(c)I–V curve and piecewise-linear model[5]

  1. Arsenic (group V) is a donor and gallium (group III) an acceptor, so the regions doped with each form a silicon pn junction diode.

    1

  2. Forward bias: the current is negligible until about \(V_\gamma \approx 0.7\) V, then rises exponentially, \(i_D = I_S(e^{v_D/V_T} - 1)\).

    1

  3. Reverse bias: a tiny, constant current \(-I_S\), until breakdown at \(-V_Z\), where the current rises sharply in the reverse direction.

    1

  4. Piecewise-linear model: zero current for \(v_D \lt V_\gamma\), then a straight line of slope \(1/r_f\) (forward resistance).

    1

  5. Label on the sketch: \(V_\gamma\), \(r_f\) (slope), \(I_S\), \(V_Z\), and which side is forward and reverse bias.

    1

The reverse current and the breakdown region are exaggerated so that they show; in reality \(I_S\) is many orders of magnitude below the forward current.

(d)Clamping to a minimum of −2 V[5]

  1. Circuit: \(v_I\) through a series capacitor \(C\) to \(v_O\); a diode from \(v_O\) to a reference, cathode at \(v_O\), so it conducts whenever \(v_O\) tries to fall below the reference.

    1

  2. Assume an ideal diode (\(V_\gamma = 0\)) and no load (or a load with \(RC\) much longer than the period), so the capacitor never discharges. Then the reference is \(V_B = -2\) V: a 2 V battery with its + terminal to ground. With \(V_\gamma = 0.7\) V use a 1.3 V battery instead.

    1

  3. On the first negative peak the diode conducts and charges \(C\) to \(v_C = -10 - (-2) = -8\) V (left plate negative), after which the diode stays off.

    1

  4. Steady state: \(v_O = v_I - v_C = 10\sin(\omega t) + 8\) V: the same sine, shifted up by 8 V.

    1

  5. Waveforms: \(v_I\) from −10 V to +10 V; \(v_O\) from −2 V to +18 V, two cycles each.

    1
\(v_O = 10\sin(\omega t) + 8\) V, minimum −2 V, maximum 18 V

The diode connects the output to −2 V. It conducts only at the negative peak, topping up the capacitor; otherwise the capacitor holds 8 V and simply adds it to the input.

The output keeps the input's shape and amplitude; only its DC level changes, so its minimum sits at the −2 V reference.

\(v_O = 6.4\) V for \(v_I \le 5.24\) V; \(v_O = 12 - \tfrac{10}{11}(11.4 - v_I)\) between; \(v_O = 12\) V for \(v_I \ge 11.4\) V

D₂ clamps the output at 6.4 V from below and R₂ pulls it to 12 V from above; in between, D₁ and the two resistors form a divider from 11.4 V.

Memo for question 2

Approach. In saturation the collector loop fixes \(R_C\). The base current at the edge of saturation is \(I_C/\beta\); doubling it and taking the input low (0 V) fixes \(R_B\). The paper does not give the on-state input; 0 V (logic low) is assumed.

\(R_C = 1.0\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_B = 56.5\ \text{k}\Omega\)

Memo for question 3

Approach. (a) Replace \(v_I\), \(R_1\), \(R_2\) and −12 V by a Thevenin source; then find where the transistor turns on and where it saturates. (b) Write \(I_C\) for the divider-biased stage and show the \(\beta\) in it nearly cancels.

(a)Transfer curve[10]

  1. Thevenin at the base: \(V_{TH} = \dfrac{100 v_I + 15(-12)}{115} = \dfrac{100 v_I - 180}{115}\).

    1

  2. \(R_{TH} = 15 \parallel 100 = 13.043\ \text{k}\Omega\).

    1

  3. Cutoff while \(V_{TH} \le 0.7\) V: \(100v_I - 180 \le 80.5\), so \(v_I \le 2.605\) V, and \(v_O = 12\) V.

    1

  4. Forward active: \(I_B = \dfrac{V_{TH} - 0.7}{R_{TH}}\), \(I_C = 80 I_B\).

    1

  5. \(v_O = 12 - 2.2\ \text{k}\Omega \cdot I_C\).

  6. Slope: \(\dfrac{dv_O}{dv_I} = -\dfrac{\beta R_C}{R_{TH}}\cdot\dfrac{100}{115} = -\dfrac{80(2.2)}{13.043}(0.8696) = -11.73\).

    1

  7. Saturation edge: \(v_O = 0.2\) V, so \(I_C = 11.8/2.2 = 5.364\) mA and \(I_B = 67.0\ \mu\text{A}\).

    1

  8. \(V_{TH} = 0.7 + (0.06705\ \text{mA})(13.043\ \text{k}\Omega) = 1.575\) V.

    1

  9. \(v_I = \dfrac{115 V_{TH} + 180}{100} = 3.61\) V. Above this the transistor is saturated and \(v_O = 0.2\) V.

    1

  10. Sketch: 12 V to the first breakpoint, a straight fall to 0.2 V, then flat to 5 V, with the three modes labelled.

The −12 V supply through R₂ holds the base below 0.7 V until the input passes 2.6 V. The active region is only 1 V wide, so the circuit behaves as an inverter.

(b)Why \(R_{TH} \approx 0.1\beta R_E\) makes \(I_C\) insensitive to \(\beta\)[10]

  1. Replace the divider by its Thevenin equivalent: \(V_{TH} = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}V_{CC}\), \(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2\).

    1

  2. KVL round the base-emitter loop: \(V_{TH} = I_B R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_E R_E\).

    1

  3. With \(I_E = (1+\beta)I_B\): \(I_B = \dfrac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E}\).

    1

  4. So \(I_C = \beta I_B = \dfrac{\beta(V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)})}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E}\).

    1

  5. If \((1+\beta)R_E \gg R_{TH}\): \(I_C \approx \dfrac{\beta}{1+\beta}\cdot\dfrac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E} \approx \dfrac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E}\), which does not contain \(\beta\).

    1

  6. With \(R_{TH} = 0.1\beta R_E\): \(I_C = \dfrac{\beta}{1.1\beta + 1}\cdot\dfrac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E}\).

    1

  7. For typical \(\beta\) of 100 to 200 the factor is 0.9009 to 0.9050: doubling \(\beta\) changes \(I_C\) by only 0.45 %.

    1

  8. Sensitivity: \(\dfrac{\beta}{I_C}\dfrac{dI_C}{d\beta} = \dfrac{R_{TH} + R_E}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E} = \dfrac{0.1\beta + 1}{1.1\beta + 1} = 0.099\) at \(\beta = 100\): a 1 % change in \(\beta\) gives about a 0.1 % change in \(I_C\).

    1

  9. Compare a base resistor with no \(R_E\): \(I_C = \beta I_B\), sensitivity 1, so \(I_C\) doubles when \(\beta\) doubles.

    1

  10. Why not make \(R_{TH}\) even smaller? It would draw more current from \(V_{CC}\) and load the input signal; \(0.1\beta R_E\) is the usual compromise between stability and input resistance.

    1
\(I_C \approx (V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)})/R_E\), independent of \(\beta\), when \(R_{TH} \ll (1+\beta)R_E\)
