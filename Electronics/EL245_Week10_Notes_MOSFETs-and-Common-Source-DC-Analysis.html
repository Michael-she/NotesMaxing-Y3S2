MOSFETs and common-source DC analysis

How a MOS gate creates a conducting channel, the current equations for n- and p-channel, enhancement and depletion devices, and how to find and design the DC operating point of a common-source circuit, including the four designs of Tutorial 10.

01The MOS structure

Reading Neamen 3.1.1 and 3.1.2; Lecture 21

The transistor family

Transistors split into bipolar junction transistors (npn, pnp) and field-effect transistors (FETs). FETs are junction FETs or metal-oxide-semiconductor FETs (MOSFETs). MOSFETs come in two modes, enhancement and depletion, each with an n-channel and a p-channel version. Unlike a BJT, a MOSFET's gate draws no DC current: the gate is insulated from the channel by an oxide.

The MOS capacitor

The basic MOS structure is a capacitor: a metal gate (for example aluminium), an insulating oxide of thickness \(t_{ox}\) and permittivity \(\varepsilon_{ox}\), and a semiconductor substrate as the second plate. For a p-type substrate:

Gate voltageWhat happens at the oxide-semiconductor interface
NegativeAccumulation: holes are attracted to the interface.
Small positiveDepletion: holes are pushed away, leaving a negative space-charge region of fixed acceptor atoms.
Larger positiveInversion: minority electrons are drawn to the interface and form an electron inversion layer.

Figure 1.1: Left to right: holes accumulate, a depletion region forms, and an electron layer appears under the oxide. The dashed outline marks the depletion region, which stays under the inversion layer.

Intuition

The gate and the body are the two plates of a capacitor. Whatever charge the gate carries, the body has to show the opposite charge, and the sign of the gate voltage decides what the body uses to supply it: majority carriers, bare ions, or minority carriers drawn to the surface.

An n-type substrate behaves the same way with all signs reversed: a positive gate accumulates electrons, and a large negative gate forms a hole inversion layer. The inversion charge grows with the applied voltage.

Definition

Enhancement mode: no channel exists at zero gate voltage; a gate voltage must be applied to create the inversion layer (positive for a p-type substrate, negative for an n-type substrate). The device is normally off.

Definition

Depletion mode: a channel is built into the device, so it already conducts at zero gate voltage. The gate is used to thin the channel until it switches off, or to thicken it so it carries more. The device is normally on.

Watch out

The inversion layer is made of minority carriers. An enhancement NMOS conducts through electrons even though its substrate is p-type, and the layer only exists while the gate voltage is above the threshold.

The n-channel enhancement MOSFET

Add two heavily doped n-type regions to the MOS capacitor, the source and the drain, and you have an n-channel enhancement MOSFET. The channel length \(L\) is typically below 1 µm and the oxide thinner than 400 Å.

Figure 1.2: With no gate voltage, the source and drain are separated by p-type material: two back-to-back diodes, so essentially no current flows. A large enough positive gate voltage forms the electron inversion layer (dashed), which connects source and drain. The source supplies the carriers and the drain collects them.

The drain current depends on the charge in the inversion layer, which depends on the gate voltage. No current flows through the substrate or through the gate.

Key idea

Gate current is zero at DC, so \(i_D\) is also the source current, and the only thing the gate does is set the channel charge. In every DC circuit this week the gate sits at whatever voltage its resistor divider gives it, with no loading from the transistor.

Units

Lengths appear in micrometres and ångströms, but the permittivity is given per centimetre, so convert to centimetres first: \(1\ \mu\text{m} = 10^{-4}\ \text{cm}\) and \(1\ \text{Å} = 10^{-8}\ \text{cm}\).

Enhancement and depletion mode

Every MOSFET type comes in both modes. The difference is whether the channel exists before any gate voltage is applied, and that shows up in one place: the sign of the threshold voltage.

02Enhancement NMOS current-voltage characteristics

Reading Neamen 3.1.3; Lecture 22

Enhancement mode

Sections 2 and 3 are about enhancement-mode devices: no channel at zero gate voltage, \(V_{TN} \gt 0\) and \(V_{TP} \lt 0\). The equations carry over unchanged to depletion-mode devices in section 4; only the sign of the threshold flips.

The threshold voltage \(V_{TN}\) is the gate-source voltage needed to create the inversion layer. Below it the transistor is off (\(i_D = 0\)). Above it, a small \(v_{DS}\) gives a current proportional to \(v_{DS}\). As \(v_{DS}\) rises, the channel near the drain thins, until at \(v_{DS(sat)} = v_{GS} - V_{TN}\) it is pinched off. Beyond that the current stops increasing: the transistor is in the saturation region.

  • Cutoff, \(v_{GS} \lt V_{TN}\): no inversion layer, so \(i_D = 0\).
  • Non-saturation (also called triode), \(v_{GS} \gt V_{TN}\) and \(v_{DS} \lt v_{DS(sat)}\): the channel runs the full length and the current rises with \(v_{DS}\).
  • Saturation, \(v_{GS} \gt V_{TN}\) and \(v_{DS} \ge v_{DS(sat)}\): the channel is pinched off at the drain end and the current stops depending on \(v_{DS}\).

\(v_{DS(sat)}\) depends on \(v_{GS}\). At pinch-off the oxide at the drain end only sees \(v_{GS} - v_{DS} = V_{TN}\), just enough to hold a layer of zero thickness, so a bigger gate voltage moves the edge to a larger \(v_{DS}\).

The designer controls \(W/L\); the process fixes \(k_n'\). In the ideal model the saturation current does not depend on \(v_{DS}\) at all, so the output resistance \(r_o = \Delta v_{DS}/\Delta i_D\) is infinite.

Intuition

A wider channel carries more current (\(W\) up), a longer one resists it (\(L\) up), a thinner oxide gives the gate a stronger grip on the channel (\(t_{ox}\) down), and more mobile carriers move faster. Only \(W/L\) is the designer's, so \(K_n\) is set by drawing a wider or narrower transistor.

Figure 2.1: Computed for \(K_n = 0.5\ \text{mA/V}^2\) and \(V_{TN} = 1\ \text{V}\). Each teal curve rises as a parabola in the non-saturation region and goes flat at \(v_{DS(sat)} = v_{GS} - V_{TN}\). The dashed amber curve, \(i_D = K_n v_{DS}^2\), joins all those corners.

Watch out

A saturated MOSFET is the opposite of a saturated BJT. In a BJT, saturation means the collector junction is forward biased and the current gain is lost. In a MOSFET, saturation is the flat part of the output curve, the region you normally want for an amplifier.

Enhancement PMOS characteristics

The p-channel enhancement MOSFET has an n-type substrate and p-type source and drain. Holes are the carriers, so a negative gate voltage (relative to the source) is needed to form the hole inversion layer. Its threshold \(V_{TP}\) is negative, it turns on when \(v_{SG} \ge |V_{TP}|\), and conventional current flows into the source and out of the drain. Writing the equations with \(v_{SG}\) and \(v_{SD}\) keeps every quantity positive.

Symbols and conventions

In this course the source and substrate (body) are assumed to be connected. The arrow is on the source and shows the direction of current: out of the source for NMOS (current flows into the drain), into the source for PMOS (current flows out of the drain). The vertical line is the gate, separated from the channel line by the oxide gap. A broken channel line means enhancement mode; a solid line means a channel exists at zero bias (depletion mode).

04Depletion mode, CMOS and the summary

Reading Neamen 3.1.7 and 3.1.8; Lecture 24

Sections 2 and 3 covered enhancement-mode devices, which are off until the gate creates a channel. In a depletion-mode MOSFET a channel exists at \(v_{GS} = 0\). For an n-channel device, a negative \(v_{GS}\) creates a space-charge region that narrows the channel, and at \(v_{GS} = V_{TN}\) (negative) the channel is fully depleted and the current is zero. A positive \(v_{GS}\) forms an electron accumulation layer and increases the current.

A real transistor's saturation current rises slightly with the output voltage because the effective channel shortens. The summary slide includes this as a factor \((1 + \lambda v_{DS})\) for an NMOS and \((1 + \lambda v_{SD})\) for a PMOS. Every calculation this week takes \(\lambda = 0\), the ideal device, as Tutorial 10 Question 4 states outright; the output resistance is then infinite.

Saturation current with channel-length modulation\[ i_D = K_n\,(v_{GS} - V_{TN})^2\,(1 + \lambda\, v_{DS}) \qquad i_D = K_p\,(v_{SG} + V_{TP})^2\,(1 + \lambda\, v_{SD}) \]
\(i_D\)
Drain current in saturation, in A.
\(K_n, K_p\)
Conduction parameters, in A/V\(^2\).
\(\lambda\)
Channel-length modulation parameter, in V\(^{-1}\). The ideal device has \(\lambda = 0\), so the factor is 1.
\(v_{DS},\ v_{SD}\)
Output voltage, in V.
Key idea

With \(\lambda = 0\), \(i_D\) in saturation depends on the gate voltage alone, and the transistor acts as a current source controlled by \(v_{GS}\). That is why the DC circuits in section 5 can be solved with a single gate-to-source loop.

05Common-source DC analysis

Reading Neamen 3.2.1; Lecture 25

In the common-source circuit the source is common to input and output. Because the gate current is zero, the gate divider is unloaded: \(V_G\) follows directly from \(R_1\) and \(R_2\). The method mirrors the BJT one: assume saturation, solve, check \(v_{DS} \ge v_{DS(sat)}\); if the check fails, redo it in the non-saturation region.

  1. Find \(V_G\) from the divider, then \(V_{GS}\) (or \(V_{SG}\)). If the device is not above threshold, \(I_D = 0\) and you are done.
  2. Assume saturation and compute \(I_D\) from the saturation formula.
  3. Find \(V_{DS}\) (or \(V_{SD}\)) from the drain loop.
  4. Compare it with the edge of saturation, \(V_{GS} - V_{TN}\) (or \(V_{SG} + V_{TP}\)). If the output voltage is larger, the assumption holds.
  5. If it is smaller, the device is in non-saturation: discard the saturation current and solve again with the non-saturation equation.
NMOS common source\[ V_{GS} = V_G = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}V_{DD} \qquad V_{DS} = V_{DD} - I_D R_D \qquad P_T = I_D V_{DS} \]
\(V_{GS}\)
Gate-source voltage (V); equal to \(V_G\) because the source is grounded.
\(V_G\)
Gate voltage set by the divider (V).
\(R_2\)
Divider resistor, gate to ground (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_1\)
Divider resistor, supply to gate (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage (V).
\(V_{DS}\)
Drain-source voltage (V).
\(I_D\)
Drain current (A).
\(R_D\)
Drain resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(P_T\)
Power dissipated in the transistor (W).

Watch out

Do not stop after the saturation calculation. An output voltage that is negative, or below the edge of saturation, always means the saturation current was too big.

Designing a bias

A design question runs the analysis backwards: the operating point is given and the resistors are wanted.

  1. Find \(R_D\) (or \(R_S\)) from the voltage across it and the drain current.
  2. Assume saturation and solve for the overdrive. Take the positive root: \(v_{GS} - V_{TN} = \sqrt{I_D/K_n}\), or \(v_{SG} + V_{TP} = \sqrt{I_D/K_p}\). The negative root would put the device below threshold.
  3. Check the assumption: the output voltage must be at least the overdrive. If the question wants a switch, use the non-saturation equation instead.
  4. Get the gate voltage from the node voltages: \(V_G = V_{GS}\) for a grounded source, \(V_G = V_{DD} - V_{SG}\) for a PMOS with its source at the supply, or \(V_G = V_S \pm\) the control voltage in general.
  5. Choose \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) to put \(V_G\) on the gate and meet the extra condition on the divider.

Divider for a given input resistance

The input resistance at the gate is \(R_1 \parallel R_2\), because the gate draws nothing. For a divider between \(V^+\) and \(V^-\), the ratio \(R_2/(R_1+R_2)\) sets \(V_G\), and \(R_{in}\) fixes the size of the resistors. For a single supply put \(V^- = 0\) and \(V^+ = V_{DD}\).

Divider from a target gate voltage and input resistance\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{in}\,(V^+ - V^-)}{V_G - V^-} \qquad R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_{in}}{R_1 - R_{in}} \]
\(R_1\)
Upper divider resistor, from \(V^+\) to the gate, in \(\Omega\).
\(R_{in}\)
Wanted input resistance, \(R_1 \parallel R_2\), in \(\Omega\).
\(V^+, V^-\)
The supplies the divider runs between, in V.
\(V_G\)
Wanted gate voltage, in V.
\(R_2\)
Lower divider resistor, from the gate to \(V^-\), in \(\Omega\).

The first formula follows because \(R_{in}/R_1 = R_2/(R_1+R_2) = (V_G - V^-)/(V^+ - V^-)\).

\(I_D\)

\(V_G\)

Diode-connected stack

When a gate is tied to its own drain, \(v_{DS} = v_{GS}\). The saturation test \(v_{DS} \ge v_{GS} - V_{TN}\) then reads \(V_{TN} \ge 0\), so an enhancement device connected this way is always saturated when it conducts. A stack of two shares one current and splits the supply between their gate-source voltages.

Stack of two diode-connected NMOS devices\[ V_{DD} = V_{GS1} + V_{GS2} \qquad K_1(V_{GS1} - V_{TN})^2 = K_2(V_{GS2} - V_{TN})^2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; V_{GS1} - V_{TN} = \frac{V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}}{1 + \sqrt{K_1/K_2}} \]
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{GS1}, V_{GS2}\)
Gate-source voltages of the upper and lower transistor, in V. The output is \(V_o = V_{GS2}\).
\(K_1, K_2\)
Conduction parameters of the two transistors, in A/V\(^2\).
\(V_{TN}\)
Common threshold voltage, in V.
\(V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}\)
The total overdrive to be shared, in V.

Taking square roots of the equal currents gives \(\sqrt{K_1}(V_{GS1} - V_{TN}) = \sqrt{K_2}(V_{GS2} - V_{TN})\), and with \(V_{GS1} + V_{GS2} = V_{DD}\) that is two linear equations. The memo squares instead and solves a quadratic in \(V_{GS1}\); it has one root between \(V_{TN}\) and \(V_{DD}\) (kept) and one above \(V_{DD}\) (rejected).

The PMOS as an LED switch

A switch is deliberately not in saturation. With the gate pulled to ground, a PMOS whose source is at the supply has \(v_{SG} = V_{DD}\), so its edge of saturation is large and it works in non-saturation with a small \(v_{SD}\). The rest of the supply falls across the load. The design uses the non-saturation equation and solves for \(K_p\).

Non-saturation design for a switch\[ R_D = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{SD} - V_\gamma}{I_D} \qquad K_p = \frac{I_D}{2\,(V_{SG} + V_{TP})\,V_{SD} - V_{SD}^{\,2}} \qquad \frac{W}{L} = \frac{2K_p}{k_p'} \]
\(R_D\)
Series resistor that sets the LED current, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{SD}\)
Chosen voltage across the switch when on, in V. It should be small.
\(V_\gamma\)
Cut-in voltage of the LED, in V.
\(I_D\)
LED current, in A.
\(K_p\)
Required conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
\(V_{SG}\)
Source-to-gate voltage with the gate low, equal to \(V_{DD}\) for \(V_I = 0\).
\(k_p'\)
Process conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
Worked example 5.8 designing the switch

A PMOS switches an LED (\(V_\gamma = 1.8\ \text{V}\)) from a 5 V supply. The source is at \(V_{DD}\), the gate is driven by \(V_I\), and the drain feeds \(R_D\) in series with the LED to ground. \(V_{TP} = -0.5\ \text{V}\), \(k_p' = 40\ \mu\text{A/V}^2\), \(\lambda = 0\). Design \(R_D\) and \(W/L\) for \(I_D = 10\ \text{mA}\) at \(V_{SD} = 0.15\ \text{V}\) when \(V_I = 0\).

1
\(R_D = (5 - 0.15 - 1.8)/10\ \text{mA} = 305\ \Omega\).
2
\(V_{SG} = 5\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{SD(sat)} = 4.5\ \text{V}\); 0.15 V is far below it, so non-saturation.
3
\(K_p = 10/[2(4.5)(0.15) - 0.15^2] = 7.533\ \text{mA/V}^2\); \(W/L = 2(7.533)/0.040 = 377\).
\(R_D = 305\ \Omega\), \(W/L = 377\). When on, the switch behaves like a resistor of \(V_{SD}/I_D = 15\ \Omega\).

08Formula sheet

Regions

Cutoff below threshold

Non-saturation: output voltage below the edge

Saturation: output voltage at or above the edge

NMOS

\(v_{DS(sat)} = v_{GS} - V_{TN}\)

Non-sat: \(i_D = K_n[2(v_{GS} - V_{TN})v_{DS} - v_{DS}^2]\)

Sat: \(i_D = K_n(v_{GS} - V_{TN})^2\)

PMOS

\(v_{SD(sat)} = v_{SG} + V_{TP}\)

Non-sat: \(i_D = K_p[2(v_{SG} + V_{TP})v_{SD} - v_{SD}^2]\)

Sat: \(i_D = K_p(v_{SG} + V_{TP})^2\)

Parameters

\(K_n = \frac{k_n'}{2}\frac{W}{L} = \frac{W\mu_n C_{ox}}{2L}\)

\(k_n' = \mu_n C_{ox}\), \(C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}\)

\(\varepsilon_{ox} = 3.9 \times 8.85\times10^{-14}\ \text{F/cm}\)

From one saturated point: \(K_n = I_{D0}/(V_{GS0} - V_{TN})^2\), \(W/L = 2K_n/k_n'\)

With \(\lambda\): multiply the saturation current by \((1 + \lambda v_{DS})\)

Threshold signs

Enhancement: \(V_{TN} \gt 0\), \(V_{TP} \lt 0\)

Depletion: \(V_{TN} \lt 0\), \(V_{TP} \gt 0\)

At zero gate voltage: \(i_D = K_nV_{TN}^2\) or \(K_pV_{TP}^2\)

Common source

\(V_G = \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}V_{DD}\) (no gate current)

NMOS: \(V_{DS} = V_{DD} - I_D R_D\); PMOS: \(V_{SG} = V_{DD} - V_G\)

\(P_T = I_D V_{DS}\)

Saturation fails: \(K_pR_Dv_{SD}^2 - [1 + 2K_pR_D(V_{SG} + V_{TP})]v_{SD} + V_{DD} = 0\); keep the root below the edge

Design

Overdrive \(= \sqrt{I_D/K}\), positive root

\(R_1 = \dfrac{R_{in}(V^+ - V^-)}{V_G - V^-}\), \(R_2 = \dfrac{R_1R_{in}}{R_1 - R_{in}}\)

\(R_1 + R_2 = \dfrac{V^+ - V^-}{fI_D}\), \(\dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \dfrac{V_G - V^-}{V^+ - V^-}\)

Diode-connected stack

\(V_{GS1} - V_{TN} = \dfrac{V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}}{1 + \sqrt{K_1/K_2}}\)

\(V_{GS2} = V_o = V_{DD} - V_{GS1}\)

PMOS LED switch

\(R_D = (V_{DD} - V_{SD} - V_\gamma)/I_D\)

\(K_p = \dfrac{I_D}{2(V_{SG} + V_{TP})V_{SD} - V_{SD}^2}\)
