ELECTRONICS 245 · CHAPTER 2 · DIODE CIRCUITS

Diode circuits: rectifiers, regulators, clippers and logic

How to turn AC into smooth DC, hold a voltage steady with a Zener diode, shape and shift waveforms, and solve any circuit that contains several diodes.

01The power supply chain

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.1

A DC supply built from the mains passes the signal through five stages. Each stage fixes one problem left by the one before it.

Figure 1.1: The transformer sets the voltage. The rectifier turns the AC into pulses of one polarity. The filter capacitor smooths the pulses into a nearly constant voltage with some ripple. The regulator removes the remaining ripple and holds the output steady as the input and load change.

Sections 2 to 4 cover the rectifier and filter, and section 5 the regulator. Sections 6 to 10 then use the same diodes to shape signals and build logic.

Notation

\(V_M\) is the peak value of the sinusoid at the point where it is named, \(V_\gamma\) is the diode cut-in voltage, and diodes follow the piecewise linear model of Week 1 with \(r_f = 0\): a diode is ON with a drop of \(V_\gamma\) or OFF as an open circuit. Take \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\) unless a question says otherwise.

02Half-wave rectifier

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.1 (1)

One diode in series with a load \(R\), driven by a secondary voltage \(v_S = V_M\sin\omega t\). The diode conducts only while the source is high enough to exceed \(V_\gamma\).

SourceDiodeOutput
\(v_S \lt V_\gamma\)OFF\(v_O = 0\) and \(i_D = 0\)
\(v_S \ge V_\gamma\)ON\(v_O = v_S - V_\gamma\) and \(i_D = (v_S - V_\gamma)/R\)
Half-wave rectifier\[ v_{O,\text{peak}} = V_M - V_\gamma \qquad \text{PIV} = V_M \] \[ \omega t_1 = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{V_\gamma}{V_M}\right) \qquad \omega t_2 = 180^\circ - \omega t_1 \qquad \%\text{ conduction} = \frac{\omega t_2 - \omega t_1}{360^\circ}\times100\% \]
\(v_{O,\text{peak}}\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak secondary voltage (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).
\(\text{PIV}\)
Peak inverse voltage, the largest reverse voltage across the OFF diode (V).
\(\omega t_1, \omega t_2\)

The cut-in voltage costs the output a small slice at each end of the half-cycle, so the diode conducts for less than \(180^\circ\) of every cycle.

Worked example 2.1 conduction angle

A half-wave rectifier has a secondary of \(12\ \text{V}\) peak and a silicon diode with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Find the peak output, the PIV and the fraction of each cycle for which the diode conducts.

1
\(v_{O,\text{peak}} = V_M - V_\gamma = 12 - 0.7 = 11.3\ \text{V}\).
2
At the negative peak the diode is OFF and carries the full source voltage, so \(\text{PIV} = V_M = 12\ \text{V}\).
3
\(\omega t_1 = \sin^{-1}(0.7/12) = 3.34^\circ\) and \(\omega t_2 = 180^\circ - 3.34^\circ = 176.66^\circ\).
4
Conduction: \((176.66 - 3.34)/360 = 48.1\%\).
Peak output \(11.3\ \text{V}\), \(\text{PIV} = 12\ \text{V}\), and the diode conducts for \(48.1\%\) of the cycle, just under the ideal \(50\%\).

03Full-wave rectifiers

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.1 (2 and 3)

A full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles, so the output pulses come twice per input cycle.

Centre-tapped

A centre-tapped secondary gives two anti-phase voltages \(v_S\) measured from the tap, which is ground. Diode \(D_1\) conducts in the positive half-cycle and \(D_2\) in the negative one. Both feed one load \(R\) with a single diode drop in the path.

Centre-tapped rectifier\[ v_{O,\text{peak}} = V_M - V_\gamma \qquad \text{PIV} = 2V_M - V_\gamma \]
\(v_{O,\text{peak}}\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak voltage of each half of the secondary, measured from the tap (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).
\(\text{PIV}\)
Peak inverse voltage across the OFF diode (V).

The OFF diode sees the other half of the winding in series with the output, so it must block about twice \(V_M\).

Bridge

A bridge uses four diodes and an ordinary secondary with no tap. In the positive half-cycle \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) conduct in series with the load. In the negative half-cycle \(D_3\) and \(D_4\) conduct. The current always passes through two diodes, so two drops are lost.

Figure 3.1: When the top of the source is positive, current flows through \(D_1\), the load and \(D_2\). When the bottom is positive, it flows through \(D_3\), the load and \(D_4\). Either way it enters the load at the top, so the output never reverses.

Bridge rectifier\[ v_{O,\text{peak}} = V_M - 2V_\gamma \qquad \text{PIV} = V_M - V_\gamma \]
\(v_{O,\text{peak}}\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak voltage of the whole secondary (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).
\(\text{PIV}\)
Peak inverse voltage across each OFF diode (V).

Centre-tapped against bridge

The bridge needs only half the secondary turns for the same output, and its diodes need about half the PIV rating. The price is a second diode drop, which matters in low-voltage supplies.

Worked example 3.1 sizing the transformer and diodes

A rectifier must give a peak output of \(14\ \text{V}\) with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Compare a centre-tapped design and a bridge.

1
Centre-tapped: \(V_M = 14 + 0.7 = 14.7\ \text{V}\) per half. The whole secondary is \(2\times14.7 = 29.4\ \text{V}\) peak.
2
\(\text{PIV} = 2(14.7) - 0.7 = 28.7\ \text{V}\).
3
Bridge: \(V_M = 14 + 2(0.7) = 15.4\ \text{V}\), and \(\text{PIV} = 15.4 - 0.7 = 14.7\ \text{V}\).
4
Secondary voltage ratio, bridge to centre-tapped: \(15.4/29.4 = 0.52\).
Centre-tapped: \(29.4\ \text{V}\) peak secondary and PIV \(28.7\ \text{V}\). Bridge: \(15.4\ \text{V}\) peak secondary and PIV \(14.7\ \text{V}\). The bridge needs about half the secondary voltage, and so about half the turns, and diodes rated for about half the reverse voltage.

04Filter capacitor and ripple

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.1 (4)

A large electrolytic capacitor \(C\) placed in parallel with the load keeps the output up while the diodes are OFF. The diodes recharge it briefly near each peak. Between peaks the capacitor alone feeds the load and its voltage decays:

Capacitor discharge\[ v_O(t') = V_M\exp\!\left(-\frac{t'}{RC}\right) \approx V_M\left(1 - \frac{t'}{RC}\right) \]
\(v_O(t')\)
Output voltage a time \(t'\) after the peak (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(t'\)
Time since the peak (s).
\(R\)
Load resistance (\(\Omega\)).
\(C\)
Filter capacitance (F).

The decay is nearly linear because the ripple is small, which gives a simple formula for the ripple voltage \(V_r = V_M - V_L\), the peak-to-peak difference between the peak and the lowest output.

Ripple voltage\[ V_r \approx \frac{V_M}{f_rRC} \qquad \text{half-wave: } f_r = f,\ V_r = \frac{V_M}{fRC} \qquad \text{full-wave: } f_r = 2f,\ V_r = \frac{V_M}{2fRC} \]
\(V_r\)
Peak-to-peak ripple voltage (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(f_r\)
Ripple frequency (Hz).
\(f\)
Source frequency (Hz).
\(R\)
Load resistance (\(\Omega\)).
\(C\)
Filter capacitance (F).

The discharge time \(T'\) is about one ripple period: \(1/f\) for half-wave and \(1/(2f)\) for full-wave. A full-wave circuit therefore needs half the capacitance for the same ripple.

Watch out

The summary quotes \(f = 60\ \text{Hz}\). South African mains is \(50\ \text{Hz}\), which gives more ripple for the same \(C\). Use the frequency in the question.

Diode currents

The diodes conduct only for a short interval \(\Delta t\) just before each peak. Because they must supply a full cycle's worth of charge in that short time, their current is a narrow spike far larger than the load current.

Conduction interval and diode current\[ \omega\,\Delta t \approx \sqrt{\frac{2V_r}{V_M}} \qquad i_{D,\text{peak}} \approx \frac{V_M}{R}\left(1 + \pi\sqrt{\frac{2V_M}{V_r}}\right) \] \[ i_{D,\text{avg}} \approx \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2V_r}{V_M}}\left(\frac{V_M}{R}\right)\left(1 + \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2V_M}{V_r}}\right) \approx I_L \]
\(\omega\)
Source angular frequency, \(2\pi f\) (rad/s).
\(\Delta t\)
Time the diode conducts each cycle (s).
\(V_r\)
Ripple voltage (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak output voltage (V).
\(i_{D,\text{peak}}\)
Peak (surge) diode current (A).
\(R\)
Load resistance (\(\Omega\)).
\(i_{D,\text{avg}}\)
Average diode current over the cycle (A).
\(I_L\)
Average load current (A).
Key idea

A larger \(C\) reduces ripple, but it also shortens \(\Delta t\) and raises the diode's peak current. Rate the diodes for the surge, not for the load current.

Worked example 4.1 a filtered bridge supply

A bridge rectifier on \(50\ \text{Hz}\) mains has a peak output of \(V_M = 14\ \text{V}\) and feeds a load of \(R = 200\ \Omega\). The ripple must not exceed \(0.7\ \text{V}\). Find \(C\), the conduction interval, and the peak and average diode current.

1
Full-wave, so \(f_r = 2f = 100\ \text{Hz}\): \(C = V_M/(2fRV_r) = 14/(2\times50\times200\times0.7) = 1000\ \mu\text{F}\).
2
\(\omega\Delta t = \sqrt{2(0.7)/14} = 0.316\ \text{rad} = 18.1^\circ\), so \(\Delta t = 0.316/(2\pi\times50) = 1.01\ \text{ms}\).
3
\(\sqrt{2V_M/V_r} = \sqrt{2(14)/0.7} = 6.32\), so \(i_{D,\text{peak}} = (14/200)(1 + \pi\times6.32) = 1.46\ \text{A}\).
4
Average: \(i_{D,\text{avg}} \approx (1/\pi)(0.3162)(0.07)(1 + 0.5\pi\times6.325) = 77\ \text{mA}\). The mean load current is about \((14 - 0.35)/200 = 68\ \text{mA}\).
\(C = 1000\ \mu\text{F}\), \(\Delta t \approx 1.0\ \text{ms}\) of each \(10\ \text{ms}\) pulse period, \(i_{D,\text{peak}} \approx 1.46\ \text{A}\), which is about 21 times the load current. The average formula is only approximate and is about 13% above \(I_L\) here.

Figure 4.1: The dashed line is the input sinusoid of \(15.4\ \text{V}\) peak, so that two drops leave \(14\ \text{V}\). The output follows the rectified peak, then decays slowly. It is a numerical simulation of the circuit with ideal \(0.7\ \text{V}\) diodes, starting from an empty capacitor.

Figure 4.2: The dashed box is the conduction interval \(\Delta t\) before the peak at \(65\ \text{ms}\). The arrow is the ripple \(V_r\). The simulation gives \(V_r = 0.62\ \text{V}\) and \(\Delta t = 0.93\ \text{ms}\). The formulas give \(0.70\ \text{V}\) and \(1.01\ \text{ms}\), so they are slightly cautious. They assume the capacitor discharges for the whole period, but it discharges for the period minus \(\Delta t\).

Watch out

The average-current formula is for the current through the rectifier as a whole. In a full-wave circuit the pulses alternate between two diodes, so each diode carries about half of \(I_L\) on average, while its peak current is unchanged.

05Zener voltage regulators

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.2

A Zener diode in reverse breakdown holds a nearly constant voltage \(V_Z\) across its terminals, whatever current it carries. Connect it in parallel with the load, and feed both through a series resistor \(R_i\). Changes in the input or in the load current then change the current through \(R_i\) and the Zener, but hardly change the output.

Figure 5.1: \(V_{Z0}\) in series with \(r_z\) models the Zener in breakdown. The current \(I_i\) through \(R_i\) divides into \(I_Z\) in the Zener and \(I_L\) in the load.

Regulator node equation\[ I_i = I_Z + I_L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{V_{PS} - V_Z}{R_i} = I_Z + \frac{V_Z}{R_L} \]
\(I_i\)
Current through the series resistor (A).
\(I_Z\)
Zener current (A).
\(I_L\)
Load current (A).
\(V_{PS}\)
Unregulated input voltage (V).
\(V_Z\)
Zener (output) voltage (V).
\(R_i\)
Series resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_L\)
Load resistor (\(\Omega\)).

Worst-case design

A design must work at the extremes of input and load. Two limits apply to the Zener.

LimitRequirement
It must stay in breakdown\(I_Z \ge I_{Z(\min)} \approx 0.1\,I_{Z(\max)}\)
It must not overheat\(P_{Z(\max)} = I_{Z(\max)}V_Z\)
Watch out

The summary says "Transistor/diode must stay in breakdown". The device meant is the Zener diode.

The Zener current is smallest when the input is lowest and the load takes most current, and largest when the input is highest and the load takes least. Each case fixes \(R_i\):

Worst-case design equations\[ R_i = \frac{V_{PS(\min)} - V_Z}{I_{Z(\min)} + I_{L(\max)}} = \frac{V_{PS(\max)} - V_Z}{I_{Z(\max)} + I_{L(\min)}} \] \[ I_{Z(\max)} = \frac{I_{L(\max)}\left[V_{PS(\max)} - V_Z\right] - I_{L(\min)}\left[V_{PS(\min)} - V_Z\right]}{V_{PS(\min)} - 0.9V_Z - 0.1V_{PS(\max)}} \]
\(R_i\)
Series resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{PS(\min)}, V_{PS(\max)}\)
Lowest and highest input voltage (V).
\(V_Z\)
Zener voltage (V).
\(I_{Z(\min)}, I_{Z(\max)}\)
Smallest and largest Zener current in the design (A).
\(I_{L(\min)}, I_{L(\max)}\)
Smallest and largest load current (A).

The second equation comes from setting the two expressions for \(R_i\) equal and using \(I_{Z(\min)} = 0.1\,I_{Z(\max)}\). Find \(I_{Z(\max)}\) first, then \(I_{Z(\min)}\), then \(R_i\).

Worked example 5.1 designing a 5.1 V regulator

A \(5.1\ \text{V}\) Zener regulates a load that draws \(5\) to \(25\ \text{mA}\) from an input that varies between \(10\ \text{V}\) and \(14\ \text{V}\). Find \(R_i\), the Zener power rating, and then check a standard \(160\ \Omega\) resistor.

1
Numerator: \(25\ \text{mA}(14 - 5.1) - 5\ \text{mA}(10 - 5.1) = 0.198\ \text{W}\). Denominator: \(10 - 0.9(5.1) - 0.1(14) = 4.01\ \text{V}\).
2
\(I_{Z(\max)} = 0.198/4.01 = 49.4\ \text{mA}\), so \(I_{Z(\min)} = 4.94\ \text{mA}\).
3
\(R_i = (10 - 5.1)/(4.94\ \text{mA} + 25\ \text{mA}) = 163.7\ \Omega\). The other case gives \((14 - 5.1)/(49.38\ \text{mA} + 5\ \text{mA}) = 163.7\ \Omega\), the same, which checks the algebra.
4
Power: \(P_{Z(\max)} = 49.38\ \text{mA}\times5.1\ \text{V} = 0.252\ \text{W}\).
5
With \(R_i = 160\ \Omega\): \(I_{Z(\min)} = (10 - 5.1)/160 - 25\ \text{mA} = 5.63\ \text{mA}\) and \(I_{Z(\max)} = (14 - 5.1)/160 - 5\ \text{mA} = 50.62\ \text{mA}\).
\(R_i = 163.7\ \Omega\) and a Zener rated above \(0.25\ \text{W}\). The \(160\ \Omega\) resistor keeps \(I_{Z(\min)} = 5.63\ \text{mA}\), above \(0.1\,I_{Z(\max)} = 5.06\ \text{mA}\), at a worst-case power of \(0.258\ \text{W}\). A \(0.5\ \text{W}\) Zener is the sensible choice.

Source and load regulation

A real Zener has a resistance \(r_z = \Delta V_Z/\Delta I_Z\), so its voltage drifts as its current changes. Two figures of merit measure how much of that drift reaches the load.

Regulation figures of merit\[ \text{Source regulation} = \frac{\Delta v_L}{\Delta V_{PS}}\times100\% = \frac{r_z}{R_i + r_z}\times100\% \] \[ \text{Load regulation} = \frac{v_{L,\text{no load}} - v_{L,\text{full load}}}{v_{L,\text{full load}}}\times100\% \]
\(\Delta v_L\)
Change in output voltage (V).
\(\Delta V_{PS}\)
Change in input voltage (V).
\(r_z\)
Zener dynamic resistance (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_i\)
Series resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(v_{L,\text{no load}}\)
Output with \(I_L = 0\) (V).
\(v_{L,\text{full load}}\)
Output at the full load current (V).

Source regulation is a voltage divider between \(R_i\) and \(r_z\). A large \(R_i\) and a small \(r_z\) both help. The first form treats the load as a fixed current, which is how it is used here.

Worked example 5.2 how steady is the output?

A Zener with \(V_{Z0} = 5.0\ \text{V}\) and \(r_z = 8\ \Omega\) is fed through \(R_i = 164\ \Omega\) from \(V_{PS} = 12\ \text{V}\). The load current ranges from \(0\) to \(25\ \text{mA}\). Find the source regulation and the load regulation.

1
Source regulation \(= 8/(164 + 8) = 4.65\%\). A \(4\ \text{V}\) change in \(V_{PS}\) moves the output by \(4\times0.0465 = 0.186\ \text{V}\).
2
The node equation \((V_{PS} - v_L)/R_i = I_L + (v_L - V_{Z0})/r_z\) gives \(v_L = (V_{PS}r_z + V_{Z0}R_i - I_LR_ir_z)/(R_i + r_z)\).
3
No load: \(v_L = (12\times8 + 5\times164)/172 = 5.326\ \text{V}\). Full load: subtract \(0.025\times164\times8/172 = 0.191\ \text{V}\) to get \(5.135\ \text{V}\).
4
Load regulation \(= (5.326 - 5.135)/5.135 = 3.71\%\).
Source regulation \(4.65\%\) and load regulation \(3.71\%\). The Zener current falls from \(40.7\ \text{mA}\) at no load to \(16.9\ \text{mA}\) at full load, which is what moves the output by \(0.191\ \text{V}\).

06Clippers

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.3 (1)

A clipper (or limiter) removes the part of a signal that goes beyond a chosen level and leaves the rest unchanged. A diode in series with a DC bias \(V_B\) conducts only when the input passes \(V_B + V_\gamma\), and from then on holds the output at that level.

A positive clipper tracks \(v_I\) until \(v_I \ge V_B + V_\gamma\), then holds \(v_O = V_B + V_\gamma\). Two opposing shunt branches clip both peaks. This is the double limiter:

Double diode clipper\[ -(V_{B2} + V_\gamma) \le v_O \le V_{B1} + V_\gamma \]
\(v_O\)
Output voltage (V).
\(V_{B1}\)
Bias battery in the branch that limits the positive peak (V).
\(V_{B2}\)
Bias battery in the branch that limits the negative peak (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).

Two back-to-back Zener diodes give symmetric limits of \(\pm(V_Z + V_\gamma)\). One Zener is in breakdown and the other is forward biased, so each limit costs one \(V_Z\) and one \(V_\gamma\).

Figure 6.1: \(D_1\) with \(V_{B1}\) turns on when \(v_O\) reaches \(3.7\ \text{V}\). \(D_2\) with \(V_{B2}\) turns on when \(v_O\) falls to \(-2.7\ \text{V}\). Between those values both diodes are OFF and the output equals the input, a line of slope 1.

Worked example 6.1 clipping a sinusoid

The clipper of Figure 6.1 has \(V_{B1} = 3\ \text{V}\), \(V_{B2} = 2\ \text{V}\) and \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\). The input is \(8\sin\omega t\) volts. Find the output limits and the angles at which clipping starts and ends.

1
Upper limit \(3 + 0.7 = 3.7\ \text{V}\). Lower limit \(-(2 + 0.7) = -2.7\ \text{V}\).
2
Positive clipping starts when \(8\sin\omega t = 3.7\): \(\omega t = \sin^{-1}(3.7/8) = 27.5^\circ\). It ends at \(180^\circ - 27.5^\circ = 152.5^\circ\).
3
Negative clipping starts when \(8\sin\omega t = -2.7\): \(\omega t = 180^\circ + \sin^{-1}(2.7/8) = 199.7^\circ\). It ends at \(360^\circ - 19.7^\circ = 340.3^\circ\).
The output swings between \(-2.7\ \text{V}\) and \(+3.7\ \text{V}\). The top is flat from \(27.5^\circ\) to \(152.5^\circ\) and the bottom from \(199.7^\circ\) to \(340.3^\circ\). The rest follows the input.

07Clampers

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.3 (2)

A clamper (DC restorer) shifts a whole AC waveform up or down by a fixed DC level without changing its shape. It needs a capacitor in series, a diode in shunt and a resistor \(R\) in parallel with the diode.

Take the circuit of Figure 7.1, with the diode anode at the output. On the first negative swing of the input the diode turns ON and charges the capacitor towards the negative peak. After the peak the diode turns OFF. If \(RC \gg T\), the capacitor cannot discharge through \(R\) during a cycle, and it acts as a battery in series with the input.

Clamper\[ v_O(t) = v_I(t) + V_C \qquad V_C = V_M - V_\gamma \]
\(v_O(t)\)
Output voltage (V).
\(v_I(t)\)
Input voltage (V).
\(V_C\)
Steady voltage across the capacitor (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak input voltage (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).

The lowest point of the output is therefore pinned at \(-V_\gamma\), the voltage at which the diode turns ON. Reversing the diode pins the highest point instead and shifts the waveform down.

Figure 7.1: The dashed wave is the input and the teal wave is the output. The waveforms are drawn for the steady state, after the capacitor has charged during the first cycle. The output is the input lifted by \(V_C = 4.3\ \text{V}\).

Watch out

Two things in the summary need care. Its text gives \(V_C = V_M - V_B\) for a clamp with a reference battery \(V_B\), without showing the circuit, and the sign of \(V_B\) depends on how the battery is connected. This page works the circuit with no battery, where \(V_C = V_M - V_\gamma\). Its diagram description shows a diode pointing down to ground but an output shifted below zero. A diode with its anode at the output clamps the negative peaks and lifts the waveform. To push the waveform below zero, reverse the diode.

Worked example 7.1 restoring a DC level

A \(5\ \text{V}\) peak sinusoid drives the clamper of Figure 7.1 with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\) and \(RC \gg T\). Find the capacitor voltage and the range of the output. Then say what happens when the diode is reversed.

1
\(V_C = V_M - V_\gamma = 5 - 0.7 = 4.3\ \text{V}\).
2
\(v_O = v_I + 4.3\), so the minimum is \(-5 + 4.3 = -0.7\ \text{V}\), which is \(-V_\gamma\) as expected, and the maximum is \(5 + 4.3 = 9.3\ \text{V}\).
3
Reversed diode: the capacitor charges on positive peaks to \(V_M - V_\gamma\) with the opposite sign, so \(v_O = v_I - 4.3\) and the output runs from \(-9.3\ \text{V}\) to \(+0.7\ \text{V}\).
Output between \(-0.7\ \text{V}\) and \(9.3\ \text{V}\), a peak-to-peak swing of \(10\ \text{V}\), the same as the input. Reversing the diode gives \(-9.3\ \text{V}\) to \(+0.7\ \text{V}\).

08Multiple-diode circuits

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.4 (1)

With several diodes you cannot tell by inspection which are ON. The method is to guess, solve the resulting linear circuit and check the guess.

  1. Assume a state for every diode. ON means replace it by \(V_\gamma\). OFF means replace it by an open circuit. With \(N\) diodes there are \(2^N\) possible sets of states.
  2. Solve the linear circuit with KVL and KCL for all unknown diode currents \(i_D\) and voltages \(v_D\).
  3. Check the assumptions. Every ON diode must have \(i_D \gt 0\). Every OFF diode must have \(v_D \lt V_\gamma\).
  4. If any check fails, change the assumed states and repeat.

A good first guess is to turn ON the diode with the largest forward drive, because when two diodes compete for the same node, the one that turns on first limits the voltage so the other cannot.

Figure 8.1: Node A is fed through \(R\) from \(V_S\). \(D_1\) with \(V_1\) would hold A at \(V_1 + V_\gamma\). \(D_2\) with \(V_2\) would hold it at \(V_2 + V_\gamma\).

Worked example 8.1 which diode conducts?

In Figure 8.1, \(V_S = 10\ \text{V}\), \(R = 1\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_1 = 2\ \text{V}\), \(V_2 = 6\ \text{V}\) and \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\). Find the voltage at A and the current in each diode.

1
Both OFF: no current flows through \(R\), so A would sit at \(10\ \text{V}\). Then \(D_1\) would have \(10 - 2 = 8\ \text{V}\) across it, more than \(V_\gamma\). The guess fails.
2
Both ON would need A at \(2.7\ \text{V}\) and \(6.7\ \text{V}\) at once. Impossible.
3
Try \(D_1\) ON and \(D_2\) OFF. A \(= V_1 + V_\gamma = 2.7\ \text{V}\), and \(i_{D1} = (10 - 2.7)/1000 = 7.3\ \text{mA}\), which is positive.
4
Check \(D_2\): its forward voltage is \(2.7 - 6 = -3.3\ \text{V}\), less than \(V_\gamma\), so it is OFF as assumed.
\(v_A = 2.7\ \text{V}\), \(i_{D1} = 7.3\ \text{mA}\) and \(i_{D2} = 0\). The low-voltage branch wins, and it keeps the higher one OFF. This circuit is a bottom clipper with two levels.

The OR form holds when the highest input is above \(V_\gamma\), and the AND form when the lowest is below \(5\ \text{V} - V_\gamma\). The summary gives the Low and High cases. The general forms follow from the same diode model.

Key idea

Every diode gate shifts the signal level by \(V_\gamma\): Highs fall by \(0.7\ \text{V}\) through an OR gate, and Lows rise by \(0.7\ \text{V}\) through an AND gate. Cascade several and the levels drift until they are no longer recognisable. This limits diode logic without active restoring amplifiers.

Worked example 9.1 level degradation

A High of \(5\ \text{V}\) passes through three diode OR gates in series, and a Low of \(0\ \text{V}\) passes through three AND gates in series. Take \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
OR chain: each gate subtracts \(0.7\ \text{V}\) from the High, so the levels are \(4.3\ \text{V}\), \(3.6\ \text{V}\) and \(2.9\ \text{V}\).
2
AND chain: each gate adds \(0.7\ \text{V}\) to the Low, so the levels are \(0.7\ \text{V}\), \(1.4\ \text{V}\) and \(2.1\ \text{V}\).
After three stages the High has fallen to \(2.9\ \text{V}\) and the Low has risen to \(2.1\ \text{V}\). The noise margin to the middle of the range shrinks with every stage, so a restoring amplifier is needed after a few gates.

10Voltage doubler and optoisolator

Reading Chapter 2 summary, section 2.5

A voltage doubler uses two diodes and two capacitors to produce a DC output of about twice the peak input.

Figure 10.1: On the negative half-cycle \(D_2\) conducts and charges \(C_1\) to about \(V_M\), with the left plate positive. On the positive half-cycle \(D_2\) is OFF. The source voltage now adds to the voltage on \(C_1\), and \(D_1\) charges \(C_2\) to about \(2V_M\).

Voltage doubler\[ V_{C1} \approx V_M \qquad V_O \approx 2V_M \qquad \text{with finite } V_\gamma:\ V_{C1} = V_M - V_\gamma,\quad V_O = 2(V_M - V_\gamma) \]
\(V_{C1}\)
Voltage on \(C_1\) (V).
\(V_M\)
Peak source voltage (V).
\(V_O\)
Output voltage across \(C_2\) (V).
\(V_\gamma\)
Diode cut-in voltage (V).

The summary gives the ideal result. The finite-\(V_\gamma\) form follows because \(C_1\) loses one diode drop when it charges and \(C_2\) loses another.

An optoisolator pairs an input LED with an output photodiode in one package. The signal travels as light, so the two sides share no electrical connection and can be isolated by more than \(1\ \text{kV}\).

Worked example 10.1 doubling a 12 V peak supply

A doubler is fed from a secondary of \(12\ \text{V}\) peak. Find the output with ideal diodes and with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
Ideal: \(V_O = 2V_M = 24\ \text{V}\).
2
With \(V_\gamma\): \(V_{C1} = 12 - 0.7 = 11.3\ \text{V}\), and \(V_O = 2(12 - 0.7) = 22.6\ \text{V}\).
\(24\ \text{V}\) ideally and \(22.6\ \text{V}\) with silicon diodes. Under load, the output also sags between charging pulses, as in a filtered rectifier.

11Practice problems

Problem 11.1 a filtered half-wave supply

A half-wave rectifier has a \(10\ \text{V}\) peak secondary, \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\), \(R = 470\ \Omega\), \(C = 470\ \mu\text{F}\) and \(f = 50\ \text{Hz}\). Find the ripple, the lowest output, the conduction interval and the peak diode current.

1
\(V_M = 10 - 0.7 = 9.3\ \text{V}\).
2
Half-wave: \(V_r = V_M/(fRC) = 9.3/(50\times470\times470\times10^{-6}) = 0.842\ \text{V}\).
3
Lowest output: \(V_L = 9.3 - 0.842 = 8.46\ \text{V}\).
4
\(\omega\Delta t = \sqrt{2(0.842)/9.3} = 0.426\ \text{rad} = 24.4^\circ\), so \(\Delta t = 1.35\ \text{ms}\).
5
\(\sqrt{2V_M/V_r} = 4.70\), so \(i_{D,\text{peak}} = (9.3/470)(1 + \pi\times4.70) = 0.312\ \text{A}\).
\(V_r = 0.84\ \text{V}\), \(V_L = 8.46\ \text{V}\), \(\Delta t = 1.35\ \text{ms}\) and \(i_{D,\text{peak}} = 0.31\ \text{A}\), about 16 times the load current of \(9.3/470 = 19.8\ \text{mA}\).
Problem 11.2 designing a 9.1 V regulator

A \(9.1\ \text{V}\) Zener regulates a load of \(10\) to \(40\ \text{mA}\) from an input of \(14\) to \(18\ \text{V}\). Find \(I_{Z(\max)}\), \(I_{Z(\min)}\), \(R_i\) and the power the Zener must dissipate.

1
Numerator: \(40\ \text{mA}(18 - 9.1) - 10\ \text{mA}(14 - 9.1) = 0.307\ \text{W}\). Denominator: \(14 - 0.9(9.1) - 0.1(18) = 4.01\ \text{V}\).
2
\(I_{Z(\max)} = 76.6\ \text{mA}\) and \(I_{Z(\min)} = 7.66\ \text{mA}\).
3
\(R_i = (14 - 9.1)/(7.656\ \text{mA} + 40\ \text{mA}) = 102.8\ \Omega\). Check: \((18 - 9.1)/(76.56\ \text{mA} + 10\ \text{mA}) = 102.8\ \Omega\).
4
\(P_{Z(\max)} = 76.56\ \text{mA}\times9.1\ \text{V} = 0.70\ \text{W}\).
\(I_{Z(\max)} = 76.6\ \text{mA}\), \(I_{Z(\min)} = 7.66\ \text{mA}\), \(R_i = 102.8\ \Omega\) and \(P_{Z(\max)} = 0.70\ \text{W}\), so a \(1\ \text{W}\) Zener is needed.
Problem 11.3 a Zener clipper

Two back-to-back \(3.3\ \text{V}\) Zener diodes with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\) clip a \(10\ \text{V}\) peak sinusoid. Find the output limits, and the fraction of each half-cycle for which the output is clipped.

1
Limits: \(\pm(V_Z + V_\gamma) = \pm4.0\ \text{V}\).
2
Clipping starts at \(\sin^{-1}(4/10) = 23.6^\circ\) and ends at \(180^\circ - 23.6^\circ = 156.4^\circ\).
3
Clipped part of the half-cycle: \((156.4 - 23.6)/180 = 73.8\%\).
The output lies between \(-4.0\ \text{V}\) and \(+4.0\ \text{V}\), and each peak is flattened for \(73.8\%\) of its half-cycle.
Problem 11.4 gates with unequal inputs

Find \(V_O\) for a two-input diode AND gate (pull-up to \(5\ \text{V}\)) and a two-input diode OR gate when the inputs are \(V_1 = 5\ \text{V}\) and \(V_2 = 3.3\ \text{V}\). Use \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
AND: the diode on the lower input, \(3.3\ \text{V}\), conducts, so \(V_O = 3.3 + 0.7 = 4.0\ \text{V}\). The diode on \(5\ \text{V}\) is OFF.
2
OR: the diode on the higher input, \(5\ \text{V}\), conducts, so \(V_O = 5 - 0.7 = 4.3\ \text{V}\).
AND gives \(4.0\ \text{V}\) and OR gives \(4.3\ \text{V}\). Intermediate inputs make the AND output a \(V_\gamma\) above the lower input, not exactly a Low.
Problem 11.5 centre-tapped or bridge?

A supply needs a \(9\ \text{V}\) peak output. Find the secondary peak voltage and the PIV for a centre-tapped rectifier and for a bridge, with \(V_\gamma = 0.7\ \text{V}\).

1
Centre-tapped: \(V_M = 9 + 0.7 = 9.7\ \text{V}\) per half, and \(\text{PIV} = 2(9.7) - 0.7 = 18.7\ \text{V}\).
2
Bridge: \(V_M = 9 + 1.4 = 10.4\ \text{V}\), and \(\text{PIV} = 10.4 - 0.7 = 9.7\ \text{V}\).
Centre-tapped: \(9.7\ \text{V}\) per half and PIV \(18.7\ \text{V}\). Bridge: \(10.4\ \text{V}\) across the whole secondary and PIV \(9.7\ \text{V}\).

12Formula sheet

Rectifiers

Half-wave: \(v_{O,\text{pk}} = V_M - V_\gamma\), \(\text{PIV} = V_M\)

Centre-tapped: \(v_{O,\text{pk}} = V_M - V_\gamma\), \(\text{PIV} = 2V_M - V_\gamma\)

Bridge: \(v_{O,\text{pk}} = V_M - 2V_\gamma\), \(\text{PIV} = V_M - V_\gamma\)

Conduction: \(\omega t_1 = \sin^{-1}(V_\gamma/V_M)\), \(\omega t_2 = 180^\circ - \omega t_1\)

Filter

\(V_r \approx V_M/(f_rRC)\)

Half-wave \(f_r = f\), full-wave \(f_r = 2f\)

\(\omega\Delta t \approx \sqrt{2V_r/V_M}\)

\(i_{D,\text{pk}} \approx (V_M/R)\left(1 + \pi\sqrt{2V_M/V_r}\right)\)

Zener regulator

\(I_i = I_Z + I_L\)

\(I_{Z(\min)} \approx 0.1\,I_{Z(\max)}\), \(P_{Z(\max)} = I_{Z(\max)}V_Z\)

\(R_i = \dfrac{V_{PS(\min)} - V_Z}{I_{Z(\min)} + I_{L(\max)}}\)

\[ I_{Z(\max)} = \dfrac{I_{L(\max)}[V_{PS(\max)} - V_Z] - I_{L(\min)}[V_{PS(\min)} - V_Z]}{V_{PS(\min)} - 0.9V_Z - 0.1V_{PS(\max)}} \]

Source regulation \(= r_z/(R_i + r_z)\)

Clippers and clampers

\(-(V_{B2} + V_\gamma) \le v_O \le V_{B1} + V_\gamma\)

Zener clipper: \(\pm(V_Z + V_\gamma)\)

Clamper: \(v_O = v_I + V_C\), \(V_C = V_M - V_\gamma\), \(RC \gg T\)

Multiple diodes and logic

Assume ON or OFF, solve, check \(i_D \gt 0\) (ON) and \(v_D \lt V_\gamma\) (OFF)

OR: \(V_O = \max(V_1, V_2) - V_\gamma\)

AND: \(V_O = \min(V_1, V_2) + V_\gamma\)

Other

Doubler: \(V_O \approx 2V_M\), or \(2(V_M - V_\gamma)\) with finite \(V_\gamma\)

Optoisolator: LED to photodiode, isolation above \(1\ \text{kV}\)
