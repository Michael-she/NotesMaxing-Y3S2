Biasing, bias stability and multistage circuits

Why a single base resistor gives a Q-point that depends on \(\beta\), how a voltage divider and an emitter resistor fix that, how to design bias-stable circuits with one or two supplies or a current mirror, and how to analyse and design circuits with two transistors.

01Single-resistor biasing

Reading Neamen 5.4.1; Lecture 18, slides 2 to 5

The simplest bias circuit needs one supply and one base resistor \(R_B\) from \(V_{CC}\) to the base. A coupling capacitor \(C_C\) passes the signal from the source but blocks DC, so the source does not disturb the bias.

Figure 1.1: For DC analysis the capacitor is an open circuit, so only \(V_{CC}\), \(R_B\), the transistor and \(R_C\) matter.

Single-resistor design\[ R_C = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CEQ}}{I_{CQ}} \qquad I_{BQ} = \frac{I_{CQ}}{\beta} \qquad R_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE(on)}}{I_{BQ}} \]
\(R_C\)
Collector resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{CC}\)
Supply voltage (V).
\(V_{CEQ}\)
Wanted quiescent collector-emitter voltage (V).
\(I_{CQ}\)
Wanted quiescent collector current (A).
\(I_{BQ}\)
Base current needed for that collector current (A).
\(\beta\)
Nominal current gain.
\(R_B\)
Base resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.

Design Figure 1.1 for \(V_{CC} = 9\ \text{V}\), \(I_{CQ} = 1.5\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CEQ} = 4.5\ \text{V}\) with a nominal \(\beta = 120\). Then find the Q-point if \(\beta\) is anywhere from 60 to 180.

1
\(R_C = (9 - 4.5)/1.5\ \text{mA} = 3\ \text{k}\Omega\).
2
\(I_{BQ} = 1.5\ \text{mA}/120 = 12.5\ \mu\text{A}\); \(R_B = (9 - 0.7)/12.5\ \mu\text{A} = 664\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\(R_B\) fixes \(I_B = 12.5\ \mu\text{A}\) whatever \(\beta\) is, so \(I_C = \beta(12.5\ \mu\text{A})\) and \(V_{CE} = 9 - 3I_C\):

Figure 1.2: The load line does not move; the Q-point slides almost from one end of it to the other as \(\beta\) changes. That is unacceptable when transistors are only specified as "\(\beta\) between 60 and 180".

02Voltage-divider biasing

Reading Neamen 5.4.2; Lecture 18, slides 6 to 11

Two resistors \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) set the base voltage, and an emitter resistor \(R_E\) is added. To analyse it, replace the divider with its Thevenin equivalent seen from the base.

Figure 2.1: \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) with \(V_{CC}\) become one source \(V_{TH}\) in series with \(R_{TH}\). The collector side is unchanged.

Worked example 2.1 divider bias and a change in β

In Figure 2.1, \(V_{CC} = 12\ \text{V}\), \(R_1 = 47\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 3\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_E = 0.5\ \text{k}\Omega\). Find the Q-point for \(\beta = 50\), 100 and 150.

1
\(R_{TH} = 47 \parallel 10 = 8.25\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(V_{TH} = \frac{10}{57}(12) = 2.105\ \text{V}\).
2
For \(\beta = 100\): \(I_{BQ} = \dfrac{2.105 - 0.7}{8.25\ \text{k} + (101)(0.5\ \text{k})} = 23.9\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_{CQ} = 2.39\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ} = 12 - (2.39)(3) - (2.42)(0.5) = 3.62\ \text{V}\).
3
Repeating for the other values:
\(\beta\)\(I_{BQ}\)\(I_{CQ}\)\(V_{CEQ}\)
5041.6 µA2.08 mA4.69 V
10023.9 µA2.39 mA3.62 V
15016.8 µA2.52 mA3.18 V
A 3:1 range in \(\beta\) now moves \(I_{CQ}\) only from 2.08 mA to 2.52 mA (about ±10 %), against 3:1 for the single resistor.
Intuition

The emitter resistor is negative feedback. If \(\beta\) rises and \(I_C\) tries to rise, the voltage across \(R_E\) rises, which leaves less of \(V_{TH}\) for the base loop and pushes \(I_B\) down again.

03Bias stability

Reading Neamen 5.4.2; Lecture 18, slides 12 to 17

From the divider equations,

\[ I_{CQ} = \frac{\beta(V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)})}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E} \]

If \(R_{TH} \ll (1+\beta)R_E\), the \(R_{TH}\) term can be dropped, and with \(\beta/(1+\beta) \approx 1\) the result no longer contains \(\beta\) at all:

Bias-stable collector current\[ I_{CQ} \approx \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE(on)}}{R_E} \qquad \text{when } R_{TH} \ll (1+\beta)R_E \]
\(I_{CQ}\)
Quiescent collector current (A).
\(V_{TH}\)
Thevenin voltage of the divider (V).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_{TH}\)
Thevenin resistance of the divider (\(\Omega\)).
\(\beta\)
Current gain.

"Much smaller" is made concrete by the general rule for bias stability:

Design rule for bias stability\[ R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E \]
\(R_{TH}\)
Thevenin resistance to design the divider for (\(\Omega\)).
\(\beta\)
Nominal current gain.
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor (\(\Omega\)).

Making \(R_{TH}\) smaller still would improve stability further, but a small \(R_{TH}\) means small divider resistors, which waste supply current and load the signal source. The factor 0.1 is the compromise.

Watch out

The Tutorial 8 memo applies the rule as \(R_{TH} = 0.1\beta R_E\) (for example \(0.1 \times 125 \times 200 = 2.5\ \text{k}\Omega\) in Question 4), while the lecture slides use \(0.1(1+\beta)R_E\). For \(\beta \ge 100\) the two differ by 1 % or less, so the final resistor values hardly change. This page follows the slides; state which form you use in a test.

Design for the centre of the load line

A design method that works every time:

Key idea

1. Find \(I_{CQ}\) and \(V_{CEQ}\) from the specification (for "centre of the load line", \(V_{CEQ}\) is half the total supply).
2. Set \(R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E\).
3. Write the base loop to get \(V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E\).
4. Use \(V_{TH} = \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}V_{CC} = \frac{R_{TH}}{R_1}V_{CC}\) to get \(R_1 = R_{TH}V_{CC}/V_{TH}\), then \(R_2 = R_{TH}V_{CC}/(V_{CC} - V_{TH})\).

Worked example 3.1 Q-point in the centre of the load line

In Figure 2.1, \(V_{CC} = 9\ \text{V}\), \(R_C = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_E = 0.3\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(\beta = 120\). Design \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) for a bias-stable Q-point in the centre of the load line.

1
Centre: \(V_{CEQ} = 4.5\ \text{V}\). From \(V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ}\left(R_C + \frac{121}{120}R_E\right)\): \(I_{CQ} = 4.5/1.8025\ \text{k}\Omega = 2.497\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{BQ} = 20.8\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
\(R_{TH} = 0.1(121)(300) = 3.63\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\[ V_{TH} = (20.8\ \mu\text{A})(3.63\ \text{k}) + 0.7 + (2.518\ \text{mA})(0.3\ \text{k}) = 1.531\ \text{V} \]
4
\(R_1 = (3.63)(9)/1.531 = 21.3\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(R_2 = (3.63)(9)/(9 - 1.531) = 4.37\ \text{k}\Omega\). Check: \(21.3 \parallel 4.37 = 3.63\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_1 = 21.3\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 4.37\ \text{k}\Omega\), giving \(I_{CQ} = 2.50\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CEQ} = 4.5\ \text{V}\).

Design for a given Q-point

Worked example 3.2 specified current and voltage

In Figure 2.1, \(V_{CC} = 10\ \text{V}\), \(R_E = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(\beta = 100\). Design a bias-stable circuit with \(I_{CQ} = 0.6\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CEQ} = 4\ \text{V}\), then check the spread for \(50 \le \beta \le 200\).

1
\(R_{TH} = 0.1(101)(2\ \text{k}) = 20.2\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(I_{BQ} = 6\ \mu\text{A}\); \(I_{EQ} = 0.606\ \text{mA}\).
2
Output loop: \(R_C = \dfrac{10 - 4 - (0.606)(2)}{0.6\ \text{mA}} = 7.98\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\(V_{TH} = (6\ \mu\text{A})(20.2\ \text{k}) + 0.7 + (0.606)(2) = 2.033\ \text{V}\).
4
\(R_1 = (20.2)(10)/2.033 = 99.4\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(R_2 = (20.2)(10)/(10 - 2.033) = 25.4\ \text{k}\Omega\).
5
With these resistors, \(I_{CQ} = 0.545\ \text{mA}\) at \(\beta = 50\) and \(0.632\ \text{mA}\) at \(\beta = 200\).
\(R_C = 7.98\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_1 = 99.4\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 25.4\ \text{k}\Omega\). A 4:1 range in \(\beta\) changes \(I_{CQ}\) by only −9 % to +5 %.

Figure 3.1: Computed from the exact equations. With a single base resistor (amber), \(I_C\) is proportional to \(\beta\). The divider of worked example 2.1 (dark), with \(R_{TH}\) about 0.16 of \((1+\beta)R_E\), is much flatter. The bias-stable design of worked example 3.2 (teal) is flattest of all.

04Positive and negative supplies

Reading Neamen 5.4.3; Lecture 19, slides 2 to 5

With split supplies the divider runs from \(V^+\) to \(V^-\), and \(R_E\) returns to \(V^-\). The Thevenin voltage is measured from ground, so it carries an offset:

Divider between two supplies\[ V_{TH} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}(V^+ - V^-) + V^- \qquad V_{TH} = I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{BE(on)} + I_{EQ}R_E + V^- \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Thevenin voltage at the base, relative to ground (V).
\(R_2\)
Divider resistor from the base to \(V^-\) (\(\Omega\)).
\(R_1\)
Divider resistor from \(V^+\) to the base (\(\Omega\)).
\(V^+\)
Positive supply (V).
\(V^-\)
Negative supply (V).
\(I_{BQ}\)
Quiescent base current (A).
\(R_{TH}\)
\(R_1 \parallel R_2\) (\(\Omega\)).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(I_{EQ}\)
Quiescent emitter current (A).
\(R_E\)
Emitter resistor (\(\Omega\)).

A useful design target with split supplies is a quiescent output of 0 V, so that the next stage can be connected without a coupling capacitor.

Figure 4.1: Both the divider and the emitter resistor return to \(V^- = -6\ \text{V}\), so the base can sit near ground while the transistor stays forward-active.

Worked example 4.1 zero quiescent output

Design Figure 4.1 (\(\beta = 120\)) to be bias stable with \(V_O = 0\) at the Q-point. Give \(I_{CQ}\), \(V_{CEQ}\), \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

1
\(V_O = 0\) means 6 V across \(R_C\): \(I_{CQ} = 6/8\ \text{k}\Omega = 0.75\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{EQ} = \frac{121}{120}(0.75) = 0.756\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{BQ} = 6.25\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
\(V_{CEQ} = 12 - (0.75)(8) - (0.756)(1.5) = 4.87\ \text{V}\).
3
\(R_{TH} = 0.1(121)(1.5\ \text{k}) = 18.15\ \text{k}\Omega\).
4
\(V_{TH} = (6.25\ \mu\text{A})(18.15\ \text{k}) + 0.7 + (0.756)(1.5) - 6 = -4.052\ \text{V}\).
5
\(\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = \frac{V_{TH} + 6}{12} = 0.1623\), and \(R_{TH} = R_1\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\), so \(R_1 = 18.15/0.1623 = 111.8\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_2 = 18.15/(1 - 0.1623) = 21.7\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(I_{CQ} = 0.75\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ} = 4.87\ \text{V}\), \(R_1 = 112\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 21.7\ \text{k}\Omega\).

05Integrated circuit biasing

Reading Neamen 5.4.4; Lecture 19, slides 6 to 11

Resistors take up a lot of space on a chip, so integrated circuits bias transistors with a constant current source made from other transistors. The basic version is the current mirror: \(Q_1\) has its base and collector joined ("diode connected"), \(Q_2\) shares its base-emitter voltage, and so \(Q_2\) carries the same collector current as \(Q_1\).

Figure 5.1: \(R_1\) from ground to \(V^-\) through \(Q_1\) sets the reference current \(I_1\). \(Q_2\) has the same \(V_{BE}\) as \(Q_1\), so it copies the current as \(I_Q\), which then flows through \(Q_O\) whatever \(R_C\) is.

Current mirror\[ I_1 = \frac{-V_{BE(on)} - V^-}{R_1} \qquad I_1 = I_Q\left(1 + \frac{2}{\beta}\right) \]
\(I_1\)
Reference current through \(R_1\) (A).
\(V_{BE(on)}\)
0.7 V.
\(V^-\)
Negative supply (V).
\(R_1\)
Reference resistor, from ground (0 V) to the joined base-collector of \(Q_1\) (\(\Omega\)).
\(I_Q\)
Mirrored current, the collector current of \(Q_2\) (A).
\(\beta\)
Current gain of \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\).

The second equation is KCL at the joined node: \(I_1 = I_{C1} + I_{B1} + I_{B2}\). Identical transistors at the same temperature with the same \(V_{BE}\) have \(I_{C1} = I_{C2}\) and \(I_{B1} = I_{B2}\), so \(I_1 = I_{C2} + 2I_{C2}/\beta\). Both mirror transistors must stay forward-active for this to hold.

Worked example 5.1 designing with a mirror

In Figure 5.1, \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\), \(V^- = -5\ \text{V}\), the base of \(Q_O\) is grounded (\(R_B = 0\)) and \(\beta = 80\) for every transistor. Design for \(I_C(Q_O) = 0.2\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{CE}(Q_O) = 3\ \text{V}\). Find \(I_Q\) and \(I_1\).

1
The base of \(Q_O\) is at 0 V, so \(V_{E,O} = -0.7\ \text{V}\) and \(V_{C,O} = -0.7 + 3 = 2.3\ \text{V}\).
2
\(R_C = (5 - 2.3)/0.2\ \text{mA} = 13.5\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\(I_Q\) is the emitter current of \(Q_O\): \(I_Q = \frac{81}{80}(0.2) = 0.2025\ \text{mA}\).
4
\(I_1 = (1 + 2/80)(0.2025) = 0.2076\ \text{mA}\); \(R_1 = (0 - 0.7 + 5)/0.2076\ \text{mA} = 20.7\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_C = 13.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_1 = 20.7\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(I_Q = 0.203\ \text{mA}\), \(I_1 = 0.208\ \text{mA}\).

06Multistage circuits

Reading Neamen 5.5; Lecture 20

Most amplifiers use more than one transistor. When stages are connected directly (no coupling capacitor), the DC bias of one stage depends on the next: the base current of the second transistor is drawn from the collector node of the first.

Analysing a two-stage circuit

Figure 6.1: The collector of \(Q_1\) drives the base of \(Q_2\). The current in \(R_{C1}\) plus the base current flowing out of \(Q_2\) equals \(I_{C1}\).

Key idea

Work from the input stage outwards. Solve \(Q_1\)'s base loop first (its base current does not depend on \(Q_2\)), then write KCL at the node joining the two stages, using \(Q_2\)'s base-emitter loop to express \(I_{B2}\) in terms of that node voltage.

Worked example 6.1 DC analysis of Figure 6.1

In Figure 6.1, \(R_1 = 120\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 60\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{C1} = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{E1} = 2.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{E2} = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{C2} = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(\beta = 120\) for both. Find all node voltages and currents.

1
\(R_{TH} = 120 \parallel 60 = 40\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(V_{TH} = \frac{60}{180}(10) - 5 = -1.667\ \text{V}\).
2
\(Q_1\) base loop: \(I_{B1} = \dfrac{-1.667 - 0.7 + 5}{40\ \text{k} + (121)(2.5\ \text{k})} = 7.69\ \mu\text{A}\); \(I_{C1} = 0.923\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{E1} = 0.930\ \text{mA}\).
3
KCL at \(V_{C1}\): \(\dfrac{5 - V_{C1}}{R_{C1}} + I_{B2} = I_{C1}\), with \(I_{B2} = \dfrac{5 - (V_{C1} + 0.7)}{(121)R_{E2}}\). Solving gives \(V_{C1} = 1.374\ \text{V}\).
4
\(V_{E2} = 1.374 + 0.7 = 2.074\ \text{V}\); \(I_{E2} = (5 - 2.074)/1.5\ \text{k} = 1.951\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{C2} = 1.935\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{B2} = 16.1\ \mu\text{A}\).
5
\(V_{E1} = (0.930)(2.5) - 5 = -2.674\ \text{V}\); \(V_{C2} = (1.935)(2) - 5 = -1.131\ \text{V}\).
6
\(V_{CE1} = 1.374 - (-2.674) = 4.05\ \text{V}\); \(V_{EC2} = 2.074 - (-1.131) = 3.20\ \text{V}\). Both transistors are forward-active.
\(I_{C1} = 0.923\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CE1} = 4.05\ \text{V}\); \(I_{C2} = 1.94\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{EC2} = 3.20\ \text{V}\); \(V_{C1} = 1.37\ \text{V}\).

Designing a two-stage circuit

To design, fix the voltages you want and work along the chain: \(V_{E1}\) from the first stage's bias, then \(V_{C1}\) from the wanted \(V_{CE1}\), then \(V_{E2}\), \(I_{E2}\) and finally the resistors.

Worked example 6.2 new collector resistors

Keep everything in worked example 6.1 except \(R_{C1}\) and \(R_{C2}\). Choose them so that \(V_{CE1} = 3\ \text{V}\) and \(V_{EC2} = 3\ \text{V}\).

1
\(Q_1\)'s bias is unchanged: \(I_{C1} = 0.923\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{E1} = -2.674\ \text{V}\). So \(V_{C1} = -2.674 + 3 = 0.326\ \text{V}\).
2
\(V_{E2} = 1.026\ \text{V}\); \(I_{E2} = (5 - 1.026)/1.5\ \text{k} = 2.649\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{C2} = 2.628\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{B2} = 21.9\ \mu\text{A}\).
3
Current in \(R_{C1}\): \(0.923 - 0.022 = 0.901\ \text{mA}\), so \(R_{C1} = (5 - 0.326)/0.901\ \text{mA} = 5.19\ \text{k}\Omega\).
4
\(V_{C2} = 1.026 - 3 = -1.974\ \text{V}\), so \(R_{C2} = (-1.974 + 5)/2.628\ \text{mA} = 1.15\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_{C1} = 5.19\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_{C2} = 1.15\ \text{k}\Omega\).

The cascode

A cascode stacks a second transistor on top of a common-emitter stage. In the full amplifier \(Q_1\) receives the signal at its base, and \(Q_2\)'s base is held at AC ground by a bypass capacitor \(C_B\) (left out of Figure 6.2, which shows only the DC circuit). Both transistors carry essentially the same current. A three-resistor divider sets both base voltages.

Figure 6.2: \(R_3\) sets the base of \(Q_1\), \(R_2\) the step up to the base of \(Q_2\), and \(R_1\) takes the rest of \(V^+\). \(Q_2\)'s emitter is \(Q_1\)'s collector.

Worked example 6.3 cascode design

Design Figure 6.2 for \(V^+ = 12\ \text{V}\), \(I_{C1} \approx I_{C2} \approx 1\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CE1} = V_{CE2} = 3.5\ \text{V}\), \(V_{R_E} = 1\ \text{V}\), and a divider current of 0.1 mA. Neglect base currents.

1
\(R_1 + R_2 + R_3 = 12\ \text{V}/0.1\ \text{mA} = 120\ \text{k}\Omega\).
2
\(V_{B1} = V_{R_E} + V_{BE(on)} = 1.7\ \text{V}\), so \(R_3 = 1.7/0.1\ \text{mA} = 17\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\(V_{B2} = V_{R_E} + V_{CE1} + V_{BE(on)} = 5.2\ \text{V}\), so \(R_2 = (5.2 - 1.7)/0.1\ \text{mA} = 35\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_1 = 120 - 35 - 17 = 68\ \text{k}\Omega\).
4
\(R_E = 1\ \text{V}/1\ \text{mA} = 1\ \text{k}\Omega\). \(V_{C2} = 1 + 3.5 + 3.5 = 8\ \text{V}\), so \(R_C = (12 - 8)/1\ \text{mA} = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_1 = 68\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 35\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_3 = 17\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_E = 1\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_C = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\).
Watch out

Neglecting base current is only reasonable because the divider current (0.1 mA) is ten times the base current at \(\beta = 100\) (0.01 mA). With a smaller divider current the base voltages would sag below the design values.

Problem 7.1 pnp divider bias and a change in β

A pnp transistor has its emitter connected to \(V^+ = 3.3\ \text{V}\) through \(R_E = 0.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), its collector connected to ground through \(R_C = 1.2\ \text{k}\Omega\), and its base biased by \(R_1 = 30\ \text{k}\Omega\) (to \(V^+\)) and \(R_2 = 30\ \text{k}\Omega\) (to ground). (a) Find \(R_{TH}\), \(V_{TH}\), \(I_{BQ}\), \(I_{CQ}\) and \(V_{ECQ}\) for \(\beta = 80\). (b) Find the percentage change in \(I_{CQ}\) and \(V_{ECQ}\) if \(\beta = 160\).

1
\(V_{TH} = 1.65\ \text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 15\ \text{k}\Omega\).
2
Loop from \(V^+\): \(V^+ = I_{EQ}R_E + V_{EB(on)} + I_{BQ}R_{TH} + V_{TH}\), so \(I_{BQ} = \dfrac{3.3 - 0.7 - 1.65}{(81)(0.5\ \text{k}) + 15\ \text{k}} = 17.1\ \mu\text{A}\).
3
\(I_{CQ} = 1.369\ \text{mA}\); \(V_{ECQ} = 3.3 - (81)(17.1\ \mu\text{A})(0.5\ \text{k}) - (1.369)(1.2) = 0.964\ \text{V}\).
4
(b) At \(\beta = 160\): \(I_{BQ} = 9.95\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_{CQ} = 1.592\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{ECQ} = 0.589\ \text{V}\).
(a) \(I_{CQ} = 1.37\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{ECQ} = 0.96\ \text{V}\). (b) \(I_{CQ}\) rises 16.2 % and \(V_{ECQ}\) falls 38.8 %. \(R_{TH} = 15\ \text{k}\Omega\) is far above \(0.1(1+\beta)R_E = 4.05\ \text{k}\Omega\), so this circuit is not bias stable.
Problem 7.2 centre of the load line with split supplies

An npn stage has \(V^+ = 5\ \text{V}\), \(V^- = -5\ \text{V}\), \(R_C = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\) to \(V^+\), \(R_E = 250\ \Omega\) to \(V^-\), and a divider \(R_1\) (to \(V^+\)), \(R_2\) (to \(V^-\)). \(\beta = 100\), \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0\). Design a bias-stable circuit with the Q-point in the centre of the load line. Use \(I_C \approx I_E\) for the load line, as the tutorial memo does.

1
Load line from \(V_{CE} = 10\ \text{V}\) to \(I_C = 10/1.75\ \text{k}\Omega\): centre at \(V_{CEQ} = 5\ \text{V}\), \(I_{CQ} = 2.857\ \text{mA}\). \(I_{BQ} = 28.6\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
\(R_{TH} = 0.1(101)(250) = 2.525\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\[ V_{TH} = (28.6\ \mu\text{A})(2.525\ \text{k}) + 0.7 + (2.886\ \text{mA})(0.25\ \text{k}) - 5 = -3.506\ \text{V} \]
4
\(\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = (-3.506 + 5)/10 = 0.1494\): \(R_1 = 2.525/0.1494 = 16.9\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 2.525/0.8506 = 2.97\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(I_{CQ} = 2.86\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ} = 5\ \text{V}\), \(R_1 = 16.9\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 2.97\ \text{k}\Omega\).
Problem 7.3 pnp design for a set emitter voltage

A pnp transistor has \(R_E = 2.5\ \text{k}\Omega\) from +5 V to its emitter, and its base is biased by \(R_1\) to +3 V and \(R_2\) to −3 V; the collector goes to −5 V. \(\beta = 100\). Design a bias-stable circuit with \(V_{EQ} = 0\ \text{V}\).

1
\(I_{EQ} = 5/2.5\ \text{k} = 2\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{BQ} = 2/101 = 19.8\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
\(R_{TH} = 0.1(101)(2.5\ \text{k}) = 25.25\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
\(V_{TH} = V_E - V_{EB(on)} - I_{BQ}R_{TH} = 0 - 0.7 - 0.5 = -1.2\ \text{V}\).
4
\(V_{TH} = \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}(6) - 3\), so \(\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = 0.3\): \(R_1 = 25.25/0.3 = 84.2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 25.25/0.7 = 36.1\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_1 = 84.2\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 36.1\ \text{k}\Omega\).
Problem 7.4 common emitter driving an emitter follower

\(Q_1\) (npn) has a divider \(R_1 = 90\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 30\ \text{k}\Omega\) from +12 V, \(R_{C1} = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_{E1} = 1.5\ \text{k}\Omega\) to ground. Its collector drives the base of \(Q_2\) (npn), whose collector is at +12 V and whose emitter goes to −12 V through \(R_{E2} = 4\ \text{k}\Omega\). \(\beta = 100\). Find the currents and \(V_{CEQ}\) of both.

1
\(V_{TH} = 3\ \text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 22.5\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(I_{B1} = (3 - 0.7)/(22.5\ \text{k} + 151.5\ \text{k}) = 13.2\ \mu\text{A}\); \(I_{C1} = 1.322\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{E1} = 1.335\ \text{mA}\).
2
\(R_{C1}\) carries \(I_{C1} + I_{B2}\): \(V_{C1} = 12 - (I_{C1} + I_{B2})(4\ \text{k})\). \(Q_2\)'s loop: \(V_{C1} = 0.7 + (101)I_{B2}(4\ \text{k}) - 12\).
3
Equate: \(I_{B2} = \dfrac{12 - 5.287 - 0.7 + 12}{404\ \text{k} + 4\ \text{k}} = 44.1\ \mu\text{A}\); \(I_{C2} = 4.41\ \text{mA}\); \(I_{E2} = 4.46\ \text{mA}\).
4
\(V_{C1} = 6.54\ \text{V}\); \(V_{CEQ1} = 6.54 - (1.335)(1.5) = 4.53\ \text{V}\); \(V_{E2} = 5.84\ \text{V}\); \(V_{CEQ2} = 12 - 5.84 = 6.16\ \text{V}\).
\(I_{C1} = 1.32\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ1} = 4.53\ \text{V}\); \(I_{B2} = 44.1\ \mu\text{A}\), \(I_{C2} = 4.41\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{CEQ2} = 6.16\ \text{V}\).
Problem 7.5 designing a pnp-npn pair

\(Q_1\) (pnp) has \(R_{E1}\) from +6 V to its emitter, a base divider \(R_1 = 60\ \text{k}\Omega\) (to +6 V) and \(R_2 = 120\ \text{k}\Omega\) (to −6 V), and \(R_{C1}\) from its collector to −6 V. Its collector drives the base of \(Q_2\) (npn), with \(R_{C2}\) to +6 V and \(R_{E2}\) to −6 V. \(\beta = 120\). Find \(R_{E1}\), \(R_{C1}\), \(R_{E2}\) and \(R_{C2}\) for \(I_{C1} = I_{C2} = 1\ \text{mA}\), \(V_{ECQ1} = 4\ \text{V}\), \(V_{CEQ2} = 5\ \text{V}\).

1
\(V_{TH} = \frac{120}{180}(12) - 6 = 2\ \text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 40\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(I_{B1} = 8.33\ \mu\text{A}\).
2
\[ R_{E1} = \dfrac{6 - 0.7 - (8.33\ \mu\text{A})(40\ \text{k}) - 2}{(121)(8.33\ \mu\text{A})} = 2.94\ \text{k}\Omega \]
3
\(V_{C1} = 6 - (1.008)(2.94) - 4 = -0.967\ \text{V}\). \(R_{C1}\) carries \(I_{C1} - I_{B2} = 0.992\ \text{mA}\), so \(R_{C1} = (-0.967 + 6)/0.992\ \text{mA} = 5.08\ \text{k}\Omega\).
4
\(V_{E2} = -1.667\ \text{V}\); \(R_{E2} = (-1.667 + 6)/1.008\ \text{mA} = 4.30\ \text{k}\Omega\); \(R_{C2} = (6 - 5 + 1.667)/1\ \text{mA} = 2.67\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_{E1} = 2.94\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{C1} = 5.08\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{E2} = 4.30\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_{C2} = 2.67\ \text{k}\Omega\).
Problem 7.6 switch design window and efficiency

An npn switch drives an LED (\(V_\gamma = 2.0\ \text{V}\)) at 20 mA from \(V_{CC} = 12\ \text{V}\) through \(R_C\). \(\beta = 150\), \(V_{CE(sat)} = 0.1\ \text{V}\). The base is driven from \(V_{BB} = 5\ \text{V}\), which can supply at most 3 mA. Available base resistors: 1.2 kΩ, 1.5 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ and 100 kΩ. (a) Find \(R_C\) and the range of \(R_B\), and choose one. (b) Find the efficiency \(P_{LED}/P_{total}\).

1
\(R_C = (12 - 2.0 - 0.1)/20\ \text{mA} = 495\ \Omega\).
2
Current limit: \(R_B \ge (5 - 0.7)/3\ \text{mA} = 1.43\ \text{k}\Omega\). Saturation: \(I_B \gt 20/150 = 0.133\ \text{mA}\), so \(R_B \lt (5 - 0.7)/0.133\ \text{mA} = 32.3\ \text{k}\Omega\).
3
1.5 kΩ and 2.2 kΩ both fit. Choose 2.2 kΩ: \(I_B = 1.95\ \text{mA}\), \(I_C/I_B = 10.2\), well saturated with less drive current.
4
\(P_{LED} = (2.0)(20\ \text{mA}) = 40\ \text{mW}\); \(P_{total} = (12)(20\ \text{mA}) + (5)(1.95\ \text{mA}) = 250\ \text{mW}\).
\(R_C = 495\ \Omega\), \(1.43\ \text{k}\Omega \le R_B \lt 32.3\ \text{k}\Omega\), choose 2.2 kΩ; efficiency 16.0 %. Most of the power is lost in \(R_C\).

08Formula sheet

Single resistor

\(R_C = (V_{CC} - V_{CEQ})/I_{CQ}\)

\(R_B = (V_{CC} - 0.7)/I_{BQ}\)

\(I_{CQ} \propto \beta\): not stable

Divider

\(V_{TH} = \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}(V^+ - V^-) + V^-\), \(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2\)

\(I_{BQ} = \dfrac{V_{TH} - 0.7 - V^-_E}{R_{TH} + (1+\beta)R_E}\)

Bias stability

\(R_{TH} = 0.1(1+\beta)R_E\)

\(I_{CQ} \approx (V_{TH} - 0.7)/R_E\)

\(R_1 = R_{TH}V/V_{TH}\), \(R_2 = R_{TH}V/(V - V_{TH})\) (single supply \(V\))

Current mirror

\(I_1 = (-0.7 - V^-)/R_1\)

\(I_1 = I_Q(1 + 2/\beta)\)

Multistage

Solve stage 1 base loop first

KCL at the joining node includes \(I_{B2}\)

Cascode: \(V_{B1} = V_{R_E} + 0.7\), \(V_{B2} = V_{B1} + V_{CE1}\)

In the divider entry, \(V^-_E\) is the voltage at the bottom of \(R_E\) (0 V for a single supply).
