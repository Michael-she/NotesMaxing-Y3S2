MOSFETs and Common-Source DC Analysis

The MOS field-effect transistor from first principles: the MOS capacitor, NMOS and PMOS enhancement devices, depletion mode, the current equations, and DC analysis and design of common-source circuits. Built from Lectures 21 to 25 and Tutorial 10.

01The MOS capacitor

A BJT is controlled by a base current. A field-effect transistor (FET) is controlled by a voltage: the voltage on an insulated gate sets how much current can flow between two other terminals, and the gate itself draws no DC current. This chapter covers the metal-oxide-semiconductor FET, or MOSFET, and its DC behaviour.

Everything about a MOSFET starts from the MOS capacitor: a metal gate, a thin insulating oxide and a semiconductor body stacked like a parallel-plate capacitor.

DefinitionMOS capacitor: a gate terminal (metal, for example aluminium), an oxide of thickness \(t_{ox}\) and permittivity \(\varepsilon_{ox}\), and a semiconductor substrate. Applying a voltage between gate and substrate puts equal and opposite charge on the two plates, and an electric field appears across the oxide.

What the bottom plate is made of depends on the sign of the gate voltage. Take a p-type substrate, where holes are the majority carriers.

  • Negative gate. The field pulls holes towards the oxide. They pile up at the interface and form the positive charge of the bottom plate. This is accumulation.
  • Small positive gate. The field pushes holes away from the interface. The fixed acceptor ions they leave behind form a negative space-charge region, and that region is the bottom plate. This is depletion.
  • Larger positive gate. The field grows until minority-carrier electrons are attracted to the interface. They form an electron inversion layer, and the amount of charge in it depends on the gate voltage.

Figure 1.1: Left to right: holes accumulate, a depletion region forms, and an electron layer appears under the oxide. The dashed outline marks the depletion region, which stays under the inversion layer.

An n-type substrate behaves the same way with the signs swapped. A positive gate accumulates electrons. A sufficiently negative gate repels electrons, leaves a positive space-charge region, and then attracts minority-carrier holes into a hole inversion layer.

DefinitionEnhancement mode: a voltage has to be applied to the gate before an inversion layer exists. On a p-type substrate the voltage is positive and the layer is electrons. On an n-type substrate the voltage is negative and the layer is holes.
IntuitionThe gate and the body are the two plates of a capacitor. Whatever charge the gate carries, the body has to show the opposite charge, and the sign of the gate voltage decides what the body uses to supply it: majority carriers, bare ions, or minority carriers dragged to the surface.
Watch outThe inversion layer is made of minority carriers. An enhancement NMOS conducts through electrons even though its substrate is p-type, and the layer only exists while the gate voltage is above the threshold.

02The n-channel enhancement MOSFET

The n-channel enhancement MOSFET adds two heavily doped n-type regions, the source and the drain, to either side of the MOS capacitor. The gate, oxide and p-type substrate (the body) are the same as before.

Figure 2.1: The channel length \(L\) is the gap between source and drain under the gate, typically below \(1\ \mu\text{m}\), and the oxide is typically thinner than \(400\ \text{Å}\).

With \(v_{GS} = 0\) the source and drain are separated by p-type material, which is equivalent to two back-to-back diodes. Whatever the drain voltage, the current is essentially zero. When a positive gate voltage above the threshold is applied, an electron inversion layer forms at the oxide-semiconductor interface and joins the two n regions. Current can now flow between them.

DefinitionThreshold voltage \(V_{TN}\): the gate-to-source voltage at which the inversion layer forms, which is the voltage needed to turn the MOSFET on. For an enhancement NMOS, \(V_{TN} \gt 0\).

The roles of the two n regions follow from what they do. The source supplies the carriers and the drain collects them. The amount of current depends on the charge in the inversion layer, which depends on \(v_{GS}\). No current flows through the gate (it is an insulator) or through the substrate.

Key ideaGate current is zero in DC, so \(i_D\) is also the source current and the only thing the gate does is set the channel charge. In every DC circuit later this week, the gate sits at whatever voltage its resistor divider gives it, with no loading from the transistor.
UnitsLengths in this chapter appear in micrometres and angstroms, but the permittivity is given per centimetre, so convert to centimetres before using it: \(1\ \mu\text{m} = 10^{-4}\ \text{cm}\) and \(1\ \text{Å} = 10^{-8}\ \text{cm}\).

03NMOS current-voltage characteristics

The current in an NMOS depends on two voltages: \(v_{GS}\), which sets the channel charge, and \(v_{DS}\), which pushes the charge from source to drain. Three regions cover every case.

  • Cutoff, \(v_{GS} \lt V_{TN}\): no inversion layer, so \(i_D = 0\).
  • Non-saturation (also called triode), \(v_{GS} \gt V_{TN}\) and \(v_{DS} \lt v_{DS}(sat)\): the channel runs the full length and the current rises with \(v_{DS}\).
  • Saturation, \(v_{GS} \gt V_{TN}\) and \(v_{DS} \gt v_{DS}(sat)\): the channel narrows to a point at the drain end and the current stops depending on \(v_{DS}\).
Edge of saturation\[ v_{DS}(\text{sat}) = v_{GS} - V_{TN} \]
\(v_{DS}(\text{sat})\)

\(v_{GS}\)

\(V_{TN}\)

CheckAt the edge, \(v_{DS} = v_{GS} - V_{TN}\), the triode equation gives \(K_n[2(v_{GS}-V_{TN})^2 - (v_{GS}-V_{TN})^2] = K_n(v_{GS}-V_{TN})^2\), which is the saturation equation. The current is continuous across the edge, so the two formulas can never disagree there.

The conduction parameter packs the geometry and the process into one number. The oxide capacitance per unit area comes from the parallel-plate formula, and the mobility \(\mu_n\) is the electron mobility in the inversion layer.

Conduction parameter\[ K_n = \frac{W\,\mu_n C_{ox}}{2L} = \frac{k_n'}{2}\,\frac{W}{L} \qquad C_{ox} = \frac{\varepsilon_{ox}}{t_{ox}} \qquad k_n' = \mu_n C_{ox} \]

\(\mu_n\)

\(C_{ox}\)

\(\varepsilon_{ox}\)

\(t_{ox}\)

\(k_n'\)

IntuitionA wider channel carries more current (\(W\) up), a longer one resists it (\(L\) up), a thinner oxide gives a stronger grip of the gate on the channel (\(t_{ox}\) down) and a more mobile carrier moves faster. Only \(W/L\) is under the designer's control, so \(K_n\) is the knob you turn by drawing a wider or narrower transistor.

The lecture's example 3.1 uses \(V_{TN} = 0.4\ \text{V}\), \(W = 20\ \mu\text{m}\), \(L = 0.8\ \mu\text{m}\), \(\mu_n = 650\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V s}\) and \(t_{ox} = 200\ \text{Å}\). That gives \(K_n = 1.40\ \text{mA/V}^2\), then \(i_D = 0.224\ \text{mA}\) at \(v_{GS} = 0.8\ \text{V}\) and \(2.02\ \text{mA}\) at \(v_{GS} = 1.6\ \text{V}\) (saturation in both cases). Section 4 applies the same steps to new numbers.

Watch outA saturated MOSFET is the opposite of a saturated BJT. In a BJT, saturation means the collector junction is forward biased and the current gain is lost. In a MOSFET, saturation is the flat part of the output curve, the region you normally want when the device is used as an amplifier.

04Kn, W/L and the region of operation

Almost every NMOS question reduces to the same four steps, and the only thing that changes is which quantity is unknown.

  1. Find \(K_n\). Either build it from \(W\), \(L\), \(\mu_n\), \(t_{ox}\), or recover it from one measurement.
  2. Compute \(v_{DS}(sat) = v_{GS} - V_{TN}\) and compare it with \(v_{DS}\).
  3. Use the equation for the region you found.
  4. If you assumed a region and the answer contradicts it, go back and use the other equation.
Kn from one measurement in saturation\[ K_n = \frac{I_{D0}}{(V_{GS0} - V_{TN})^2} \qquad \frac{W}{L} = \frac{2K_n}{k_n'} \]
\(K_n\)
Conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
\(I_{D0}\)
Measured drain current, taken with the transistor in saturation, in A.
\(V_{GS0}\)
Gate-source voltage at which it was measured, in V.
\(V_{TN}\)
Threshold voltage, in V.
\(W/L\)
Width-to-length ratio, a pure number.
\(k_n'\)
Process conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).

Worked example 4.2 Recover Kn from a measurement and test the region

An NMOS with \(V_{TN} = 0.8\ \text{V}\) carries \(0.5\ \text{mA}\) at \(v_{GS} = 2.8\ \text{V}\) and \(v_{DS} = 4.0\ \text{V}\). Find the drain current when (a) \(v_{GS} = 1.8\ \text{V}\), \(v_{DS} = 3.0\ \text{V}\) and (b) \(v_{GS} = 2.8\ \text{V}\), \(v_{DS} = 1.2\ \text{V}\).

2

3

4

Worked example 4.3 Finding the width of a transistor

An NMOS has \(V_{TN} = 0.7\ \text{V}\), \(L = 1.2\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(k_n' = 100\ \mu\text{A/V}^2\). It carries \(0.5\ \text{mA}\) in saturation at \(v_{GS} = 1.7\ \text{V}\). Find \(W\), then the current at the same \(v_{GS}\) when \(v_{DS} = 0.3\ \text{V}\), and \(v_{DS}(sat)\).

05The p-channel enhancement MOSFET

A p-channel enhancement MOSFET works like the NMOS with the roles of holes and electrons exchanged. A negative gate voltage, measured against the source, induces a hole inversion layer. The p-type source supplies the holes, they flow to the drain, and conventional current leaves the transistor at the drain terminal.

DefinitionThreshold voltage \(V_{TP}\): for an enhancement PMOS, \(V_{TP} \lt 0\). The device conducts when \(v_{SG} \gt |V_{TP}|\), that is when \(v_{SG} + V_{TP} \gt 0\).

The subscripts are written source first (\(v_{SG}\), \(v_{SD}\)) so that both voltages are positive in normal operation. The PMOS equations are the NMOS equations with \(v_{GS} \to v_{SG}\), \(v_{DS} \to v_{SD}\) and \(V_{TN} \to -V_{TP}\).

06Symbols, conventions and regions

The symbol tells you the type of transistor at a glance, and the voltage subscripts tell you which formula applies. The conventions below hold throughout the course, with the source and the body wired together.

Figure 6.1: The arrow is on the source and shows the direction of conventional current. An unbroken line between source and drain marks a channel that exists with no gate voltage, which is how depletion devices are drawn.

  • The arrow sits on the source terminal and points along the direction of conventional current.
  • NMOS: carriers flow from source to drain and current enters at the drain. PMOS: current leaves at the drain.
  • A vertical solid line is the gate electrode, and the gap between it and the channel line stands for the oxide.
  • A broken channel line (no shaded conductive path) means enhancement mode. A continuous channel line means depletion mode.
  • NMOS voltages are \(v_{GS}\) and \(v_{DS}\). PMOS voltages are \(v_{SG}\) and \(v_{SD}\).

Problems often give the node voltages of a transistor and ask which region it is in. The test is the same for every case: work out the control voltage first, then compare the output voltage with the edge of saturation.

Non-saturation\(v_{DS} \lt v_{GS} - V_{TN}\)\(v_{SD} \lt v_{SG} + V_{TP}\)
Saturation\(v_{DS} \ge v_{GS} - V_{TN}\)\(v_{SD} \ge v_{SG} + V_{TP}\)

07Depletion-mode MOSFETs

A depletion-mode MOSFET is built with a channel already in place. It conducts with zero gate voltage, and the gate is used to shrink the channel or, in the other direction, to enrich it.

n-channel depletion. With \(v_{GS} = 0\) an n-type channel exists and a drain-to-source current can flow. A negative gate voltage (\(V_{TN} \lt v_{GS} \lt 0\)) induces a space-charge region under the oxide, which thins the channel, lowers its conductance and reduces the current. At \(v_{GS} = V_{TN}\) the space-charge region reaches through the whole channel and the current drops to zero, so \(V_{TN}\) is negative for this device. A positive \(v_{GS}\) creates an electron accumulation layer and increases the current.

p-channel depletion. A p-type channel exists with \(v_{SG} = 0\). A positive gate voltage (against the source) is what turns it off, so \(V_{TP}\) is positive for this device.

Key ideaThe equations do not change. Only the sign convention on the threshold does: \(V_{TN} \lt 0\) for a depletion NMOS and \(V_{TP} \gt 0\) for a depletion PMOS, against \(V_{TN} \gt 0\) and \(V_{TP} \lt 0\) for the enhancement versions.

Because the threshold is on the other side of zero, setting the gate to zero volts already gives a positive overdrive, and the current at \(v_{GS} = 0\) follows directly.

\(i_D\)
Drain current, in A.
\(K_n, K_p\)
Conduction parameters, in A/V\(^2\).
\(V_{TN}\)
Depletion NMOS threshold, which is negative, in V.
\(V_{TP}\)
Depletion PMOS threshold, which is positive, in V.
\(v_{DS}(\text{sat}),\ v_{SD}(\text{sat})\)

09Common-source DC analysis

The common-source circuit is the MOSFET's basic amplifier stage: the source terminal is common to the input (gate) and the output (drain). Here only the DC solution matters. The gate takes no current, so the divider \(R_1, R_2\) sets the gate voltage with no loading from the transistor, and the drain current flows through \(R_D\) alone.

Figure 9.1: Both use a resistor divider on the gate. In the n-channel circuit the source is at ground and \(R_D\) is on the supply side. In the p-channel circuit the source is at \(V_{DD}\) and \(R_D\) is on the ground side, so the two circuits are mirror images.

Gate voltage and n-channel analysis\[ V_G = V_{GS} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\,V_{DD} \qquad I_D = K_n(V_{GS} - V_{TN})^2 \qquad V_{DS} = V_{DD} - I_D R_D \qquad P_T = I_D V_{DS} \]
\(V_G\)
Gate voltage, in V. It equals \(V_{GS}\) because the source is grounded.
\(R_1, R_2\)
Divider resistors, upper and lower, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(I_D\)
Drain current, in A. The saturation formula applies only if the check below passes.
\(K_n, V_{TN}\)
Conduction parameter and threshold of the NMOS.
\(V_{DS}\)
Drain-to-source voltage, from the drain loop, in V.
\(R_D\)
Drain resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(P_T\)
Power dissipated in the transistor, in W. The gate draws no current, so this product is the whole of it.
Gate voltage and p-channel analysis\[ V_G = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\,V_{DD} \qquad V_{SG} = V_{DD} - V_G \qquad I_D = K_p(V_{SG} + V_{TP})^2 \qquad V_{SD} = V_{DD} - I_D R_D \]
\(V_G\)
Gate voltage to ground, in V.
\(V_{SG}\)
Source-to-gate voltage. The source is at \(V_{DD}\), so this is the part of the supply not across \(R_2\).
\(I_D\)
Drain current, in A.
\(K_p, V_{TP}\)

\(V_{SD}\)
Source-to-drain voltage, from the drain loop, in V.
\(R_D\)
Drain resistor, in \(\Omega\).

The saturation formula is an assumption until it is checked. The routine for either type is:

  1. Find \(V_G\) from the divider, then \(V_{GS}\) (or \(V_{SG}\)). If the device is not above threshold, \(I_D = 0\) and you are done.
  2. Assume saturation and compute \(I_D\) from the saturation formula.
  3. Find \(V_{DS}\) (or \(V_{SD}\)) from the drain loop.
  4. Compare it with the edge of saturation, \(V_{GS} - V_{TN}\) (or \(V_{SG} + V_{TP}\)). If the output voltage is larger, the assumption holds and you are finished.
  5. If it is smaller, the device is in non-saturation. Discard the saturation current and solve again with the non-saturation equation, as set out below.
Worked example 9.1 NMOS common-source stage

An NMOS stage has \(V_{DD} = 5\ \text{V}\), \(R_1 = 200\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = 100\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(R_D = 10\ \text{k}\Omega\), \(V_{TN} = 0.6\ \text{V}\) and \(K_n = 0.2\ \text{mA/V}^2\). Find \(I_D\), \(V_{DS}\) and the power dissipated in the transistor.

3

4
Check: \(V_{DS}(sat) = 1.667 - 0.6 = 1.067\ \text{V}\), and \(2.72 \gt 1.067\), so the assumption was correct.
5

When the saturation assumption fails

If the drain loop gives an output voltage below the edge of saturation, the transistor is in non-saturation and the current is smaller than the saturation formula claimed. Substitute the drain-loop equation into the non-saturation current formula and you get a quadratic. Working in terms of the output voltage keeps the algebra short. For the p-channel circuit, with \(I_D = (V_{DD} - v_{SD})/R_D\):

Non-saturation quadratic (PMOS; the NMOS form swaps the subscripts)\[ K_p R_D\,v_{SD}^{\,2} - \Big[1 + 2K_pR_D\,(V_{SG} + V_{TP})\Big]\,v_{SD} + V_{DD} = 0 \qquad I_D = \frac{V_{DD} - v_{SD}}{R_D} \]
\(K_p\)
Conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
\(R_D\)
Drain resistor, in \(\Omega\).
\(v_{SD}\)
The unknown source-to-drain voltage, in V.
\(V_{SG} + V_{TP}\)
The overdrive, fixed by the divider and so known.
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(I_D\)
Drain current from the drain loop, in A.

The quadratic has two roots, and only one is physical. Test both against the non-saturation condition \(v_{SD} \lt V_{SG} + V_{TP}\). The root that fails the test belongs to the extension of the triode parabola beyond its peak, where the formula no longer describes the device, and you reject it. The lecture does exactly this for its example 3.4, where the valid root is \(0.515\ \text{mA}\) with \(V_{SD} = 1.14\ \text{V}\) and the rejected one is \(0.276\ \text{mA}\) with \(V_{SD} = 2.93\ \text{V}\), above the \(1.7\ \text{V}\) edge. In that example the saturation guess gave \(I_D = 0.578\ \text{mA}\) and \(V_{SD} = 0.665\ \text{V}\), below the edge, so the guess failed.

10Designing common-source circuits

A design question runs the analysis backwards. You are given the operating point (\(I_D\) and an output voltage) and you must find the resistors. The saturation equation is solved for the overdrive instead of the current.

  1. Find \(R_D\) (or \(R_S\)) from the voltage that has to appear across it and the drain current.
  2. Assume saturation and solve for the overdrive. Take the positive square root: \(v_{GS} - V_{TN} = \sqrt{I_D/K_n}\), or \(v_{SG} + V_{TP} = \sqrt{I_D/K_p}\). The negative root would put the device below threshold.
  3. Check the assumption: the output voltage must be at least as large as the overdrive. If the question wants the device in non-saturation (a switch), use the non-saturation equation instead.
  4. Get the gate voltage from the node voltages: \(V_G = V_{GS}\) for a grounded source, \(V_G = V_{DD} - V_{SG}\) for a PMOS with its source at the supply, or \(V_G = V_S \pm\) the control voltage in general.
  5. Choose \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) to put \(V_G\) on the gate and meet the extra specification on the divider.

Divider with a specified input resistance

The input resistance seen at the gate is the divider's \(R_1 \parallel R_2\), because the gate itself draws nothing. For a divider between supplies \(V^+\) and \(V^-\), the fraction \(R_2/(R_1+R_2)\) sets \(V_G\), and the specified \(R_{in}\) fixes the size of the resistors. For a single supply, put \(V^- = 0\) and \(V^+ = V_{DD}\).

Divider from a target gate voltage and input resistance\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{in}\,(V^+ - V^-)}{V_G - V^-} \qquad R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_{in}}{R_1 - R_{in}} \]
\(R_1\)
Upper divider resistor, from \(V^+\) to the gate, in \(\Omega\).
\(R_{in}\)
Wanted input resistance, \(R_1 \parallel R_2\), in \(\Omega\).
\(V^+, V^-\)
The supplies the divider runs between, in V.
\(V_G\)
Wanted gate voltage, in V.
\(R_2\)
Lower divider resistor, from the gate to \(V^-\), in \(\Omega\).

The first line follows because \(R_{in} = R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)\) and \(V_G - V^- = (V^+ - V^-)\,R_2/(R_1+R_2)\), so \(R_{in}/R_1 = R_2/(R_1+R_2) = (V_G - V^-)/(V^+ - V^-)\).

\(V^+, V^-\)

\(R_2\)
Lower resistor, in \(\Omega\). Then \(R_1\) is the total minus \(R_2\).
Worked example 10.2 Depletion PMOS with a source resistor and \(\pm 5\ \text{V}\) supplies

A depletion PMOS has \(V_{TP} = +1.2\ \text{V}\) and \(K_p = 0.4\ \text{mA/V}^2\). Its source connects to \(+5\ \text{V}\) through \(R_S = 2.5\ \text{k}\Omega\), its drain connects to ground, and the divider \(R_1, R_2\) runs from \(+5\ \text{V}\) to \(-5\ \text{V}\). Design the circuit so that \(V_{SD} = 3\ \text{V}\) with a divider current of no more than 10 % of \(I_D\).

1
The drain is at ground, so the source is at \(V_{SD} = 3\ \text{V}\), and \(R_S\) carries \(I_D = (5 - 3)/2.5\ \text{k}\Omega = 0.8\ \text{mA}\).
2
Assume saturation: \(v_{SG} + V_{TP} = \sqrt{I_D/K_p} = \sqrt{0.8/0.4} = 1.414\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{SG} = 1.414 - 1.2 = 0.214\ \text{V}\). This is positive and above \(-V_{TP}\), so it is allowed.
3
Check: \(V_{SD}(sat) = 1.414\ \text{V}\), and \(V_{SD} = 3\ \text{V}\) is larger, so the assumption holds.
4
Gate voltage: \(V_G = V_S - V_{SG} = 3 - 0.214 = 2.786\ \text{V}\).
5
Divider current: \(I_R = 0.1 \times 0.8\ \text{mA} = 80\ \mu\text{A}\), so \(R_1 + R_2 = 10\ \text{V}/80\ \mu\text{A} = 125\ \text{k}\Omega\).
6
Split: \(R_2/(R_1+R_2) = (V_G + 5)/10 = 0.7786\), so \(R_2 = 97.3\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_1 = 27.7\ \text{k}\Omega\).
\(R_1 = 27.7\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_2 = 97.3\ \text{k}\Omega\), with \(V_{SG} = 0.214\ \text{V}\) and the transistor saturated.

Diode-connected stack

When a gate is tied to its own drain, \(v_{DS} = v_{GS}\). The saturation test \(v_{DS} \ge v_{GS} - V_{TN}\) then reads \(V_{TN} \ge 0\), so an enhancement device connected this way is always saturated and can never leave that region. A stack of two such transistors shares one current and splits the supply between their gate-source voltages.

Stack of two diode-connected NMOS devices\[ V_{DD} = V_{GS1} + V_{GS2} \qquad K_1(V_{GS1} - V_{TN})^2 = K_2(V_{GS2} - V_{TN})^2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; V_{GS1} - V_{TN} = \frac{V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}}{1 + \sqrt{K_1/K_2}} \]
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{GS1}, V_{GS2}\)
Gate-source voltages of the upper and lower transistor, in V. The output is \(V_o = V_{GS2}\).
\(K_1, K_2\)
Conduction parameters of the two transistors, in A/V\(^2\).
\(V_{TN}\)
Common threshold voltage, in V.
\(V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}\)
The total overdrive to be shared, in V.

The shared current is the same through both, so taking square roots gives \(\sqrt{K_1}\,(V_{GS1}-V_{TN}) = \sqrt{K_2}\,(V_{GS2}-V_{TN})\) with both overdrives positive. Together with \(V_{GS1} + V_{GS2} = V_{DD}\), that is two linear equations. The memo reaches the same answer by squaring and solving a quadratic in \(V_{GS1}\), which has one root between \(V_{TN}\) and \(V_{DD}\) (kept) and one above \(V_{DD}\) (rejected).

Worked example 10.3 Two diode-connected transistors in series

Two NMOS transistors are connected in series between \(V_{DD} = 4\ \text{V}\) and ground. The upper one (\(M_1\)) has its gate and drain at \(V_{DD}\). The lower one (\(M_2\)) has its gate and drain joined to the output \(V_o\), which is the source of \(M_1\). Both have \(V_{TN} = 0.5\ \text{V}\) and \(k_n' = 100\ \mu\text{A/V}^2\), with \((W/L)_1 = 20\) and \((W/L)_2 = 10\). Find \(V_{GS1}\), \(V_{GS2}\), \(V_o\) and \(I_D\), and show that both are always saturated.

1
\(K_1 = (50\ \mu\text{A/V}^2)(20) = 1.00\ \text{mA/V}^2\) and \(K_2 = (50\ \mu\text{A/V}^2)(10) = 0.50\ \text{mA/V}^2\).
2
Total overdrive: \(V_{DD} - 2V_{TN} = 4 - 1.0 = 3.0\ \text{V}\).
3
\(V_{GS1} - V_{TN} = 3.0/(1 + \sqrt{1.00/0.50}) = 1.243\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{GS1} = 1.743\ \text{V}\).
4
\(V_{GS2} = V_{DD} - V_{GS1} = 2.257\ \text{V}\), and \(V_o = V_{GS2} = 2.257\ \text{V}\).
5
Current: \(I_D = K_1(V_{GS1} - V_{TN})^2 = 1.00(1.243)^2 = 1.544\ \text{mA}\). Check with the other transistor: \(K_2(V_{GS2} - V_{TN})^2 = 0.50(1.757)^2 = 1.544\ \text{mA}\).
6
Region: for each transistor \(V_{DS} = V_{GS}\), so \(V_{DS} - V_{DS}(sat) = V_{GS} - (V_{GS} - V_{TN}) = V_{TN} = 0.5\ \text{V} \gt 0\). The inequality does not depend on the supply or on \(W/L\), so both transistors are saturated for any bias.
\(V_{GS1} = 1.74\ \text{V}\), \(V_{GS2} = V_o = 2.26\ \text{V}\) and \(I_D = 1.54\ \text{mA}\). The device with the larger \(W/L\) takes the smaller gate-source voltage.

The PMOS as an LED switch

A switch is deliberately not in saturation. With the gate pulled all the way to ground, a PMOS whose source is at the supply has \(v_{SG} = V_{DD}\), so its edge of saturation is large and the transistor operates in non-saturation with a small \(v_{SD}\). The remaining supply voltage falls across the load. The design uses the non-saturation equation and solves for \(K_p\).

Non-saturation design for a switch\[ R_D = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{SD} - V_\gamma}{I_D} \qquad K_p = \frac{I_D}{2\,(V_{SG} + V_{TP})\,V_{SD} - V_{SD}^{\,2}} \qquad \frac{W}{L} = \frac{2K_p}{k_p'} \]
\(R_D\)
Series resistor that sets the LED current, in \(\Omega\).
\(V_{DD}\)
Supply voltage, in V.
\(V_{SD}\)
Chosen voltage across the switch when on, in V. It should be small.
\(V_\gamma\)
Cut-in voltage of the LED, in V.
\(I_D\)
LED current, in A.
\(K_p\)
Required conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
\(V_{SG}\)
Source-to-gate voltage with the gate low, equal to \(V_{DD}\) for \(V_I = 0\).
\(k_p'\)
Process conduction parameter, in A/V\(^2\).
Worked example 10.4 Designing the switch

A PMOS switches an LED with cut-in voltage \(V_\gamma = 1.8\ \text{V}\) from a \(5\ \text{V}\) supply. The source is at \(V_{DD}\), the gate is driven by \(V_I\), and the drain feeds \(R_D\) in series with the LED to ground. The transistor has \(V_{TP} = −0.5\ \text{V}\), \(k_p' = 40\ \mu\text{A/V}^2\) and \(\lambda = 0\). Design \(R_D\) and \(W/L\) so that \(I_D = 10\ \text{mA}\) for \(V_I = 0\) and \(V_{SD} = 0.15\ \text{V}\), and verify the region.

1
Output loop: \(V_{DD} = V_{SD} + I_DR_D + V_\gamma\), so \(R_D = (5 - 0.15 - 1.8)/10\ \text{mA} = 305\ \Omega\).
2
With \(V_I = 0\), \(V_{SG} = 5\ \text{V}\) and \(V_{SD}(sat) = V_{SG} + V_{TP} = 4.5\ \text{V}\). The wanted \(V_{SD} = 0.15\ \text{V}\) is far below that, so the transistor is in non-saturation.
3
\(K_p = 10\ \text{mA}/[2(4.5)(0.15) - 0.15^2] = 7.533\ \text{mA/V}^2\).
4
\(W/L = 2K_p/k_p' = 2(7.533\ \text{mA/V}^2)/(40\ \mu\text{A/V}^2) = 377\).
5
Verify: with \(W/L = 377\), the device equation gives \(i_D = 7.533[2(4.5)(0.15) - 0.15^2] = 10.0\ \text{mA}\), and \(0.15 \lt 4.5\) confirms the region.
\(R_D = 305\ \Omega\) and \(W/L \approx 377\). A wide transistor is the price of a small \(V_{SD}\): the switch behaves like a resistor of \(V_{SD}/I_D = 15\ \Omega\) when on.

13Formula sheet

Regions

\[ \text{NMOS: } v_{DS}(\text{sat}) = v_{GS} - V_{TN} \]

\[ \text{PMOS: } v_{SD}(\text{sat}) = v_{SG} + V_{TP} \]

Cutoff below threshold. Non-saturation when the output voltage is below the edge, saturation when it is above.

NMOS currents

\[ i_D = K_n\left[2(v_{GS} - V_{TN})v_{DS} - v_{DS}^2\right] \]

\[ i_D = K_n(v_{GS} - V_{TN})^2 \]

PMOS currents

\[ i_D = K_p\left[2(v_{SG} + V_{TP})v_{SD} - v_{SD}^2\right] \]

\[ i_D = K_p(v_{SG} + V_{TP})^2 \]

Conduction parameter

\[ K_n = \frac{k_n'}{2}\frac{W}{L} \quad k_n' = \mu_n C_{ox} \quad C_{ox} = \frac{\varepsilon_{ox}}{t_{ox}} \]

\[ \varepsilon_{ox} = 3.9 \times 8.85 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{F/cm} \]

\[ K_n = \frac{I_{D0}}{(V_{GS0} - V_{TN})^2} \quad \frac{W}{L} = \frac{2K_n}{k_n'} \]

Threshold signs

\[ \text{enhancement: } V_{TN} \gt 0,\ V_{TP} \lt 0 \]

\[ \text{depletion: } V_{TN} \lt 0,\ V_{TP} \gt 0 \]

\[ i_D\big|_{v_{GS}=0} = K_nV_{TN}^2 \quad i_D\big|_{v_{SG}=0} = K_pV_{TP}^2 \]

Channel-length modulation

\[ i_D = K_n(v_{GS} - V_{TN})^2(1 + \lambda v_{DS}) \]

\(\lambda = 0\) unless a problem says otherwise.

Common-source analysis

\[ V_G = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}V_{DD} \]

\[ \text{NMOS: } V_{GS} = V_G,\ V_{DS} = V_{DD} - I_DR_D \]

\[ \text{PMOS: } V_{SG} = V_{DD} - V_G,\ V_{SD} = V_{DD} - I_DR_D \]

\[ P_T = I_D V_{DS} \]

Saturation assumption fails

\[ K_pR_D v_{SD}^2 - [1 + 2K_pR_D(V_{SG} + V_{TP})]v_{SD} + V_{DD} = 0 \]

Keep the root below the edge of saturation. Then \(I_D = (V_{DD} - v_{SD})/R_D\).

Design

\[ \text{overdrive} = \sqrt{I_D/K} \text{ (positive root)} \]

\[ R_1 = \frac{R_{in}(V^+ - V^-)}{V_G - V^-} \quad R_2 = \frac{R_1R_{in}}{R_1 - R_{in}} \]

\[ R_1 + R_2 = \frac{V^+ - V^-}{f I_D} \quad \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{V_G - V^-}{V^+ - V^-} \]

Diode-connected stack

\[ V_{GS1} - V_{TN} = \frac{V_{DD} - 2V_{TN}}{1 + \sqrt{K_1/K_2}} \]

\[ V_{GS2} = V_{DD} - V_{GS1} = V_o \]

PMOS LED switch

\[ R_D = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{SD} - V_\gamma}{I_D} \quad K_p = \frac{I_D}{2(V_{SG} + V_{TP})V_{SD} - V_{SD}^2} \]
